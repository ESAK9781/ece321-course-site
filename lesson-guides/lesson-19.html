Lesson 19: Common-Drain Amplifier Analysis and Large-Signal Design

19-1

Common Drain Amplifier

(*) Let's assume we need an amplifier circuit that satisfies the following

Expansion note: how to read the specification box

The box is the amplifier we must design. A 50 Ω source (the function generator, VFG,int with its internal resistance) drives its input, and a load resistor RL sits on its output. The specification limits the open-circuit gain |Avo| from above (it cannot exceed 1 for this stage) and below, requires a large input resistance Ri so the amplifier does not load the source, and requires a small output resistance Ro so the load does not load the amplifier.

(*) We previously showed that for a CS-amp, Ro≈ RD and for our last design RD= [handout value]† This is too large to interface with RL= 100 Ω or else the gain drops by

RR+R= RLRL+Ro =100Ω100Ω+RD = [handout value]†

Expansion note: why a large Ro costs gain at the load

The output of any amplifier can be modeled as an ideal source Avovi in series with Ro. Connecting RL forms a voltage divider, so the voltage that reaches the load is multiplied by RL/(RL+Ro), which is the factor in the line above. When Ro is comparable to or larger than RL most of the gain is lost inside the amplifier; when Ro≪RL the factor is close to 1.

So, we need to follow a CS-amp by a CD-amp which will have much lower output resistance

Expansion note: why a common-drain stage is added after the common-source stage

The common-source stage supplies the voltage gain but has a high output resistance (about RD). The common-drain stage (also called a source follower) has a gain just below 1 but a very low output resistance, about 1/gm in parallel with RS. Placing it between the common-source stage and the load acts as a buffer: the load sees the small output resistance instead of RD.

19-2

(*) Let's analyze this using our small-signal model

— short VDD supply

— short coupling capacitors

— use T-model b/c RS connected directly to the source

Expansion note: why VDD and the capacitors are shorted for the small-signal analysis

Small-signal analysis looks only at the signal. An ideal DC supply holds a constant voltage, so its signal component is zero, which is the same as a short to ground. The coupling capacitors are chosen large enough that at the signal frequency their impedance is negligible, so they act as shorts too, while blocking DC.

Expansion note: why the T-model is used when a resistor is connected to the source

In the T-model the gate carries no current and the resistor 1/gm sits between the gate-side node and the source, so any resistor attached to the source, such as RS, simply adds in series with 1/gm. The current in that series path, I, is the same current the controlled source passes from the drain, so I=gmvgs.

19-3

(*) First, let's find Ri

— Since ig= 0, looking in with vsig disconnected

Ri = RG1∥RG2

Expansion note: why the MOSFET does not appear in Ri

The gate of a MOSFET is insulated from the channel, so no signal current flows into it (ig=0). The transistor therefore adds nothing to the resistance seen at the input; only the two bias resistors, which are in parallel from the gate node to ground once VDD is shorted, remain.

(*) Next, let's find the open-circuit gain

— disconnect RL

— find Avo

The control signal I= Vi1/gm+RS

The output voltage Vo= IRS

So Vo= ViRSRS+1/gm

and Avo= VoVi = RSRS+1/gm=gmRS1+gmRS

Expansion note: how the open-circuit gain follows from the T-model

With the load disconnected the input voltage Vi appears across the series combination of 1/gm and RS (the gate draws no current and ties the top of 1/gm to the input), so I=Vi/(1/gm+RS). The same current flows through RS and sets Vo=IRS. Dividing by Vi: Avo=RSRS+1/gm=gmRS1+gmRS<1 Because gmRS is positive the gain is always below 1, and it approaches 1 when gmRS≫1.

(*) Finally, let's find Ro

— disconnect RL

— set Vi= 0

— look back from the output (source) node

19-4

Note, Vi= 0, shorting out (RG1∥RG2) and the dependent current source, so looking back we see

Ro = RS∥1gm

Expansion note: why the dependent source drops out when looking back

The source is controlled by vgs, which is the voltage across 1/gm. With Vi=0 the gate is tied to ground and nothing excites the circuit from the input, so the controlled current is zero and the source is an open circuit. What remains between the output node and ground is 1/gm in parallel with RS: Ro=RS∥1gm=RS/gmRS+1/gm Since 1/gm is typically only tens of ohms, Ro≈1/gm whenever RS≫1/gm.

Thus, our model for the common-drain (CD) amplifier is

Expansion note: how to use this model with a source and a load

The source resistance (50 Ω here) forms a divider with Ri at the input, and the load RL forms a divider with Ro at the output. The loaded gain is Av=AvoRLRL+Ro (the input divider is nearly 1 because Ri is far larger than the source resistance).

19-5

(*) Because we have large-signal variations at the output, our design approach will be different than the CS-amplifier

Expansion note: why large output swings change the design approach

The common-source design in the earlier lessons assumed small signals around the bias point. Here the output must swing several volts into a 100 Ω load, so the load pulls large currents (peaks of tens of milliamps) through the source resistor. The bias must keep the transistor conducting at the most demanding point of the swing, so we design from the load current outward, step by step, instead of starting from a gain target.

Assume

Kn= 150† mA/V²

Vt= 1.5† V

λ= 0†

Expansion note: why λ is taken to be zero

The channel-length modulation parameter λ describes a small increase of drain current with drain voltage. Setting λ=0 removes the output resistance ro of the transistor; hand calculations ignore it unless the problem gives it.

Goal: Desire 3† Vpp output swing for RL= 100 Ω with Rin≥ 500 kΩ†

  1. ①

    Assume Vin,pp≈ 3.5 V to account for the gain being less than 1

    Expansion note: why the input swing is larger than the output swing

    The follower's gain with the load connected is below 1, so the input peak-to-peak must exceed the 3 V peak-to-peak required at the output: Vin,pp≈Vo,pp/Av. A loaded gain near 0.86 gives about 3.5 V.

  2. ②

    Pick VS ≈ ½ VDD → VS= 8 V†

    Expansion note: why the source is biased near half the supply

    Half of VDD leaves about equal room above and below the source voltage for the output to swing: down toward ground and up toward VDD. Half of 15 V is 7.5 V; the design continues from 8 V.

  3. ③

    Find the peak load current IL,pk= Vo,pkRL

    = 1.5V100Ω=15mA

    Expansion note: why the peak is half of the peak-to-peak swing

    A 3 V peak-to-peak output swings 1.5 V above and 1.5 V below its average, so the largest voltage across the load is the peak, Vo,pk=3V/2=1.5 V, and the load current peaks at 15 mA.

  4. ④

    Specify minimum drain current to keep FET on

    ID,min= 5 mA†

    Expansion note: why a minimum drain current is specified

    On the negative output peak the load pushes current back into the source node, and the transistor supplies only the difference between the current RS carries and the load current. If that difference reached zero the transistor would turn off (cutoff) and the output would clip. A few milliamps of margin, ID,min, prevents this.

  5. ⑤

    Find worst-case current thru RS

    IRS,max= IL,pk + ID,min = 15mA+5mA=20mA

    Expansion note: why this is the worst case for RS

    KCL at the source node gives iD=iRS+iL, where iL is the current flowing out of the node into the load. At the negative output peak the load pushes current back into the node, iL=−IL,pk, so iRS=iD+IL,pk. With iD at its lowest value ID,min, the current RS must carry at that instant is IL,pk+ID,min, and the source voltage is also at its lowest, so the voltage across RS is smallest there.

  1. ⑥

    With the desired output voltage swinging, find the minimum voltage across RS

    VRS,min= VS−Vo,pk=8V−1.5V=6.5V

  2. ⑦

    Calculate RS

    RS= VRS,minIRS,max = 6.5V20mA = 325 Ω

    Expansion note: why 325 Ω satisfies both requirements

    RS must drop at least VRS,min (6.5 V) while carrying IRS,max (20 mA), so RS=VRS,min/IRS,max. With a smaller resistor the negative peak would drive the drain current below ID,min; with a larger one the quiescent current falls and the follower cannot supply the load peaks.

    With no signal the coupling capacitor blocks DC, so no DC current reaches the load. Everything the transistor conducts flows down through RS, so ID=VS/RS.

Expansion note: how to answer the triode check

The transistor leaves saturation (enters triode) if the gate rises above the drain by more than the threshold: VGD=VG−VD>Vt. The drain is fixed at VDD=15 V, and the gate never exceeds about 11.8 V (its 10.07 V bias plus the input peak of about 1.73 V at the gate), so VGD stays near −3.2 V and is far below Vt: the transistor stays in saturation.

Expansion note: what the THD measurement tells us

Total harmonic distortion is the combined size of the harmonics (2 kHz, 3 kHz, …) relative to the 1 kHz fundamental in the output. A perfect follower reproduces the input exactly and has zero THD; the square-law transistor makes a small, nonlinear change to the waveform, which shows up as harmonics. The resolution setting is the frequency step of the analysis and must divide the fundamental evenly (100 Hz divides 1 kHz), and the display setting selects percent instead of decibels.

Appendix

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Judging whether an amplifier's output resistance is acceptable for a load

Recognize it

You have an amplifier with output resistance Ro and a load RL, and must find how much gain is lost or what to add.

Method

  1. Model the output as Avovi in series with Ro.
  2. Loaded gain: Av=AvoRLRL+Ro.
  3. Gain lost: the divider factor RLRL+Ro.
  4. If the factor is too small, follow the stage with a buffer whose Ro is much smaller than RL (a common-drain stage).

Checks and mistakes

  • The factor is always below 1; it tends to 1 as Ro/RL→0.
  • Use the output resistance of the stage (for a CS amplifier about RD), not the load.

Worked in these notes: p. 19-1.

A2. Small-signal analysis of a common-drain amplifier: Ri, Avo, Ro

Recognize it

You are asked for the input resistance, open-circuit gain and output resistance of a source follower, or for its two-port model.

Method

  1. Short VDD and the coupling capacitors; replace the MOSFET with the T-model.
  2. Input resistance: ig=0, so Ri=RG1∥RG2.
  3. Open-circuit gain (load disconnected): I=Vi1/gm+RS, Vo=IRS, so Avo=RSRS+1/gm=gmRS1+gmRS.
  4. Output resistance (load disconnected, Vi=0): Ro=RS∥1gm.
  5. Assemble the model: Ri at the input, AvoVi in series with Ro at the output.
  6. Find gm=KnVOV from the bias point first.

Checks

  • 0<Avo<1.
  • Ro comes out small, roughly 1/gm.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting 1/gm in the gain denominator and getting Avo=1.
  • Leaving RL connected while finding Avo or Ro.
  • Including the MOSFET gate in Ri.

Worked in these notes: pp. 19-2 to 19-4.

A3. Designing a common-drain stage for a large output swing

Recognize it

Design a source follower for a given load RL, output swing Vo,pp, supply VDD, device (Kn, Vt) and input-resistance requirement.

Method

  1. Estimate the input swing: Vin,pp≈Vo,pp/Av.
  2. Pick VS≈12VDD.
  3. Peak load current: IL,pk=Vo,pk/RL.
  4. Choose a minimum drain current ID,min to avoid cutoff.
  5. Worst-case RS current: IRS,max=IL,pk+ID,min.
  6. Smallest RS voltage: VRS,min=VS−Vo,pk.
  7. RS=VRS,min/IRS,max.
  8. Quiescent current: ID=VS/RS.
  9. VOV=2ID/Kn, VGS=Vt+VOV, VG=VGS+VS.
  10. Design the divider (type A4) and verify by simulation.

Checks

  • VG<VDD with room for the positive input peak.
  • Saturation at the positive peak: VGD=VG−VD stays below Vt.
  • Simulated output peak-to-peak meets the requirement; the readings agree with the hand values.

Variants

  • Smaller RL or larger swing: IL,pk grows, RS falls and ID rises.
  • Output clips on the negative peak: the transistor reaches cutoff, so raise ID,min.
  • Different device: only the VOV and VG steps change.

Common mistakes

  • Using peak-to-peak instead of peak for IL,pk and Vo,pk.
  • Computing RS from ID alone and forgetting the load current.
  • Expecting a gain of 1 into a low-resistance load.

Worked in these notes: pp. 19-5 to 19-8.

A4. Designing the gate divider for a target VG and input resistance

Recognize it

You know the required gate voltage and a minimum Rin; choose RG1 and RG2.

Method

  1. VG/VDD=RG2/(RG1+RG2) and Rin=RG1∥RG2=RG1VG/VDD.
  2. RG1≥RinVDD/VG.
  3. RG2=RG1/(VDD/VG−1).
  4. Recompute the built VG and Rin.

Common mistakes

  • Including the MOSFET in the divider (the gate draws no current).
  • Swapping which resistor goes to VDD and which to ground.

Worked in these notes: step 12, p. 19-8.

A5. Sketching the circuit waveforms and checking the region of operation

Recognize it

Draw VL, iL, VRS, iRS and iD against time for a given sine output, and decide whether the transistor stays in saturation.

Method

  1. VL: a sine of the output peak Vo,pk about zero (the coupling capacitor removes the DC).
  2. iL=VL/RL.
  3. VRS=VS+VL and iRS=VRS/RS.
  4. KCL at the source node: iD=iRS+iL.
  5. Mark each maximum and minimum with a dashed line; all five waveforms are in phase.
  6. Triode check: VGD=VG−VD against Vt.

Checks

  • The minimum of iD equals ID,min and its average equals the quiescent ID.

Common mistakes

  • Centering VRS at zero instead of VS.
  • Writing iD=iRS−iL.

Worked in these notes: p. 19-7.

A6. Measuring distortion with the Multisim distortion analyzer

Recognize it

"Quantify the distortion of the output" in a simulation.

Method

  1. Place the distortion analyzer (XDA1) and connect its input to the output node.
  2. Set the fundamental frequency to the input frequency (1 kHz), the resolution to a value that divides it (100 Hz), and the display to percent.
  3. Run an interactive simulation and read the THD.

Common mistakes

  • Connecting the analyzer to the input instead of the output.
  • A fundamental setting that differs from the source frequency gives a meaningless reading.

Worked in these notes: pp. 19-8 and 19-10.
