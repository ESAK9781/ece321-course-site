Lesson 20: Common-Gate Amplifier and Partially Bypassed Common-Source Design

20-1

Common Gate Amplifier

(*) We've now introduced, characterized, and designed two types of NMOS-based amplifiers

Common Source

  • Ri RG1∥RG2
  • Ro ≈RD
  • Avo ≈−RD1/gm+RS1

Common Drain

  • Ri RG1∥RG2
  • Ro ≈1/gm
  • Avo ≈RSRS+1/gm
Expansion note: where each characteristic comes from

In the common-source stage the input sees only the gate divider, so Ri=RG1∥RG2 because no DC or signal current enters the gate. The output looks back into the drain resistor RD (the transistor's ro is ignored unless λ matters). The gain is the drain resistor divided by the total resistance in the source path, 1/gm plus the unbypassed RS1, and it is negative because the stage inverts.

In the common-drain stage the input again sees only the divider. The output looks back into the source, where the transistor offers 1/gm, so Ro≈1/gm. The gain is a voltage divider between RS and 1/gm, a little below 1 and not inverting.

(*) We analyze these amplifier† characteristics using our small-signal models

Hybrid-π Model

T Model

Expansion note: the two small-signal models describe the same transistor

The hybrid-π model controls a current source gmvgs by the voltage between gate and source. The T model replaces that voltage by the current through 1/gm placed in the source lead: the same current i=gmvgs flows in the source resistor, so the two give the same answers. The T model is convenient when something is connected to the source, because 1/gm then sits in series with it and shows up directly in the input resistance. The gate carries no current in either model (ig=0).

20-2

Q. What is the Common Gate topology?

  • The gate is ac-grounded by a bypass capacitor
  • The input source is ac-coupled in to avoid disturbing† the DC† bias voltages
  • The output is taken from the drain and ac-coupled to remove the DC component†
Expansion note: why the gate capacitor makes this a common-gate stage

For DC the divider RG1, RG2 sets the gate voltage. For the signal, CG is a short, so the gate sits at signal ground and is common to the input (source) and the output (drain). Compare the common-source stage, where the source is common, and the common-drain stage, where the drain is at supply (signal ground) and common to both.

(*) Let's small-signal analyze this

→ DC power supplies go to ground

→ Large capacitors go to short circuits

→ Use the T-model because we have RS† in the circuit

Expansion note: why a DC supply becomes ground and a large capacitor a short

A DC supply holds its voltage fixed, so it carries no signal voltage: for small signals its terminal is at ground. A large capacitor has negligible impedance at the signal frequency, so it acts as a short for the signal while still blocking DC. Together these two rules turn the full circuit into the signal-only circuit that the model is dropped into.

Expansion note: why the common-gate stage does not invert

With the gate at ground, a positive input vi at the source gives vgs=−vi. The drain current falls by gmvi: that is the current I=−gmvi in the arrow's direction. The drain current flows through RD from the supply, so less current means a higher drain voltage, Vo=−IRD=+gmRDvi. In the common-source stage the input drives the gate directly, vgs=+vi, and the output falls instead.

Expansion note: where RD and the source current come from in this drawing

The redrawn model shows only the transistor. RD is the drain resistor of the circuit on page 20-2, connected between the d terminal and the supply (signal ground), so the output voltage is the drop it develops from the dependent source's current. The input source vi is across 1/gm and sets that current by Ohm's law.

Expansion note: why zeroing the source opens the dependent source

To find an output resistance, switch off the independent sources. With vi=0 the resistor 1/gm has no voltage across it, so I=gmvi=0. A current source that supplies zero current is an open circuit, so the only path back from the output terminal is RD. If λ is included, ro appears in parallel with the open source and Ro=RD∥ro (Equation Sheet 4 writes Ro≈RD∥ro).

(*) Having a low Ri and high Ro makes the CG amplifier especially† useful for a current† amplifiers. It's good for a current† buffer and it has attractive characteristics for high-Frequency applications

Expansion note: why low input resistance and high output resistance suit a current amplifier

A current amplifier wants to accept a current from the source without dropping much voltage, which needs a small input resistance, and to deliver that current into the load regardless of the load, which needs a large output resistance. The common-gate stage has Ri=RS∥1/gm, small, and Ro≈RD∥ro, moderate to large. The same property makes it a current buffer. Its good high-frequency behavior is a matter of the signal path: the gate is grounded, so there is no gate-to-drain capacitance feeding back from output to input.

Permutations on our CS-Amplifier Design

(*) Recall how we set our DC bias point

Expansion note: what moving the voltage from RS to RD does

The supply is shared by RD, the transistor and RS in series: VRD+VDS+VRS=VDD. The left circuit gives each a third. Taking voltage from RS and giving it to RD (middle) raises the gain for a given current, since RD=VRD/ID is larger, but leaves less RS feedback to hold the bias steady. Giving RS more and RD less (right) does the opposite: a steadier bias point at the cost of gain per unit current. Examples 1 and 2 use the middle choice.

Expansion note: what THD of 0.604% tells you

Total harmonic distortion is the combined size of the harmonics (2 kHz, 3 kHz, ...) that the stage adds to the 1 kHz output, as a percent of the fundamental. A square-law transistor bends the sine slightly, and 0.604% means the extra harmonic content is about 6 parts in a thousand of the output, a small amount for a stage with vgs,pk at 20 mV against Vov = 0.32 V.

Appendix

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Naming an amplifier and listing its Ri, Ro and Avo

Recognize it

"Common (blank) amplifier": a circuit is shown and you must say which terminal is common to input and output and give its three characteristics.

Method

  1. Find where the signal enters and where it leaves: gate in, drain out is common source; gate in, source out is common drain; source in, drain out is common gate.
  2. Replace DC supplies by ground and large capacitors by shorts.
  3. Read the characteristics from the table below (λ ignored).
RiRoAvo
CS (partial bypass)RG1∥RG2≈RD−RD1/gm+RS1
CDRG1∥RG2≈1/gmRSRS+1/gm
CGRS∥1/gm≈RD∥rogmRD (not inverting)

Checks

  • Only the common-source stage inverts.
  • The common-drain gain is below 1; the common-gate gain has the same magnitude as the CS gain without RS1.

Common mistakes

  • Using RG1∥RG2 for the common-gate input resistance: the input is at the source, so it sees 1/gm.
  • Forgetting that the CS gain falls when RS1 is left unbypassed.

Worked in these notes: pp. 20-1, 20-3, 20-4.

A2. Small-signal analysis of the common-gate amplifier

Recognize it

The input source feeds the source terminal and the gate is bypassed to ground. You must find Ri, Avo and Ro.

Method

  1. Zero the DC sources, short the large capacitors, replace the transistor by its T model.
  2. Input resistance: Ri=RS∥(1/gm); for the bare model, 1/gm.
  3. With the gate grounded, vgs=−vi, so the dependent current is I=−gmvi.
  4. No-load output: Vo=−IRD=gmRDvi, so Avo=gmRD.
  5. Output resistance: set vi=0, the dependent source is open, so Ro=RD (with λ: RD∥ro).

Checks

  • The gain is positive. The input resistance is small, the output resistance large.

Variants

  • With a load RL, replace RD by RD∥RL in the gain; with a source resistance Rsig, the signal divides across Rsig and Ri.

Common mistakes

  • Dropping the minus sign in vgs=−vi and concluding that the stage inverts.
  • Leaving RS out of Ri in the full circuit.

Worked in these notes: pp. 20-2 to 20-4.

A3. Splitting the supply between RD, the transistor and RS

Recognize it

"Recall how we set our bias point": the supply is divided among three series elements and the split trades gain, bias stability and swing.

Method

  1. Write VDD=VRD+VDS+VRS.
  2. Start from equal thirds. Give RD more and RS less to get more gain for a given current; give RS more for a steadier bias point.
  3. Keep VDS≥Vov so the device stays in saturation, and note VS=VRS, VD=VDD−VRD.

Common mistakes

  • Taking VDS below Vov (triode).
  • Forgetting to carry the new split into the current formula (Step 2).

Worked in these notes: p. 20-4, Ex. 1 Step 1, Ex. 2 Step 1, p. 20-11.

A4. Designing a common-source amplifier with partial RS feedback

Recognize it

You are given |Avo|, RIN, vg,pk, VDD and the device (Vt, Kn, λ) and a bias split. Choose ID, RD, RS1, RS2, RG1 and RG2.

Method

  1. Choose VRD, VDS, VRS (A3).
  2. ID=2Kn[VRD2|Avo|]2 for RS1=1/gm; with VRD=VDD/3 this is 2Kn[VDD/(6|Avo|)]2.
  3. RD=VRD/ID, RS=VRS/ID.
  4. Vov=2ID/Kn, gm=KnVov, RS1=1/gm, RS2=RS−RS1.
  5. VGS=Vov+Vt, VG=VGS+VS.
  6. Gate divider (A6), swing and linearity checks (A7), simulate (A8).

Checks

  • RS2>0: RS must exceed RS1.
  • VDS=8 V is far above Vov.

Variants

  • A larger gain needs less current (ID goes as 1/|Avo|2); a larger Kn needs more current for the same gain.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing mA/V² with A/V².
  • Using the fully bypassed gain formula gmRD when RS1 is present.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1, pp. 20-5 to 20-7.

A5. Redesigning with a chosen RS1

Recognize it

"Re-design the previous amplifier but set RS1 = ...": the same specifications with a different feedback resistor, so the current formula changes.

Method

  1. Write RS1=(k−1)/gm, so |Avo|=gmRD/k (k = 2 for 1/gm, 2.5 for 1.5/gm).
  2. ID=2Kn[VRDk|Avo|]2.
  3. Repeat Steps 3 to 7 of A4 with RS1=(k−1)/gm; Step 8 does not change.
  4. vgs,pk=vg,pk/(1+gmRS1)=vg,pk/k.

Checks

  • Gain from gmRD/k equals the target.

Common mistakes

  • Keeping the old ID formula. A larger k lowers ID and raises RD.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 2, pp. 20-8 and 20-9.

A6. Designing the gate divider for VG and RIN

Recognize it

The gate voltage and a minimum input resistance are known; choose RG1 and RG2.

Method

  1. RG1≥(VDD/VG)RIN.
  2. RG2=RG1/(VDD/VG−1).
  3. Pick convenient values and recompute VG and RG1∥RG2.

Common mistakes

  • Swapping RG1 and RG2 (inverts the ratio VG/VDD).
  • Loading the divider with the transistor: the gate draws no current.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1 Step 7, Ex. 2 Step 7.

A7. Checking swing and linearity

Recognize it

"Check VD,max and VD,min" and "Check linearity".

Method

  1. VD,max/min=VD±|Avo|vg,pk; both must stay between VG−Vt and VDD.
  2. vgs,pk=vg,pk/(1+gmRS1) and require vgs,pk≤0.2Vov.

Common mistakes

  • Using the whole input in the linearity test even though RS1 takes part of it.
  • Using peak-to-peak values for peak quantities.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1 Steps 8 and 9; Ex. 2 Steps 8 and 9.

A8. Reading the Multisim results

Recognize it

A DC operating-point table, a transient plot with two cursors, and a distortion analyzer reading.

Method

  1. Map nodes: V(2) is the drain, V(3) the gate, V(6) the source; I(MQ1[ID]) is the drain current. Compare them with VD, VG, VS, ID of the hand design.
  2. Gain from the cursors: put them on a crest and a trough, then |Avo|=dy/(2vg,pk).
  3. THD: read the percent shown; compare designs by it.

Common mistakes

  • Using dy/dx as the gain.
  • Dividing by vg,pk instead of its peak-to-peak value.

Worked in these notes: pp. 20-7, 20-10, 20-11.
