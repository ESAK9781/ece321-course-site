Lesson 17: Common-Source Amplifier Design with No Source Resistor

17-1

Common Source Amplifier Design

(*) C1 and C2 couple the† source and load to the amplifier† without a†ffecting the DC biasing (ID and VGS).

The polarity requirement sets the +† side where the DC† voltage is higher†.

Expansion note: why the coupling capacitors leave the bias alone

A capacitor blocks DC, so C1 and C2 keep the DC levels at the gate (set by RG1 and RG2) and at the drain apart from the source and the load. A 1 kHz signal passes with almost no drop because the capacitors are large. Each capacitor ends up holding a DC voltage: the plate on the transistor side sits at the higher DC level (the gate for C1, the drain for C2), so that is the + side.

(*) We found the small-signal gain (no load) is :

Avo = − gm RD = − KnVOV RD

and we know the drain current in saturation

ID = ½ KnVOV2

Expansion note: why the gain can be written two ways

The transconductance is gm=KnVOV, so gmRD and KnVOVRD are the same number. The first form is the circuit view (a current source gmvgs pushing current through RD); the second shows that the gain grows with the overdrive VOV and with RD. The minus sign says the output is inverted.

(*) The gate resistors RG1 and RG2 program the voltage VG

VG = VGS = RG2RG1+RG2 × VDD

Expansion note: why VG and VGS are the same here

With no source resistor the source sits at ground, so VGS=VG−VS=VG−0. The gate draws no DC current, so the divider is unloaded and gives VG=VDDRG2/(RG1+RG2).

Invert the divider equation, VDDVGS=RG1+RG2RG2=RG1RG2+1, then subtract 1. The ratio of the two resistors is fixed by VGS and VDD; the size of each is a separate choice (page 17-3).

The first form comes from |Avo|=KnVOVRD and needs only the gain and VOV. The second is Ohm's law across RD: it carries ID and drops VDD−VDS. Both give the same value when ID=12KnVOV2.

Expansion note: what fixes the size of RG2

The equation above fixes only the ratio RG1/RG2. Together the two resistors form the amplifier's input resistance (found below), so their size is set by how large that input resistance must be: both can be scaled up together without changing VG. Page 17-4, step 4, turns the requirement RIN into a minimum RG1.

(*) Let's loop in our small-signal model

– All DC sources become shorts† → to ground†

– Coupling capacitors → short†

Expansion note: why DC sources and coupling capacitors are shorts for the signal

A small-signal analysis keeps only the changes around the bias point. An ideal DC supply does not change, so it is a 0 V source, a short to ground. A large coupling capacitor has an impedance 1/(2πfC) far below the surrounding resistances at the signal frequency, so it acts as a short as well.

For Ri : leave output open, what resistance "seen" looking into the input (the gate)†

Ri = RG1∥RG2

For Ro : leave output open, what resistance "seen" looking into the output (the drain)† with Vi = 0†

Ro = RD

Expansion note: reading Ri and Ro off the model

The gate draws no current, so the signal source sees only the two divider resistors. With the DC supply shorted, both connect to ground and are in parallel: Ri=RG1∥RG2. For Ro the input is set to zero, so vgs=0 and the dependent source gmvgs is zero, an open circuit. Only RD remains between the output and ground (the model has no ro).

Design a CS-amplifier with 45† < |Avo| < 55†

RIN > 100 kΩ† with vgs,pk = 10 mV† For an NMOS transistor with Kn = 80† mA/V² Vt = 1.8† V.

Expansion note: what the design targets mean

|Avo| is the open-circuit voltage gain wanted, given as a range so a nearby value is acceptable. RIN is the smallest acceptable input resistance, so that the amplifier does not load the signal source. vgs,pk is the peak signal across the gate and source, which sets how much output swing the design must carry and whether the small-signal model is valid (step 6). Kn = 80 mA/V² is 0.080 A/V²; the formulas need one consistent unit system.

  1. Step 1

    Choose a target VDS† → near VDD/2† should ensure maximum swing†. If insufficient legroom (step 5)†, move closer to VDD†

    VDS = VDD/2=6V

    Expansion note: why VDS starts at half the supply

    On the voltage transfer curve the drain can rise to VDD (point A, where the transistor turns off) and fall to VDS,B (point B, where it enters triode). A bias point VDS in the middle leaves equal room above and below for the output swing. Because VDS,B is small but not zero, the exact middle is slightly above VDD/2; if step 5 shows too little room below (legroom), raise VDS toward VDD and repeat.

    Choose |Avo| = 50† and Find VOV

    Expansion note: why 50 and what VOV = 0.24 V implies

    A gain of 50 lies in the range above and gives a round number. With VDS=VDD/2, VOV=VDD/|Avo|=12/50. A small overdrive means a small VDS,B (lots of legroom) but also a small allowed signal, since the linearity check in step 6 limits vgs,pk to 0.2VOV. Do not make VOV too small.

    Expansion note: headroom, legroom and the triode boundary

    The drain voltage swings ±vds,pk about VDS (the source is at ground). Headroom is the room above, up to the supply: VDS,max must stay below VDD. Legroom is the room below, down to the triode boundary: VDS,min must stay above VDS,B, otherwise the bottom of the wave is clipped as the transistor leaves saturation. Here 6.5 V is below 12 V and 5.5 V is far above 0.335 V.

    The boundary is where VDS=VOV. Using VDS=VDD−12KnRDVOV2 with VDS=VOV gives 12KnRDVOV2+VOV−VDD=0, whose positive root is the printed VDS,B (the same value is VOV,B).

    Expansion note: why the signal must stay below 0.2VOV

    The small-signal model replaces iD=12Kn(VOV+vgs)2 by its linear part. The dropped term is 12Knvgs2 against the kept term KnVOVvgs, a ratio of vgs/(2VOV). Requiring vgs≪2VOV is made concrete as vgs,pk≤0.2VOV, which keeps that ratio at 10% or less. The 10 mV signal is 10/240 = 4% of VOV.

– If due to parameter variations† ID ↑† , then

Expansion note: how RS pulls the current back

All of ID flows through RS, so VS=IDRS. Suppose a transistor with a larger Kn (or smaller Vt) tries to carry more current. The extra current raises VS. The gate is held by the divider, so VGS=VG−VS falls, and the square law ID=12Kn(VGS−Vt)2 then lowers ID again. The current ends up much closer to its intended value than without RS. This is negative feedback: the change in the output current feeds back to oppose itself.

Q. How does this change the gain ?

(*) Small-Signal Model → use the T†-model

Expansion note: why the T-model suits a circuit with RS

The T-model carries the same current gmvgs from drain to source as the first model, but it shows a resistor 1/gm between the gate node and the source. Since the gate draws no current, the transistor looks like 1/gm in the source path, and an external RS simply adds in series with it. That makes the effect of RS on the gain a series-resistance calculation.

Expansion note: the same result from the loop current

The same current I flows through 1/gm and RS in series, so I=vi/(1/gm+RS). That current is the drain current, and it flows through RD upward from ground, so vo=−IRD. Dividing by vi gives Avo=−RD/(1/gm+RS). Multiplying top and bottom by gm gives the second form. With RS=0 it reduces to the earlier −gmRD.

(*) IF RS ≥† RD , then Avo ≤ 1†

Expansion note: why a large RS caps the gain at 1 in magnitude

The denominator 1/gm+RS is larger than RS, so |Avo|=RD/(1/gm+RS)<RD/RS. If RS≥RD the ratio RD/RS is at most 1, so the gain magnitude is below 1: the stage no longer amplifies. (The sign of Avo is negative; the limit is on its magnitude.)

RS : source degeneration resistance

(*) We can bypass† all or part of RS† to balance† stabilizing the bias† versus maintaining the gain†

Expansion note: what bypassing part of RS buys

A capacitor across a resistor is open for DC and a short for the signal. If it is placed across all of RS, the bias keeps the full feedback but the signal sees RS=0 and the full gain −gmRD. If it is placed across only RS2, the signal still sees RS1, so the gain is −RD/(1/gm+RS1): less gain, but the signal vgs is smaller and the stage stays linear for a larger input. The split is a trade between bias stability and gain.

Q What does that mean ?

Avo = −RD1/gm+RS

  • RS = RS1 + RS2 is there to stabilize the operating point† for variations in Kn† and Vt†
  • We do not want RS too large† or else it swamps out the 1/gm†
  • We do not want RS too small† or else the operating point† varies too much with Kn and Vt†

→ We will choose RS = RD†

Expansion note: why RS cannot be too large or too small

Too large: when RS≫1/gm the gain approaches −RD/RS, set by two resistors and no longer by the transistor, and it may fall below 1. Too small: when RS≪1/gm the voltage IDRS is only a few tenths of a volt, so a change in ID barely moves VGS and the feedback does little. The way out is to let RS be large enough for good bias stability, and then bypass all or part of it so that the signal does not pay for it in gain.

Expansion note: how RS is sized in the next design

The partial-bypass design in Equation Sheet 4 splits the supply into thirds, VRD=VDS=VRS=VDD/3. RD and RS then carry the same current and drop the same voltage, so RS=RD=(VDD/3)/ID.

Appendix

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Designing a common-source amplifier with no source resistor

Recognize it

You are asked for a CS amplifier with a gain range, a minimum input resistance RIN, a signal size vgs,pk, a supply VDD and a device (Kn, Vt). The source of the NMOS goes straight to ground, there are two coupling capacitors, and you must find RD, RG1 and RG2.

Method

  1. Pick VDS=VDD/2 (type A2).
  2. Pick |Avo| inside the allowed range and compute VOV=2(VDD−VDS)|Avo|.
  3. RD=|Avo|KnVOV.
  4. VGS=VOV+Vt (which is also VG).
  5. Gate divider: RG1≥VDDVGSRIN and RG2=RG1VDD/VGS−1 (type A3).
  6. Check headroom and legroom (type A2) and linearity (type A4).
  7. Simulate and compare (type A5).

Checks

  • VDS,min>VDS,B and VDS,max<VDD.
  • vgs,pk≤0.2VOV.
  • ID=12KnVOV2 times RD equals VDD−VDS (here 2.304 mA × 2604 Ω = 6.0 V).

Variants

  • A different VDS target changes VOV and everything after it; the only equation that changes is step 2.
  • If the gain target is high, VOV becomes small and the linearity limit on vgs,pk tightens.
  • A standard resistor value slightly off the computed one shifts the bias a little; re-evaluate with the built value.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing mA/V² and A/V²: 80 mA/V² is 0.08 A/V².
  • Using VGS where VDD/VGS is needed in the divider formulas (inverting the ratio).
  • Forgetting that the gain target fixes VOV first, so ID is a result, not a choice.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1, pages 17-4 and 17-5; derivation on pages 17-1 to 17-3.

A2. Choosing VDS and checking headroom and legroom

Recognize it

"Choose a target VDS" and "make sure there is sufficient headroom and legroom": decide where on the voltage transfer curve the amplifier sits and whether the output swing fits.

Method

  1. Start at VDS=VDD/2, near the middle of the saturation segment.
  2. Output swing: vds,pk=|Avo|vgs,pk.
  3. VDS,max=VDS+vds,pk and VDS,min=VDS−vds,pk.
  4. Triode edge: VDS,B=1+2KnRDVDD−1KnRD.
  5. Headroom if VDS,max<VDD; legroom if VDS,min>VDS,B.

Checks

  • Both inequalities hold with some margin, not just barely.
  • In saturation, VDS≥VOV at the bias point.

Variants

  • Too little legroom: raise VDS toward VDD and redo the design.
  • Too little headroom: lower VDS a little (toward VDS,B).

Common mistakes

  • Testing the swing against 0 V instead of VDS,B; below VDS,B the transistor is already in triode.
  • Confusing VOV (a bias quantity) with vgs,pk (a signal amplitude).

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1, step 1 (page 17-4) and step 5 (page 17-5).

A3. Sizing the gate divider from VGS and RIN

Recognize it

"Find RG1 and RG2": the gate must sit at VGS and the input resistance must be at least RIN.

Method

  1. Divider ratio: RG2RG1+RG2=VGSVDD.
  2. Input resistance: RG1∥RG2=RG1VGSVDD≥RIN, so RG1≥VDDVGSRIN.
  3. Choose RG1 at or above that value (a standard value).
  4. Then RG2=RG1VDD/VGS−1.

Checks

  • RG1∥RG2≥RIN with the values actually chosen.
  • VDDRG2/(RG1+RG2) reproduces VGS.

Variants

  • A larger RIN scales both resistors up together; the gate voltage is unchanged.
  • Very large resistors make the bias sensitive to leakage; very small ones load the signal and waste supply current.

Common mistakes

  • Using RIN=RG1 or RG2 alone instead of the parallel combination.
  • Swapping RG1 (top, to VDD) and RG2 (bottom, to ground).

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1, step 4 (pages 17-4 and 17-5); derivation on pages 17-1 to 17-3.

A4. Checking that the small-signal model is valid

Recognize it

"Check the assumption vgs,pk≤0.2VOV": the amplifier equations are only accurate for small signals.

Method

  1. Take the peak signal across gate and source (vgs,pk).
  2. Compute the limit 0.2VOV.
  3. Compare.

Checks

  • The distortion term is about vgs/(2VOV) of the signal term; at the limit it is 10%.

Variants

  • If the check fails, lower the gain (raising VOV) or lower the input signal.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing the output swing vds,pk instead of vgs,pk.
  • Using VGS in place of VOV in the limit.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1, step 6 (page 17-5).

A5. Reading a Multisim check: DC operating point and gain from cursors

Recognize it

"Confirm in simulation": a schematic, a DC operating point table and a transient plot with two cursors.

Method

  1. Read the DC table: V(2) is the drain, V(3) the gate, I(MQ1[ID]) the drain current.
  2. Compare with the hand design: VG, ID and VD=VDD−IDRD.
  3. On the plot, put one cursor on a crest and one on a trough; dy is the output peak-to-peak.
  4. Divide by the input peak-to-peak (twice the source's peak) and add the minus sign for inversion: Av=−dy/(2vs,pk).

Checks

  • Is the simulated bias within a few percent of the hand design, and is the gain close to the target?
  • Do the cursor x values differ by half a period (dx=−0.5 ms for 1 kHz)?

Variants

  • Swap the transistor (for example a higher Vt) and repeat to see how much the bias and gain move (type A6).

Common mistakes

  • Reading dy/dx as the gain; it is a slope in V/s.
  • Dividing by the source's peak instead of its peak-to-peak.

Worked in these notes: pages 17-6 and 17-7.

A6. Predicting the effect of a different transistor (Vt or Kn spread)

Recognize it

"What changes if Vt = … for a different transistor from the bin?" The resistors stay the same and only the device changes.

Method

  1. The divider fixes VGS (with no RS), so recompute VOV=VGS−Vt for the new Vt.
  2. ID=12KnVOV2, VD=VDD−IDRD, gm=KnVOV.
  3. The gain magnitude is gmRD (times the loading factor if a load is present).

Checks

  • The drain voltage must stay above VOV for the device to remain in saturation.

Variants

  • A different Kn scales ID directly and gm in proportion to KnVOV.
  • With a source resistor the same change moves ID much less (type A8).

Common mistakes

  • Assuming ID stays constant because the resistors did not change.

Worked in these notes: the question on page 17-6 and the second simulation on page 17-7.

A7. Small-signal Ri, Ro and Avo, with and without RS

Recognize it

"Loop in our small-signal model": draw the equivalent circuit and read off the input resistance, output resistance and open-circuit gain.

Method

  1. Short the DC supply and the coupling capacitors.
  2. Ri: leave the output open; the gate takes no current, so Ri=RG1∥RG2.
  3. Ro: set vi=0 so the dependent source is zero; Ro=RD.
  4. Gain: with RS, vgs=vi1/gm1/gm+RS, the current is I=vi/(1/gm+RS) and vo=−IRD.

Checks

  • Setting RS=0 must give back −gmRD.

Variants

  • With a load, replace RD by RD∥RL, or use Av=AvoRL/(RL+Ro).
  • With partial bypass, only RS1 appears in the signal path.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to short VDD, which hides that RG1 and RG2 are in parallel.
  • Leaving RS out of the T-model when it is not bypassed.

Worked in these notes: pages 17-3, 17-8 and 17-9.

A8. Choosing a source resistor: bias stability against gain

Recognize it

"How does this change the gain?" and "What does that mean?": decide how large RS should be and whether to bypass it.

Method

  1. Understand the feedback: an increase in ID raises VS=IDRS, lowers VGS, and pulls ID back.
  2. Gain with RS unbypassed: Avo=−RD/(1/gm+RS); if RS≥RD then |Avo|<1.
  3. Choose RS large enough for stable bias but not so large that it swamps 1/gm.
  4. Bypass all of RS (full gain, full feedback on DC) or only RS2 (the signal still sees RS1).

Checks

  • With all of RS bypassed the gain equals the no-RS value −gmRD.

Variants

  • Partial bypass trades some gain for a smaller vgs and better linearity.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing RS by looking only at the gain; the point of RS is the DC bias.
  • Thinking a bypass capacitor removes RS for DC; it does not.

Worked in these notes: pages 17-8 to 17-10.
