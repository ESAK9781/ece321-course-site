Lesson 30: CMOS Inverter VTC, Noise Margin and Asymmetry

30-1

Review: Synthesizing a circuit from a logic expression

ex. Create the PUN & PDN for  Y = A·B + C·D

STEP 1: PUN → As written, creating the PUN is difficult → so, apply DeMorgan's theorem

Y = A·B · C·D →

Y = (A + B)(C + D) →

Remember, MOS works with complemented inputs

Expansion note: why DeMorgan's theorem is needed for the PUN

A PMOS conducts when its gate is low, so a PMOS path switches on when the input is 0, that is, when the complemented input is 1. A pull-up network must therefore be written as an expression in complemented inputs. The expression as given, A·B + C·D, is a complement of a sum of products, which is awkward to read as a network. DeMorgan's theorem, X + Z = X·Z and X·Z = X + Z, pushes the bar down onto the inputs, giving Y = (A + B)(C + D).

Expansion note: reading a network from the expression

Two rules turn an expression into a network: a product (AND) is a series connection, because every transistor in the chain must conduct, and a sum (OR) is a parallel connection, because any one branch may conduct. In the PUN, (A + B) is PMOS A in parallel with PMOS B, and the product of the two sums puts those two parallel pairs in series. In the PDN the expression is used as written with NMOS devices, which conduct on a high input.

STEP 2: PDN → Y = A·B + C·D, as written: AND is series, OR is parallel

Expansion note: why the two networks never conduct together

For every input combination exactly one of the networks connects the output to a rail. If A·B + C·D is 1, the PDN conducts and pulls the output to ground, while the PUN, written in the complemented inputs, is open; otherwise the PUN conducts and the PDN is open. That is why a CMOS gate draws no static current.

CMOS Inverter VTC: Noise Margin & Asymmetry

(∗) We will initially consider matched devices

Q. What does that imply?

(1)  Vtn = |Vtp|

(2)  Kn = Kp

Kn =kn′(W/L)n = Kp

(W/L)p = μnμp (W/L)n

Expansion note: what "matched" means and why it is the starting point

A matched inverter has equal thresholds, Vtn=|Vtp|, and equal transconductance parameters, Kn=Kp, where K=k′(W/L) and k′=μCox. With equal strength in both directions the transfer curve is symmetric about VDD/2, the pull-up and pull-down currents are equal, and the two noise margins come out equal. Unmatched devices are treated later in the lesson as a departure from this case.

The channel lengths are made all the same. So,

Wp = kn′kp′ Wn

or

Wp = μnμp Wn ≈ 2 to 4 Wn for silicon

Expansion note: why the PMOS is made two to four times wider

With equal lengths, Kn=Kp requires μnCox(W/L)n=μpCox(W/L)p, so Wp/Wn=μn/μp. Holes in silicon move more slowly than electrons, μp is about 0.25 to 0.5 of μn, and so the ratio is about 2 to 4. The price is silicon area, which the example on pages 30-11 and 30-12 quantifies.

Q. What do we expect as the VTC basic shape?

Multisim

    Kn = 0.81 mA/V²†  Kp = 0.81 mA/V²†
  • Perform DC Sweep → Use the input source Vin → choose VOUT, the inverter output node
Expansion note: why a DC sweep gives the VTC

The voltage-transfer characteristic is the steady-state output voltage for each steady input voltage. A DC sweep steps the input source slowly from 0 to VDD, solves the circuit at every step, and plots the output node against the swept source, which is exactly that curve. The matched values here follow the lesson's later 65-nm example, with K=(540μA/V2)(1.5) = 0.81 mA/V² for both devices.

30-3
Expansion note: why the VTC has this shape

With the input low, QN is off and QP is on, so the output sits at VOH=VDD. With the input high the roles reverse and the output sits at VOL=0. Between the two, both transistors conduct for a narrow range of input around VDD/2, and because neither has a significant output resistance in saturation (λ = 0) the gain there is very large, so the curve drops almost vertically. The sketch has five segments, which the following pages identify as A to B, B to C and so on.

(∗) Now, let's add VIL and VIH then recall how those voltage levels relate to NML and NMH

Note,

VIL:  Vin interpreted as logic 0 (low)

VIH:  Vin interpreted as logic 1 (high)

NML = VIL − VOL    NMH = VOH − VIH

Expansion note: what the noise margins measure

VIL is the highest input that a following gate must still read as a logic 0, and VIH is the lowest input it must still read as a logic 1. A driver guarantees outputs of at least VOH for a 1 and at most VOL for a 0, so the room between what a driver delivers and what a receiver accepts is NMH=VOH−VIH for a high and NML=VIL−VOL for a low. Noise smaller than the margin cannot flip the logic level.

The standard way to define VIL and VIH is to require them to have:

slope = −1

So

VIL must be in region A to B

VIH must be in region C to D

Expansion note: why the unity-gain points mark the logic thresholds

Where the magnitude of the slope is below 1 the output changes less than the input, so noise is shrinking as it passes through the gate. Where it is above 1, noise is amplified and the level is on its way to flipping. The points where the slope reaches −1 are the borders between the two behaviours: input levels outside them (below VIL or above VIH) are restored toward a rail. Region A to B holds the left unity-slope point and C to D the right one.

Cutoff, saturation and triode are defined with VGSN and VDSN, but the plot has VIN and VOUT on its axes. For QP the source is at VDD, so VSGP=VDD−VIN and VSDP=VDD−VOUT. Cutoff, VSGP<Vt, becomes VIN>VDD−Vt. Q. How much does the midpoint VM shift if our transistors are mismatched?

Note at Vm

(1)  VIN = VOUT = Vm

(2)  PMOS & NMOS in saturation

So, once again set

IDN = IDP

Expansion note: why both transistors are saturated at Vm

Vm is the input value at which the output equals the input. In the matched inverter that point lies on the vertical segment between B and C, where both devices are saturated and the gain is infinite (for λ = 0). In an unmatched inverter the segment moves left or right but stays vertical, so the same assumption holds and the saturation current equations of both devices apply.

Expansion note: why taking the square root helps

Equating the two saturation currents gives a quadratic in Vm if the squares are expanded. Both sides are perfect squares, however, so taking the square root of each side gives a relation that is linear in Vm. The only care needed is the sign: with both Vm−Vtn and VDD−Vm−|Vtp| positive (both devices are on at Vm), the positive roots are used on both sides.

Expansion note: how r describes the mismatch

The parameter r=Kp/Kn measures how much stronger the PMOS is than the NMOS. r=1 is the matched inverter. With r=1 and |Vtp|=Vtn the formula gives Vm=VDD/2. A weaker PMOS (r<1) is overpowered by the NMOS, so the output reaches the input level earlier and Vm moves below VDD/2; a stronger PMOS moves it above. Vm is a weak function of r because it appears in both numerator and denominator.

  • To avoid recomputing VIL (VIH), assume they change by the same amount, ΔVm at both thresholds and in the same direction as Vm.
Expansion note: why it is acceptable to estimate the shift of VIL and VIH

Redoing the unity-gain derivation for an unmatched inverter is long. Moving the transfer curve left or right by ΔVm moves VIL and VIH by about the same amount, so NML changes by about +ΔVm and NMH by about −ΔVm. The estimate is good when the curve keeps its shape, which holds for modest changes of r.

Expansion note: how Wn and Wp come out of the aspect ratio

The length is fixed by the process, L=65 nm, so Wn=(W/L)nL=1.5(65 nm)=97.5 nm. Equal lengths and matching require Wp=(μn/μp)Wn=5.4(97.5 nm)=526.5 nm, which is about 5.4 times wider than the NMOS, larger than the usual 2 to 4 because this process has a large mobility ratio.

NML = VIL−VOL = 0.4625 − 0 = 0.4625 V ≈ 0.46 V

Expansion note: how close these margins are to the ideal

The best possible margin for a supply of 1.0 V would be 0.5 V, half the supply. The matched inverter gives 0.4625 V, about 93% of that. Equal noise margins are the benefit of the symmetric design, and they are slightly smaller than VDD/2 because the transition region is not infinitely sharp.

Appendix

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Synthesizing a CMOS pull-up and pull-down network from a logic expression

Recognize it

You are given a logic function such as Y = A·B + C·D and asked for the transistor circuit that implements it.

Method

  1. PDN: use the expression as written with NMOS devices. A product (AND) of terms is a series path; a sum (OR) is a parallel pair of paths.
  2. PUN: apply DeMorgan's theorem until the bars sit on single inputs, which gives an expression in complemented inputs.
  3. Build the PUN from PMOS devices with the same rule: product is series, sum is parallel.
  4. Connect the PUN between VDD and the output, and the PDN between the output and ground.

Checks

  • The PUN and PDN are duals: where one has series devices the other has parallel devices.
  • For every input combination exactly one network conducts.

Variants

  • A different expression only changes how the AND and OR groups are nested.

Common mistakes

  • Using the uncomplemented expression for the PMOS network.
  • Putting NMOS in the pull-up network; they pass only a degraded high level.

Worked in these notes: the review, p. 30-1.

A2. Reading the matched inverter's VTC and marking VOH, VOL, VM, VIL and VIH

Recognize it

You are asked to sketch the transfer curve of a matched CMOS inverter, or to read levels and thresholds off one.

Method

  1. Mark VOH=VDD and VOL=0 on the vertical axis.
  2. Mark VM=VDD/2 for a matched device; the steep segment lies there between VDD/2±Vt.
  3. Draw the flat part at VDD up to VIN=Vt and the flat part at 0 beyond VDD−Vt.
  4. Join the pieces with the curve and mark VIL and VIH where the slope is −1.

Checks

  • The curve is symmetric about the point (VDD/2,VDD/2).
  • VIL<VM<VIH.

Common mistakes

  • Placing VIL and VIH at the corners of the curve instead of where the slope is exactly −1.

Worked in these notes: pp. 30-2 to 30-4.

A3. Finding the operating mode of QN and QP for a given VIN and VOUT

Recognize it

You are asked whether each transistor is in cutoff, saturation or triode at a given point of the VTC.

Method

  1. Convert to device voltages: VGSN=VIN, VDSN=VOUT, VSGP=VDD−VIN, VSDP=VDD−VOUT.
  2. NMOS: off if VIN<Vt; saturated if VOUT>VIN−Vt; triode if VOUT<VIN−Vt.
  3. PMOS: off if VIN>VDD−Vt; saturated if VOUT<VIN+Vt; triode if VOUT>VIN+Vt.

Checks

  • On the steep segment, both are saturated; at the ends one is off and the other is in triode.

Common mistakes

  • Using VOUT for the PMOS without converting to VSDP.

Worked in these notes: pp. 30-4 and 30-5.

A4. Deriving VIH and VIL for a matched inverter

Recognize it

You must derive the unity-gain input levels from the transistor equations.

Method

  1. At VIH, QN is in triode and QP in saturation: set IDN=IDP and substitute VGSN=VIN, VDSN=Vo, VSGP=VDD−VIN.
  2. Set Kn=Kp and cancel.
  3. Differentiate both sides implicitly with respect to VIN (product and chain rules).
  4. Set VIN=VIH and dVo/dVIN=−1 to find Vo=VIH−VDD/2.
  5. Substitute back into the current equation, expand, gather and solve: VIH=18(5VDD−2Vt).
  6. By symmetry, VIL=VDD−VIH=18(3VDD+2Vt).

Checks

  • Check that VIL<VDD/2<VIH and that Vo>0 at VIH (here Vo=VIH−VDD/2).

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the chain-rule factor dVo/dVIN on the Vo2 term.
  • Omitting the −1 from the derivative of VDD−VIN−Vt.

Worked in these notes: pp. 30-6 to 30-8.

A5. Computing noise margins

Recognize it

You are asked for NMH and NML of an inverter, given VDD and Vt (matched), or its measured levels.

Method

  1. Find VOH, VOL, VIH and VIL. For a matched inverter these are VDD, 0, 18(5VDD−2Vt) and 18(3VDD+2Vt).
  2. NMH=VOH−VIH and NML=VIL−VOL.

Checks

  • For a matched inverter NMH=NML=18(3VDD+2Vt), just under VDD/2.

Variants

  • For an unmatched inverter, estimate NML≈NML,matched+ΔVm and NMH≈NMH,matched−ΔVm.

Common mistakes

  • Subtracting in the wrong order (a noise margin must come out positive).

Worked in these notes: pp. 30-3, 30-9 and 30-11 (c).

A6. Sizing Wp for a given Vm, and finding Vm for a given sizing

Recognize it

Either "find Wp so that Vm=VDD/2" or "find the new Vm if Wp is changed".

Method

  1. Matched case: Wp=(μn/μp)Wn for equal lengths; Wn=(W/L)nL.
  2. Any other sizing: r=Kp/Kn=μp(W/L)p/μn(W/L)n.
  3. Vm=Vtn+r(VDD−|Vtp|)1+r.
  4. ΔVm=Vm−VDD/2.

Checks

  • r=1 gives Vm=VDD/2 if Vtn=|Vtp|.
  • r<1 gives Vm<VDD/2 and r>1 gives Vm>VDD/2.

Common mistakes

  • Inverting the ratio inside r (it is PMOS over NMOS).
  • Forgetting the square root.

Worked in these notes: pp. 30-9 to 30-12 (a) and (d).

A7. Trading silicon area against noise margin

Recognize it

You are asked for the silicon area of a sizing and how much it saves compared with the matched design.

Method

  1. Area of the two channels: A=L(Wn+Wp).
  2. Compute the area for each sizing.
  3. Percent reduction = (Amatched−Anew)/Amatched×100.
  4. Compare with the loss of NML estimated from ΔVm.

Checks

  • A narrower PMOS must give a smaller area and a smaller NML.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that the area is L times the sum of both widths, not a product.

Worked in these notes: p. 30-11 (b) and p. 30-12 (e).

A8. Simulating the VTC in Multisim

Recognize it

You are asked to confirm the shape of the transfer curve with a simulation.

Method

  1. Place the virtual NMOS and PMOS and wire them as an inverter.
  2. Edit the thresholds and transconductance parameters to the matched values.
  3. Run a DC sweep of the input source from 0 to VDD and plot the output node.

Checks

  • The curve is flat at the two ends and falls steeply near VDD/2.

Common mistakes

  • Using different K values for the two devices and then expecting a symmetric curve.

C. Equations Used Throughout

C1. Transistor currents

NMOS triode: IDN=Kn[(VGSN−Vt)VDSN−12VDSN2]

NMOS saturation: IDN=12Kn(VGSN−Vt)2

PMOS triode: IDP=Kp[(VSGP−Vt)VSDP−12VSDP2]

PMOS saturation: IDP=12Kp(VSGP−Vt)2

transconductance parameter: K=k′WL   k′=μCox

C2. Voltage relations of the inverter

VGSN=VIN   VDSN=VOUT

VSGP=VDD−VIN   VSDP=VDD−VOUT

VOH=VDD   VOL=0

C3. Matched inverter thresholds and margins

VIH=18(5VDD−2Vt)   VIL=18(3VDD+2Vt)

NMH=VOH−VIH   NML=VIL−VOL

NMH=NML=18(3VDD+2Vt)

C4. Sizing and switching voltage

Wp=μnμpWn   r=KpKn

Vm=Vtn+r(VDD−|Vtp|)1+r   ΔVm=Vm−VDD2

NML≈NML,matched+ΔVm   A=L(Wn+Wp)
