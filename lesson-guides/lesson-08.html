Lesson 8: MOSFET Structure, Channel Formation and I-V Characteristics

Chapter 5

Idea: Use the voltage† at one terminal to control the current† between other terminals.

(*) Let’s make an n†-channel MOSFET, then figure out the resulting electrical† characteristics.

STEP 1: Dope silicon† material with N impurity.

STEP 2: Grow a silicon-dioxide† layer on the top ≈ [not specified]† nm.

Expansion note: why the oxide is patterned before the source and drain are formed

The gate oxide must insulate the gate from the silicon surface, while the source and drain need direct semiconductor junctions into the substrate. Patterning opens selected regions for later processing while retaining oxide beneath the eventual gate.

Expansion note: why the MOSFET has three signal terminals although its structure shows a body

The physical device includes a body (bulk) contact, making four terminals in general. Introductory circuit analysis commonly ties the body to the source or a fixed reference, leaving gate, drain and source as the three independently used terminals. The note’s exact tie is not legible, so the guide names the common convention without treating it as a universal connection.

8-3

(*) We will see soon why this is n†-channel and why it is in enhancement† mode. The circuit symbol for it is:

We apply VGS† to control how much current flows from drain† to source†. Consider the subscript labeling of VGS, VDS, VBS† and ID, IS, IG†, where the SiO2 means silicon dioxide (the gate insulator)†.

Expansion note: distinguish conventional current from electron motion

For an n-channel device, electrons drift from source toward drain when VDS is positive. Conventional current uses positive-charge direction, so ID is drawn into the drain and IS leaves the source. The gate current is ideally zero because the oxide insulates the gate.

(*) Recall, our goal with transistors is to produce analog† and digital† circuits with microprocessor chips containing billions† of devices.

8-4

Let’s Create a Channel: Ground source and apply VGS

(*) The positive† charge on the gate† causes electrons† to be attracted† just under the oxide†.

It also attracts† electrons from the p†-type material, leading to the channel becoming n†-type. So we now have a conducting† path from source† to drain†. If we apply VDS†, current will flow†.

(*) The value of VGS† where sufficient mobile electrons† accumulate to form a conducting channel† is called the threshold† voltage and denoted Vt†. For this enhancement NMOS, Vt > 0†; a conducting channel requires VGS > Vt†.

Expansion note: why a positive gate makes an n-channel in a p-type substrate

The positive gate voltage establishes an electric field through the oxide. It repels holes from the silicon surface and attracts electrons; once the surface electron concentration is sufficient, the surface becomes an n-type inversion layer connecting the n+ source and drain. The oxide blocks dc conduction into the gate.

8-5

(*) The electric† field across the channel† controls the amount of current† in the channel†. Hence VGS controls channel charge† → hence drain current†.

The amount of voltage above the threshold applied to VGS is called the over†drive voltage, so VOV=VGS−Vt.

(*) The charge in the channel (Recall Q = CV†) is given by

|Qchan|=(Cox×WL)×VOV,Cox=εoxtox.

Apply small VDS with channel (≈ a few† mV) → electrons† carry current from source† to drain† (hence names).

The charge per unit channel length† is |Qchan|length=CoxWVOV.

The VDS establishes an electric field |E|=VDSL.

Expansion note: why oxide capacitance is specified per unit area

The gate and inversion layer act like the plates of a capacitor separated by the oxide. A parallel-plate approximation gives capacitance per area Cox=εox/tox. Multiplying by channel area WL gives total gate-to-channel capacitance, and multiplying by the overdrive gives the magnitude of channel charge in this simple uniform-charge picture.

(*) This electric field causes electron drift† with velocity magnitude vn,drift=μn|E|=μnVDSL.

Current is drift velocity × charge/length, or

ID=CoxWVOVVDSL.

Regrouping and substituting:

ID=μnCoxWL(VGS−Vt)VDS.

Where Kn′=μnCox (process parameter); Kn=Kn′WL (MOSFET parameter).

Thus VDSID=r=1KnVOV=LμnCoxWVOV and for a given VGS, the channel looks like a resistor†.

Expansion note: how the small-VDS resistance follows from the current equation

With VGS fixed and VDS small, ID=KnVOVVDS. Rearranging gives VDS/ID=1/(KnVOV). This is the channel’s approximate resistance in the low-field region; it changes with gate overdrive, so the MOSFET is not simply a fixed resistor.

(*) To conduct, an inversion channel† must be induced:

  • VGS>Vt
  • ID>0 and IG=0

VDS increased. Channel depth depends on local gate-to-channel voltage†. Voltage between gate† and channel† along the channel† decreases from Vt+VOV at source† to Vt+VOV−VDS at drain†. At VDS=VOV → channel no longer extends to the drain† → resistance increases† → so ID–VDS curve bends over†.

The key is that the voltage varies and has an average at the middle† of the channel, so we replace VOV in ID=(KnVOV)VDS by VOV−VDS2:

ID=Kn(VOV−VDS2)VDS.

Expansion note: why the average channel overdrive decreases by VDS/2

In the gradual-channel approximation, the channel potential rises from approximately 0 V at the source to VDS at the drain. The local overdrive therefore falls linearly from VOV to VOV−VDS. Its spatial average is VOV−VDS/2, which produces the long-channel triode-region equation shown here.

VDS≤VOV.

(*) Above VOV, increasing VDS does not deepen† the channel†; the zero-overdrive† point of the channel at the drain† gives rise to pinch-off† through the channel. We call this saturation†.

ID=KnVOV22=Kn2(VGS−Vt)2.

Pinch-off does NOT imply zero current†, as electrons† are accelerated through the high-field pinch-off region†.

Expansion note: why pinch-off does not turn the transistor off

At the saturation boundary the channel charge reaches zero at the drain end, but the source continues injecting electrons. They traverse the short depletion/high-field region to the drain. Thus the ideal square-law model predicts a nonzero current that stays approximately constant as VDS increases further; real devices have additional effects such as channel-length modulation that this packet does not develop.

Appendix

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Predict the long-channel NMOS operating region and drain current

Recognize it. The available quantities include VGS, Vt, VDS, and Kn (or μn, Cox, W and L). The desired result is whether the channel conducts and whether it is in triode or saturation, with ID.

  1. Find overdrive: VOV=VGS−Vt. If VOV≤0, the enhancement NMOS is off in the ideal model and ID≈0.
  2. If VOV>0, compare VDS to VOV. For 0≤VDS<VOV, use ID=Kn(VOVVDS−VDS2/2).
  3. For VDS≥VOV, use saturation current ID=KnVOV2/2.
  4. If geometry and process data are supplied, compute Kn=μnCox(W/L); keep units consistent. Check that the selected region inequality matches the chosen equation.

Common mistakes. Using VGS instead of VOV; treating pinch-off as zero current; using the saturation formula below its boundary; or confusing electron motion with conventional current direction.

Worked in these notes. The model is developed on pp. 8-5–8-8; this packet contains no numerical transistor problem.

B. Topics Covered by the Lesson 8 Notes

Device fabrication and terminals (pp. 8-1–8-3); inversion-channel formation and threshold (p. 8-4); charge, drift and the low-VDS model (pp. 8-5–8-6); channel pinch-off and the ideal saturation model (pp. 8-7–8-8).

B1. MOSFET structure and processing
  • N-channel enhancement MOSFET formed in p-type silicon (pp. 8-1–8-3).
  • Gate oxide, source/drain regions, contacts, and channel dimensions L and W (pp. 8-1–8-2).
  • Gate, drain, source and body terminals; gate oxide and the two internal pn junctions (pp. 8-2–8-3).
B2. Inversion and threshold
  • Positive gate voltage attracts electrons to the surface of a p-type substrate (p. 8-4).
  • Inversion channel connects n+ source and drain; Vt marks its onset (p. 8-4).
  • Overdrive is VOV=VGS−Vt (p. 8-5).
B3. Channel charge and low-field current
  • Oxide capacitance per area Cox=εox/tox and channel charge (p. 8-5).
  • Drain-source electric field produces electron drift and drain current (pp. 8-5–8-6).
  • K′n=μnCox and Kn=K′nW/L (p. 8-6).
B4. Triode and saturation operation
  • Small VDS yields an approximately resistive channel (p. 8-6).
  • Increasing VDS reduces the local gate overdrive toward the drain (p. 8-7).
  • Pinch-off begins at VDS=VOV; current then follows the ideal saturation square law (pp. 8-7–8-8).

Overdrive: VOV=VGS−Vt.
