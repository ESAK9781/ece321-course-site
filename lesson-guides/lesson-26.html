RS1 and RS2 are in series from the source to ground, and Cby1 short-circuits only RS2 at signal frequencies. For DC the whole RS=RS1+RS2 sits in the source and holds the operating point steady. For the signal the source looks like RS1 alone, so the gain is −RD/(1/gm+RS1) instead of −gmRD.

The gain now depends on the ratio of two resistances rather than on gm alone, which is why it varies less from device to device.

    A follower's source follows the input, so the source can swing both ways only if it rests halfway up the supply. Splitting the 24 V evenly puts VS = VRS = 12 V and leaves VDS = 12 V, the same headroom above and below the output.

Expansion note: how the five sketches fit together

Expansion note: why the load current is subtracted

Current leaves the source node three ways: into RS, into the load through C2, and nowhere else. The transistor supplies it all, so ID=IRS+IL and therefore IRS=ID−IL. Q. How measure Ro in a CD-amplifier ?

Measure the output voltage with no load (open circuit), voc. Then connect a known load RL (or a potentiometer) and measure vL. Then Ro=RL(vocvL−1); with a potentiometer, adjust it until the output falls to half of voc and read its resistance, which equals Ro.†

Given the following biasing circuit for an NMOS transistor with Vt = 1V, Kn = 5.813mA/V², λ = 0V⁻¹

Find the DC operating point and confirm the operating mode

  1. Step 1

    Assume saturation then solve for VG

    VG = 5V1MΩ306kΩ+1MΩ = 3.828 V

    Expansion note: why VG can be found first

    No current flows into the gate, so the two gate resistors form an unloaded voltage divider and VG is fixed before anything is known about the transistor. The exact quotient is 3.8285 V; the printed 3.828 V is rounded, and the unrounded value is what makes the quadratic below come out to exactly 6 and 8.

  2. Step 2

    VGS = VG − VS = 3.828 − (1 kΩ)ID

  3. Step 3

    Substitute into the saturation drain current equation

    ID = ½ Kn ( VGS − Vt )²

    ID = ½ 5.813 ( 3.828 − ID − 1 )²

    2ID5.813 = [ 2.828 − ID ]²

    Expansion note: why ID is written in mA here

    The source resistor is 1 kΩ, so with ID in mA the product (1kΩ)ID is already in volts. Kn = 5.813 mA/V² then keeps the equation free of powers of ten.

    The numbers on this page use ID = 2 mA; the question at the bottom of the page shows what the 4 mA root would give and why it is rejected.

  2. Step 5

    Solve for Vov and confirm saturation

    VGS = VG−VS = 3.828 V − 2 V = 1.828 V

Q. What about the 4mA solution ?

VD = 5 − 1 ID = 1 V        VS = 1 ID = 4 V

VDS = 1 V − 4 V = −3 V

VGS = 3.828 − 4 = −0.172 V

VGS = −0.172 V is below Vt = 1 V, so a transistor with these voltages would be off, which contradicts a 4 mA current. The 4 mA root is false; the answer is 2 mA.†

Expansion note: why a quadratic gives a root that is not a real solution

Squaring (VGS−Vt) throws away its sign. The saturation equation is only valid when VGS>Vt, but the square is positive whether VGS−Vt is positive or negative, so the algebra also accepts a root where VGS−Vt is negative. That root satisfies the equation yet violates the condition the equation assumed, so it must be checked and discarded.

ex. 4

Given the CD-amplifier

(a) Create the small-signal model. Include ro. Assume a DC operating point current of 20mA. Use the π† -model

Vov=2ID/Kn=2(20mA)/(100mA/V²) = 0.632 V

gm=KnVov = (100 mA/V²)(0.632 V) = 63.2 mA/V,   ro=VA/ID = 100 V/20 mA = 5 kΩ

Expansion note: how the small-signal model is built from the circuit

For small signals, every DC source and every large capacitor is replaced by its ac equivalent: VDD becomes a ground (the drain sits at ac ground), C1 and C2 become shorts, and the gate divider appears as RG=RG1∥RG2 between the source and the gate. The transistor becomes a controlled current source gmvgs from drain to source in parallel with ro. The load RLoad and RS are both connected from the source node to ground, so the output vo is across their parallel combination.

Expansion note: π-model or T-model

For a MOSFET the hybrid-π model (the gate open, a current source gmvgs between drain and source) and the T-model (a resistor 1/gm from source to the gate-side node, with the current source gmvgs drawn differently) describe the same transistor and give the same answers. The π form is used here because it is the form drawn on p. 26-15.

26-10

(b) Re-draw the circuit so you can analytically find Ro (amplifier output resistance)

With vsig = 0 and RLoad removed, vgs=−vx and the controlled source draws gmvx from the output, which is a resistor 1/gm. Then

Ro=vxix=1gm∥ro∥RS = 15.81 Ω ∥ 5 kΩ ∥ RS = 15.76 Ω ∥ RS

Expansion note: why the signal source is shorted and the load removed

The output resistance of the amplifier is what the load sees looking back into the output with the input signal turned off. Setting vsig to zero turns off the independent source and the load is not part of the amplifier, so it is removed. A test source vx is then connected to the output and the resistance is the ratio vx/ix. RS is not given in this problem, so the result is left in terms of it.

ex. 5

BJT Comparisons with FET

  1. a. The base is analogous to the FET’s gate†
  2. b. BJT current flow is [ Diffusion†, Drift ]
  3. c. T F† : A FET uses both majority and minority carriers while a BJT only uses majority carriers
  4. d. [ α β† ] is big while [ α† β ] is < 1†
  5. e. T† F BJT base current must flow for collector current to flow
  6. f. The BJT emitter† is the heaviest doped
Expansion note: why c is false

The roles are the other way round. A FET's current is carried by only one type of carrier in its channel (electrons in an n-channel device), so it is called unipolar and uses majority carriers only. A BJT is bipolar: the emitter injects carriers that are minority carriers in the base and cross it by diffusion, so it relies on both kinds.

Expansion note: why β is big and α is below 1

β=IC/IB is the large common-emitter current gain (typically 50 to 300). α=IC/IE is the common-base gain; since IE=IC+IB is always a little larger than IC, α is just under 1.

ex. 7

Sketch the Voltage Transfer Curve (VTC) From the last problem

Expansion note: how to read the curve

For vGS below Vt = 2 V the transistor is off, no current flows through RD and vDS sits at the 8 V supply. At A (vGS=Vt) it starts to conduct and vDS falls in the saturation region along VDD−12KnRD(vGS−Vt)2. At B the drain voltage has fallen to the overdrive voltage, the edge of saturation, and beyond B the transistor is in triode. An amplifier is biased between A and B, where the curve is steep and nearly straight; the Q point of Example 6 (2.403 V, 3.93 V) is between them.

VDS,B = 1+2KnRDVDD−1KnRD = 0.546 V

= 1+2(0.1A/V²)(500Ω)(8V)−1(0.1A/V²)(500Ω) = (28.30 − 1)/50

→ VGS,B = VDS,B+Vt = 0.546 V + 2 V = 2.546 V

Expansion note: where the formula for VDS,B comes from

At the edge of saturation vDS=vGS−Vt, and the drain current through RD obeys VDD−vDS=RD·12KnvDS2. Writing this as 12KnRDvDS2+vDS−VDD=0 and taking the positive root gives the formula; Kn is entered in A/V² (0.1) so the product KnRD is dimensionless.

Q. What are the disadvantages of this circuit ?

With no source resistor the operating point depends strongly on Kn and Vt (the bias current and the gain change a lot from one device to the next), the gain is not set by resistors, and the signal must stay small for the square-law device to be linear.†

Q. What is gained with a fully-bypassed RS ?

RS sets the DC current by negative feedback, so the Q point is stable against Kn and Vt, while the bypass capacitor shorts RS at signal frequencies and so the full gain −gmRD returns.†

Q. What is gained by a partially-bypassed RS ?

A stable Q point as before, plus a gain set mostly by resistors (−RD/(1/gm+RS1)) so it varies much less with Kn, and a smaller vgs for a given input so the amplifier is more linear, at the price of lower gain.†

Q. How can we increase the gain magnitude to 10 ?

The gain is |Avo|≈gmRD=2VRD/Vov, and here Vov=0.8VD−1 and VRD=5−VD. Setting 2(5−VD)/(0.8VD−1)=10 gives VD = 2.0 V, so Vov = 0.6 V and ID=12(10)(0.6)2 = 1.8 mA. The required drain resistor is RD=(5−2)/1.8mA = 1.67 kΩ, and then gm = 6 mA/V and |Avo| = 10.0 (exact −9.98). A larger Kn (a wider device) would also raise gm at the same bias.†

Expansion note: why a larger RD raises the gain here but only to a limit

A larger RD drops more of the supply across itself, so the drain voltage, and with it the gate voltage 0.8VD, fall. That lowers Vov and gm, but not as fast as RD grows, so gmRD rises. The cost is headroom: at VD = 2 V and Vov = 0.6 V the drain can fall only 1.4 V before the transistor leaves saturation, so the output swing shrinks as the gain goes up.

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Designing a common-source amplifier with a partially bypassed source resistor

Recognize it

You are given a device (Kn, Vt), a supply VDD, a target gain magnitude |Avo|, an input-resistance window and the input amplitude, and asked for RD, RS1, RS2, RG1 and RG2.

Method

  1. Split the supply: VRD=VDS=VRS=VDD/3.
  2. Choose RS1=1/gm, so |Avo|≈gmRD/2.
  3. Drain current from the gain: ID=2Kn[VDD/(6|Avo|)]2.
  4. RD=RS=(VDD/3)/ID.
  5. Vov=2ID/Kn, gm=KnVov, RS1=1/gm, RS2=RS−RS1.
  6. VGS=Vt+Vov, VG=VGS+VRS.
  7. Divider: RG1≥(VDD/VG)Rin, RG2=RG1/(VDD/VG−1).
  8. Swing: vd,pk=|Avo|vg,pk, VD,max=VD+vd,pk and VD,min=VD−vd,pk.
  9. Linearity: vgs,pk=vg,pk/(1+gmRS1)<0.2Vov.

Checks

  • VD,min stays above VS+Vov (saturation at the bottom of the swing).
  • VD,max stays below VDD.
  • The built divider's RG1∥RG2 is inside the input-resistance window.

Variants

  • Fully bypassed RS: no RS1, |Avo|=gmRD, and ID=2Kn[VDD/(3|Avo|)]2.
  • Larger RS1: gain falls and linearity improves.
  • Standard-value rounding of RD, RS or the divider shifts the bias a little; recompute VGS and the swing.

Common mistakes

  • Using the unbypassed RS formula for the gain, or forgetting that the bypass makes RS2 vanish only for the signal.
  • Using vg,pk instead of vgs,pk in the linearity test.
  • Forgetting the exponent in ID=2Kn[…]2.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1, pp. 26-1 to 26-2 (values reconstructed †).

A2. Designing a common-drain amplifier to drive a load, and finding its output resistance

Recognize it

You must deliver a given peak-to-peak output into a load with a given input-resistance window; a source follower is the stage that does it.

Method

  1. Centre the source: VS=VRS=VDD/2.
  2. Peak load current: IL,pk=Vo,pk/RL.
  3. Worst case at the negative output peak: IRS,worst=IL,pk+ID,min at VRS,worst=VS−Vo,pk.
  4. RS=VRS,worst/IRS,worst, round to a standard value, then ID=VS/RS.
  5. Vov=2ID/Kn, VGS=Vt+Vov, VG=VS+VGS.
  6. Divider as in A1.
  7. Output resistance: Ro=(1/gm)∥RS (also ∥ro if λ is given).

Checks

  • ID at the negative peak, (VS−Vo,pk)/RS−IL,pk, is positive.
  • At the positive peak VDS is still larger than Vov.

Variants

  • A different RL rescales IL,pk and so RS.
  • When RL is large, IL,pk is small and ID,min dominates the choice of RS.

Common mistakes

  • Adding the load current to ID with the wrong sign: IRS=ID−IL with IL negative at the negative peak.
  • Using peak-to-peak where a peak value is needed.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 2, pp. 26-3 to 26-6, with the Multisim check.

A3. Finding a MOSFET's DC operating point when the source has a resistor

Recognize it

A circuit with a gate divider, RD and RS and a request for ID, VDS and the operating mode.

Method

  1. Assume saturation and find VG from the divider (no gate current).
  2. Write VS=RSID and VGS=VG−RSID.
  3. Substitute into ID=12Kn(VGS−Vt)2 and expand into ID2−bID+c=0.
  4. Solve the quadratic; keep the root with VGS>Vt.
  5. VD=VDD−RDID, VDS=VD−VS.
  6. Confirm saturation: VDS>Vov=VGS−Vt.

Checks

  • The kept root gives VGS>Vt and VDS>Vov.
  • Substituting it back reproduces ID.

Variants

  • If both roots fail, the saturation assumption was wrong: redo the analysis in triode.
  • Including λ adds a factor (1+λVDS) and makes the equation implicit.

Common mistakes

  • Keeping the larger root without checking it.
  • Mixing mA and A so the coefficients are off by 1000.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 3, pp. 26-7 to 26-8.

A4. Building a small-signal model and finding output resistance

Recognize it

"Create the small-signal model" or "find the amplifier output resistance" for a given circuit.

Method

  1. Find the DC current ID, then Vov=2ID/Kn, gm=KnVov=2ID/Vov and ro=VA/ID=1/(λID).
  2. Replace DC supplies by ground, large capacitors by shorts, and the transistor by gmvgs from drain to source with ro in parallel.
  3. For Ro: set the independent source to zero, remove the load, attach a test source vx and compute vx/ix.

Checks

  • The dependent source points from drain to source for an NMOS.
  • For a follower Ro is close to 1/gm.

Variants

  • T-model: same results, with 1/gm shown as a resistor.
  • With a load connected, the gain is gm(RS∥RL∥ro)/(1+gm(RS∥RL∥ro)).

Common mistakes

  • Leaving the load in when finding Ro.
  • Forgetting that VDD becomes ac ground.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 4, pp. 26-9 to 26-10 and the Ro estimate in Ex. 2.

A5. BJT and FET conceptual comparisons

Recognize it

True/false and fill-in questions about carriers, terminals, gains and doping.

Method

  1. Match terminals: base ↔ gate, collector ↔ drain, emitter ↔ source.
  2. BJT current is carried by diffusion of injected minority carriers across the base; the FET channel conducts by drift of majority carriers.
  3. BJT: IC=αIE=βIB with β ≫ 1 and α just below 1; no base current means no collector current.
  4. FET: no gate current at DC; the emitter is the most heavily doped BJT region.

Checks

  • β=α/(1−α) for consistency between the two gains.

Variants

  • PNP and PMOS devices swap polarities and carrier types.

Common mistakes

  • Swapping which device is unipolar.
  • Saying a FET draws base current.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 5, p. 26-10 (answers †).

A6. Operating point, gain, swing and linearity of a CS amplifier with no source resistor

Recognize it

A gate divider, RD, a grounded source and a request for ID, VD, Avo, the drain swing and a linearity check.

Method

  1. VG=VDDRG2/(RG1+RG2) and VGS=VG.
  2. ID=12Kn(VGS−Vt)2, VD=VDD−RDID; confirm VD>Vov.
  3. gm=KnVov, Avo=−gmRD.
  4. vds,pk=|Avo|vgs,pk.
  5. Linearity: vgs,pk≤0.2Vov.
  6. To redesign for more gain, lower VDS, set Vov=2(VDD−VDS)/|Avo| and RD=(VDD−VDS)/ID.

Checks

  • The lowest drain voltage VD−vds,pk stays above Vov.

Variants

  • A load RL replaces RD by RD∥RL in the signal gain.
  • With λ, replace RD by RD∥ro.

Common mistakes

  • Using a source resistor in the gain for a circuit that has none.
  • Skipping the linearity test.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 6 and Ex. 8, pp. 26-11 to 26-13 (Ex. 8 targets †).

A7. Sketching the voltage transfer curve and finding point B

Recognize it

"Sketch the VTC" or "find the end of the saturation region" for a common-source stage.

Method

  1. Below Vt the output sits at VDD (point A is at vGS=Vt).
  2. In saturation, vDS=VDD−12KnRD(vGS−Vt)2.
  3. Point B: VDS,B=(1+2KnRDVDD−1)/(KnRD) and VGS,B=VDS,B+Vt.
  4. Beyond B the device is in triode and vDS falls slowly toward 0.

Checks

  • The Q point lies between A and B.
  • The slope at the Q point is the small-signal gain.

Variants

  • With λ the saturation curve acquires a slight slope.

Common mistakes

  • Using Kn in mA/V² inside the square root without converting units.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 7, p. 26-12.

A8. Biasing and gain with the gate fed back from the drain

Recognize it

RG1 connects the drain to the gate and RG2 takes the gate to ground; the problem says the current in RG1 is negligible.

Method

  1. Express VD=VDD−RDID and VG=VDRG2/(RG1+RG2).
  2. Substitute into ID=12Kn(VG−Vt)2 and solve the resulting quadratic.
  3. Reject a root that puts VD below ground or VGS below Vt.
  4. VG=VGS because the source is grounded; check VD>Vov.
  5. Small-signal nodal equation at the drain, with vgs=vin: voRD+gmvgs+vo−vgsRG1=0⇒vovin=1/RG1−gm1/RD+1/RG1≈−gmRD

Checks

  • IRD≫IRG1 holds: IRG1=(VD−VG)/RG1 = 0.5 µA against 5 mA.

Variants

  • Different RD changes both the bias and the gain; see the question on p. 26-16.

Common mistakes

  • Treating VG as a divider off VDD.
  • Keeping the root with VD<0.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 9, pp. 26-14 to 26-16.

A9. Choosing between amplifier configurations and measuring R_o

Recognize it

"What are the disadvantages of this circuit?", "What is gained with a bypassed RS?" and "How measure Ro?"

Method

  1. No RS: highest gain, but the Q point moves a lot with Kn and Vt.
  2. Fully bypassed RS: stable Q point and full gain −gmRD, but the gain still follows gm.
  3. Partially bypassed RS: stable Q point, gain set mostly by resistors, better linearity, lower gain.
  4. Common drain: gain just below 1 and small Ro; used to drive a low-resistance load.
  5. Measuring Ro: compare the open-circuit and loaded outputs, Ro=RL(voc/vL−1).

Checks

  • The loaded gain is Av=AvoRL/(RL+Ro).

Variants

  • Two stages in cascade: use each stage's Ri, Ro and Avo.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring Ro with the signal source and load both connected.

Worked in these notes: Q's on pp. 26-4, 26-13 and 26-16 (answers †).
