11-1

The Intrinsic Body Diode

(*) Recall the NMOS structure.

With the body doped p and drain doped n+, we see a natural diode appear. When body is attached to source, we often augment our device symbol to reflect its presence.

It can be useful in power electronics to realize a bi-directional current capability.

(*) It?s important that we understand what we are viewing when we see a family of curves for a MOSFET. For saturation, with λ=0, we have ID=12knVOV2.

Expansion note: where the intrinsic diode comes from

The p-type body and n-type drain form a PN junction. In the usual NMOS connection the body is tied to the source, so that junction appears as the body diode between body/source and drain. The symbol adds that diode to the MOSFET representation; it is a physical junction, not an extra discrete component.

For triode, the curve becomes ID=kn[(VGS−Vtn)VDS−12VDS2].

  • We improve the curves in saturation by providing a non-zero slope: λ→1/ro, so ro≈1/slope≈VA/ID,sat.
  • We design external circuitry to create a load line similar to what we see with the diode. The intersection with our family of curves establishes the operating point, or quiescent point. We will see next chapter what makes for a good Q-point.
Expansion note: why a load line selects an operating point

The transistor curves show the drain current allowed by the device for each pair of gate and drain voltages. The external circuit imposes another relation between drain current and drain voltage. Their intersection must satisfy both relations at once and is the circuit?s operating (quiescent) point.

Q. How can we drive into triode? Increase RD or VG.

Expansion note: check the operating-region inequality

For an NMOS to remain in saturation, VDS≥VGS−Vt. If the drain resistor is increased, the drain voltage falls for a given current; if the gate voltage is increased, the overdrive VGS−Vt grows. Either change can make the saturation inequality fail and move the operating point into triode, depending on the circuit?s resulting current.

Appendix A: Problem-type guides

Identify the body diode and terminal connections

Locate the p-type body and n-type drain regions, identify the junction, then check whether the body is tied to source. Read the diode orientation from the junction polarity. In power applications, account for that junction when interpreting reverse current or bidirectional switching.

Read an NMOS output-characteristic family

Identify the axes (ID and VDS), then select the curve matching VGS−Vt. Locate the triode-to-saturation knee. A nonzero saturation slope indicates channel-length modulation; estimate ro from the reciprocal slope at the chosen operating current.

Solve and verify a self-biased NMOS operating point

Find the gate divider voltage, express VD, VS, and VGS as functions of ID, and substitute into the assumed-region current equation using consistent units. Solve for candidate roots; discard any that violate cutoff/turn-on assumptions. Compute VDS and check VDS≥VGS−Vt before accepting saturation.

Use a curve tracer to measure device characteristics

Wire the device with the correct gate, drain, source, and bulk connections. For transfer curves, hold VDS fixed and sweep VGS. For output families, step VGS while sweeping VDS. Save the resulting table and graph, export the data, and choose saturated points when estimating channel-length modulation.

Estimate Vt, kn, λ, and ro

From the transfer characteristic, identify the turn-on threshold and fit the saturation-region relation to estimate kn. From the output curve, fit the nearly linear saturation segment: slope gives 1/ro, and extrapolated voltage-axis intercept estimates −VA=−1/λ. Repeat for three curves and average the individual λ estimates as the notetaker directs.
