Lesson 3: PN-Junction Electrostatics and Diode Current

3-1

Recap of PN-Junction

V= V0

(*) We call this a barrier voltage or the junction built-in voltage and denote it by V0.

Expansion note: why the depletion region contains fixed charge

Electrons diffuse from the n side into the p side, while holes diffuse in the opposite direction. Recombination near the boundary leaves immobile ionized donors on the n side and ionized acceptors on the p side. Their electric field opposes further diffusion and creates the equilibrium built-in potential.

3-2

(*) A derivation gives

V0=VTln(NANDni2).

voltage = 0.697V† = 697† mV

• For silicon, we find VT<V0< 1.12† V.

• We also find that the thermal voltage relates the diffusion constant and mobility through the relationship

VT=Dnμn=Dpμp.

Expansion note: how the numerical built-in voltage is obtained

At 300 K, VT=kT/q≈25.85mV. With NA=1017cm^{-3}, ND=5×1014cm^{-3}, and the common room-temperature approximation ni=1010cm^{-3}, the doping product divided by ni2 is 5×1011. Thus V0=(25.85mV)ln(5×1011)≈0.6964V. The packet does not state temperature or ni numerically, so the dagger marks those assumptions.

The relation between VT, diffusion coefficient, and mobility is the Einstein relation. Its full thermal definition is VT=kT/q.

Width of Depletion Region: NA>ND

Expansion note: why depletion width is unequal on the two sides

Charge neutrality requires the total fixed charge on both sides to have equal magnitude. Since NA>ND, fewer donor charges occupy a given volume on the n side; the depletion region therefore extends farther into that more lightly doped side.

Expansion note: how charge neutrality sets each depletion width

From NAxp=NDxn and W=xp+xn, substitute xp=(ND/NA)xn into the width equation. This gives xn=WNA/(NA+ND) and xp=WND/(NA+ND). The lower-doped n side takes most of the width when NA≫ND.

Expansion note: where semiconductor permittivity appears

In a parallel-plate approximation, capacitance is C=εA/d. A junction depletion region also separates charge over a width, so its electrostatic behavior depends on the semiconductor permittivity εs.

PN-Junction with Applied Voltage

Reverse Bias

VR adds to V0 → increasing the barrier dramatically

so Iext is then dominated by thermally generated carriers

which will be really small

(*) The increase in barrier voltage means more charge, which in turn requires a bigger

W=xn+xp=2εsq(1NA+1ND)(V0+VR).

Expansion note: why reverse bias widens the depletion region

Reverse polarity raises the junction barrier from V0 to approximately V0+VR. A larger potential drop must be supported by the uncovered ionized dopants, so the depletion width grows according to the square-root relation.

Forward Bias

VF subtracts from V0 → reduced depletion region and barrier

More carriers can diffuse across the junction, so diffusion current dominates and is many orders bigger than reverse current and Iext= forward current.

Q. Recall how diffusion works

Expansion note: connect bias polarity to the I-V curve

Forward bias lowers the barrier and injects carriers across the junction, producing rapidly rising positive diode current. Reverse bias raises the barrier, leaving only a small minority-carrier current until breakdown. The current reference follows conventional current, not electron motion.

3-6

(*) Let's consider holes injected into the n-region. Key result from device physics is the concentration of holes at the depletion-region edge is

pn(xn)=pn0eV/VT.

[which increases as V increases]

the excess concentration becomes

=pn0(eV/VT−1).

(*) This “excess” rolls off as a function of the distance from the depletion edge

pn(x)=pn0+pn0(eV/VT−1)e−(x−xn)/Lp,x≥xn.

The smaller Lp, the faster the rolloff

Expansion note: read the forward-bias minority-carrier profile

At the depletion edge, forward bias raises the minority-hole concentration above its equilibrium value by the factor eV/VT. Farther into the n region the injected excess decays exponentially over the diffusion length Lp, returning to pn0. A smaller Lp makes that return happen over a shorter distance.

(*) We can now find the hole-diffusion current density using what we introduced last time

Jp(x)=−qDpdpn(x)dx.

we love derivatives!

Jp(x)=−qDp[−pn0Lp(eV/VT−1)e−(x−xn)/Lp].

(*) The current density is largest at the depletion-region edge

Jp(xn)=qDpLppn0(eV/VT−1).

Note, Jp decreases because of recombination.

But this recombination requires current to replace the carriers it removes.

Result: As Jp(x) decreases, electron-current component increases by the same amount.

Similarly

Jn(−xp)=qDnLnnp0(eV/VT−1).

(*) Finally, we get the external current by

I=A[Jp(xn)+Jn(−xp)].

Expansion note: why total current stays constant through the junction

As holes recombine while diffusing into the n region, the hole-current component falls. Electron current rises by the same amount, so their sum remains constant. At the depletion edges, the two diffusion components can be added to obtain the terminal current.

The factor Aq(Dp/(LpND)+Dn/(LnNA))ni2 is the saturation-current scale IS. Substituting the equilibrium minority concentrations into the diffusion-current sum turns it into the Shockley form. For reverse voltage well below breakdown, the exponential term approaches zero and iD≈−IS. Forward current grows exponentially once vD is several thermal voltages positive.

3-9

Reverse Breakdown

(*) When sufficiently large of a reverse voltage is applied we see breakdown

this is termed reverse breakdown and is potentially destructive, but this current MUST BE limited

Zener effect (VBR< 5† V): electric field in depletion region becomes strong enough to point of breaking covalent bonds, thus generating pairs.

Avalanche effect (VBR> 5† V): increase in electric field gives carriers sufficient energy to break covalent bonds through impact ionization.

Expansion note: distinguish Zener and avalanche breakdown

Zener breakdown is associated with a strong junction field that enables carriers to tunnel across the depletion-region barrier. Avalanche breakdown occurs when accelerated carriers create additional electron-hole pairs by impact ionization. The approximate 5 V division is a teaching rule of thumb; real device mechanisms may overlap. A series element must limit current so the diode stays within its rated dissipation.

Appendix

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Find the equilibrium built-in voltage

Recognize it: A silicon pn junction is described by acceptor and donor concentrations and intrinsic concentration.

Method: 1. Set temperature and obtain VT=kT/q. 2. Calculate V0=VTln(NAND/ni2). 3. Check that the result is positive and is larger than VT for ordinary silicon doping.

Checks and mistakes: Keep concentrations in matching units inside the ratio; do not take the logarithm of a dimensional quantity. Do not confuse V0 with an applied diode voltage. Worked on 3-1–3-2.

A2. Determine depletion width and which side it occupies

Recognize it: Given NA, ND, and junction potential, find total width or its p- and n-side portions.

Method: 1. Use charge neutrality NAxp=NDxn. 2. Use W=xp+xn=(2εs/q)(1/NA+1/ND)V0. 3. Split the width: xn=WNA/(NA+ND) and xp=WND/(NA+ND). 4. Verify the lower-doped side has the greater depletion distance.

Checks and mistakes: Convert units consistently; for SI, use concentrations per m³ and permittivity in F/m. Worked on 3-2–3-3.

A3. Predict how applied bias changes the junction

Recognize it: Determine the effect of a stated forward or reverse polarity.

Method: Forward bias subtracts from the built-in barrier and narrows depletion width; reverse bias adds to the barrier and widens it. For reverse bias, substitute V0+VR into the depletion-width relation.

Checks and mistakes: Identify the p- and n-side source polarities before calling a bias forward or reverse. Wider depletion width corresponds to more separated fixed charge. Worked on 3-4–3-5.

A4. Relate minority-carrier profile to diffusion current

Recognize it: A forward-biased junction has excess minority carriers at its depletion edges that decay into the neutral regions.

Method: 1. Use the edge relation pn(xn)=pn0eV/VT and analogous electron relation. 2. Subtract equilibrium concentration to find excess. 3. Apply the exponential profile with diffusion length. 4. Use J=−qDdc/dx for holes and the corresponding electron-current expression. 5. Sum edge-current densities and multiply by area.

Checks and mistakes: The gradient direction and carrier charge both affect current sign. Hole excess decays over Lp and electron excess over Ln. Worked on 3-5–3-8.

A5. Use the Shockley relation and recognize breakdown

Recognize it: Relate diode terminal current to voltage, or identify the high-reverse-voltage region.

Method: Use iD=IS(evD/VT−1) within the ordinary operating region. For reverse breakdown, identify the Zener or avalanche mechanism and include current limiting in the circuit.

Checks and mistakes: The Shockley equation does not model destructive breakdown; never treat breakdown current as inherently limited. The approximate 5 V mechanism boundary is not exact. Worked on 3-8–3-9.

C. Equations Used Throughout

C1. Built-in voltage and thermal voltage

Thermal voltage: VT=kT/q=Dn/μn=Dp/μp.

Built-in voltage: V0=VTln(NAND/ni2).

C2. Depletion width and charge balance

Charge neutrality: NAxp=NDxn.

Equilibrium width: W=xp+xn=(2εs/q)(1/NA+1/ND)V0.

Reverse-biased width: W=(2εs/q)(1/NA+1/ND)(V0+VR).

Edge widths: xn=WNA/(NA+ND); xp=WND/(NA+ND).

C3. Minority carriers and diffusion current

Hole equilibrium concentration: pn0=ni2/ND; electron equilibrium concentration: np0=ni2/NA.

Forward-bias hole concentration at edge: pn(xn)=pn0eV/VT.

Hole profile: pn(x)=pn0+pn0(eV/VT−1)e−(x−xn)/Lp.

Hole diffusion current density: Jp=−qDpdpn/dx; at xn, Jp=q(Dp/Lp)pn0(eV/VT−1).

Terminal current: I=A[Jp(xn)+Jn(−xp)].

C4. Shockley diode relation

IS=Aq(Dp/(LpND)+Dn/(LnNA))ni2.

iD=IS(evD/VT−1).
