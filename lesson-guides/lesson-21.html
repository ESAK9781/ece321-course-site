Lesson 21: Common-Drain Design, Common-Source Analysis and Lab 3

21-1

Ex. 1

Design a CD-amplifier with an n-channel enhancement MOS with Kn = 70† mA/V² and Vt = 2 V†

We want to drive RL = 80 Ω with a 4† Vpp output while satisfying Rin = ≥ 100 kΩ†

Choose VDD = 20 V

Expansion note: why this is a common-drain (source-follower) amplifier

The drain connects straight to VDD and the output is taken from the source through C2. A grounded (ac) drain makes it common-drain. Its voltage gain is just below 1, but its output resistance is small, which is what lets it drive an 80 Ω load.

Expansion note: why driving an 80 Ω load is the hard part

A 4 Vpp swing is easy to produce; delivering it into 80 Ω is not. Ohm's law says the load needs ±25 mA at the peaks, and that current has to come from the transistor and RS. That is why the steps below start from the load current, not from the gain, and why the design ends up biased at a large 37 mA.

  1. Step 1

    Assume Vin,pp≈ 5 V to account for |Av,CD| < 1 (about 0.8)

    Expansion note: why the input must be larger than the output

    A follower loses a little signal, so to get 4 Vpp out you need more than 4 Vpp in:

    Vin,pp≈Vo,pp|Av|=4V0.8=5V

    The simulations on p. 21-3 show it: 4.5 Vpp in gives only 3.68 Vpp out, while 5 Vpp in gives 4.06 Vpp.

  2. Step 2

    Pick VS ≈ ½ VDD → VS = 10 V

    Expansion note: why the source sits at half the supply

    The output can swing up until the MOSFET runs out of VDS and down until it cuts off. Putting the source halfway up the supply leaves about equal room in both directions.

  3. Step 3

    Find the peak load current : IL,pk = Vo,pkRL = 2V80Ω = 25 mA

    Expansion note: why the peak, not the peak-to-peak value

    A 4 Vpp output swings 2 V above and 2 V below its average, so the largest voltage the load ever sees is the 2 V peak. The coupling capacitor passes this ac current, so the load needs 25 mA at each peak.

  4. Step 4

    Choose ID,min = 5 mA† to avoid cutoff

    Expansion note: why the drain current is lowest at the negative peak

    At the negative peak, vo = −2 V, so the load pushes 25 mA back into the source node through C2. KCL at the source gives

    IRS=ID+IL,pk⇒IRS,worst=IL,pk+ID,min

    RS still needs its current, so the MOSFET supplies 25 mA less than RS carries. If that dropped to zero the device would cut off and the output would clip. Keeping a few milliamps flowing prevents that.

  5. Step 5

    Find IRS,worst = 25 mA + 5 mA = 30 mA

  1. Step 6

    Find VRS,worst = VS−Vo,pk = 10 V − 2 V = 8 V

  2. Step 7

    Calculate RS=VRS,worstIRS,worst = 8V30mA = 266.7 Ω → Pick RS = 270 Ω

    Expansion note: does rounding up to 270 Ω break the design?

    Only slightly. With 270 Ω, the worst-case resistor current is 8V/270Ω=29.6mA, so ID,min=29.6−25=4.6mA instead of 5 mA. That still keeps the device well on. 270 Ω is the nearest standard value.

  3. Step 8

    Find DC (quiescent) drain current

    ID = VSRS = 10V270Ω = 37.04 mA

    Expansion note: why all of ID flows through RS

    With no signal, C2 is an open circuit to DC, so no DC current reaches the load. Every milliamp the MOSFET conducts goes down through RS, which is why ID=VS/RS.

  4. Step 9

    Find the required over-drive voltage

    VOV = 2IDKn = 2(37.04mA)70mA/V² = 1.029 V

    Expansion note: where this formula comes from

    It is the saturation equation ID=12KnVOV2 solved for VOV: how far above threshold the gate must sit for the device to carry 37 mA.

  5. Step 10

    Find VG

    VGS = Vt + VOV = 2 V + 1.029 V = 3.029 V

    VG = VGS + VS = 3.029 V + 10 V = 13.03 V

  6. Step 11

    Find RG1 and RG2

    RG1>VDDVGRIN = 2013.03(100kΩ) = 153.5 kΩ → 160 kΩ

    RG2=RG1VDDVG−1 = 160kΩ1.535−1 = 299.0 kΩ → 300 kΩ

    Expansion note: where the RG1 inequality comes from

    The divider sets RG2RG1+RG2=VGVDD. The input resistance is the parallel combination, so

    Rin=RG1RG2RG1+RG2=RG1VGVDD

    Requiring Rin to be at least the spec gives RG1≥(VDD/VG)Rin. Solving the divider ratio for RG2 gives the second formula. The built divider gives Rin=160kΩ∥300kΩ=104.3kΩ.

Expansion note: what the 2.417 % THD means

THD compares the energy at the harmonics (2 kHz, 3 kHz, …) to the energy at the 1 kHz fundamental. It is not zero because the square law makes gm change as the drain current swings between about 5 and 71 mA, so the gain at the top of the waveform differs from the gain at the bottom. A larger swing or a smaller ID,min raises it. Lab 3 sets a limit on it (p. 21-7).

    Expansion note: why the divider ignores the MOSFET, and 4.70 V or 4.702 V?

    No current flows into the gate, so the divider is unloaded, and the 50 Ω source and C1 do not affect DC. The exact value is 4.7022 V; rounding to 4.7 V is what makes the constant on p. 21-6 come out to exactly 10.24. Expansion note: where 6.413 comes from, and which root is real

Moving the left-hand ID across turns 480 into 481; dividing everything by 75 gives the printed form:

75ID2−481ID+768=0⇒ID2−6.413ID+10.24=0

Check VGS=4.7−ID for each root. For 3.413 mA, VGS = 1.287 V, below Vt = 1.5 V, so the device would be off; that root is false. For 3.000 mA, VGS = 1.700 V. Keep ID = 3.000 mA.

  1. Step 5

    Find VOV

    ID=12KnVOV2→VOV = 2IDKn = 2(3mA)150mA/V² = 0.2 V

  2. Step 6

    Find VD and VS

    VD=VDD − RDID = 15 − (2 kΩ)(3 mA) = 9 V

    VS=RSID = (1 kΩ)(3 mA) = 3 V

    VDS = 9 V − 3 V = 6 V

  3. Step 7

    Check mode VDS > VOV

    Expansion note: why compare VDS with VOV

    Saturation needs the drain at least VOV above the source; otherwise the channel is not pinched off and the device is in triode, where the square law used in Step 3 does not hold. Here VDS = 6 V is far above VOV = 0.2 V, so the device is in saturation and the assumption holds.

  4. Step 8

    Find Av = −gm(RD∥ro) = −2IDVOV(RD∥1λID) = −(30mS)(2kΩ∥66.7kΩ) = −58.3

    Expansion note: why RS is missing and ro is included

    The 100 µF capacitor across RS is a short at 1 kHz, so the source is at ac ground and RS drops out of the gain. λ is given, so ro is finite and sits in parallel with RD:

    gm=2(3mA)0.2V=30mA/Vro=1(0.005)(3mA)=66.7kΩ

    Without ro the gain would be −60. This is the open-circuit gain Avo the problem asks for; with the 1 MΩ load connected, Av=−gm(RD∥ro∥RL)=−58.1.

Requirements :

[handout value]† ≤ Rin ≤ [handout value]†    [handout value]† < |Av| < [handout value]†    [handout value]† Vpp    [handout value]† distortion

Expansion note: what each requirement measures
  • Rin: the resistance the signal source sees. Too low and the source's own resistance eats part of the signal before the amplifier gets it.
  • |Av|: the loaded voltage gain, vo/vin, with RL connected. A band (a minimum and a maximum) rather than a single number allows for device variation.
  • Vpp: the largest output the amplifier can deliver to the load without clipping.
  • Distortion: how much the output's shape differs from a pure sine, measured as THD in percent.

Constraints : [handout value]† ≤ VDD ≤ [handout value]†

CS → ID < [handout value]†

CD → ID < [handout value]†

power within each transistor's and resistor's rating†

Desire :

  • Stable Q-point† and gain† indep. of device parameters (Kn, Vt)†
  • Adequate output swing† with low THDF < [handout value]†
Expansion note: what "stable and independent of device parameters" asks for

MOSFETs from the same bin vary widely in Kn and Vt. A design with a source resistor and a stiff gate divider (Ex. 1) keeps ID close to VS/RS whatever the device does, and the follower gain stays near 0.8.

(*) Cannot realize with just a single stage†

Expansion note: why one stage cannot meet the requirements

The two examples in this lesson show it. The Ex. 2 CS stage has Avo = −58.3 but Ro≈RD∥ro = 1.94 kΩ. Driving 80 Ω directly, its gain collapses:

Av=AvoRLRL+Ro=−58.3·8080+1942=−2.3

The Ex. 1 CD stage has Ro=RS∥1/gm = 13.2 Ω, which drives 80 Ω easily, but its gain is only 0.82. Cascading a CS stage for gain into a CD stage for drive gets both.

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Designing a common-drain stage to drive a load with a given swing

Recognize it

You are asked to choose RS, the bias point and usually the gate divider for a source follower. The givens are a load RL, an output swing Vo,pp, a supply VDD, the device (Kn, Vt) and often an input-resistance requirement.

Method

  1. Size the input you will need: Vin,pp≈Vo,pp/|Av|. Start from |Av|≈0.8 for a follower driving a small load.
  2. Bias the source mid-supply: VS≈12VDD.
  3. Peak load current: IL,pk=Vo,pk/RL, with Vo,pk=12Vo,pp.
  4. Pick a minimum drain current ID,min of a few mA so the device never cuts off.
  5. Worst case is the negative output peak, where the load pushes current back into the source node: IRS,worst=IL,pk+ID,min,VRS,worst=VS−Vo,pk
  6. RS=VRS,worst/IRS,worst, then pick a standard value (rounding down keeps ID,min at or above target).
  7. Quiescent current ID=VS/RS.
  8. VOV=2ID/Kn, VGS=Vt+VOV, VG=VS+VGS.
  9. Design the gate divider for VG and Rin (problem type A2).
  10. Check the gain with type A6 and the power: PD=(VDD−VS)ID, PRS=ID2RS.

Checks

  • VG<VDD with room for the positive input peak.
  • At the negative peak, ID=(VS−Vo,pk)/RS−IL,pk>0.
  • At the positive peak, VDS=VDD−VS−Vo,pk is still above VOV at that current.

Variants

  • Larger swing or smaller RL: IL,pk grows, RS must shrink and the bias current rises; recheck power.
  • RS rounds up past its computed value: ID,min drops slightly below target. Accept it if it stays well above zero, or round down.
  • Output clips on the negative peak: the device is cutting off. Raise ID,min (smaller RS).
  • Output clips on the positive peak: not enough VDS left. Lower VS or raise VDD.
  • Different device: only the VOV, VG and divider steps change.

Common mistakes

  • Using peak-to-peak instead of peak for IL,pk.
  • Forgetting that the load current adds to the RS current at the negative peak; sizing RS from ID alone gives far too little drive.
  • Expecting a gain of 1: with an 80 Ω load the follower gives about 0.8.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1, pp. 21-1 to 21-3.

A2. Designing a gate divider for a target VG and input resistance

Recognize it

You know the gate voltage the bias needs and a minimum Rin; you must choose RG1 (to VDD) and RG2 (to ground).

Method

  1. Use VG/VDD=RG2/(RG1+RG2) and Rin=RG1∥RG2. Together these give Rin=RG1VGVDD
  2. So RG1≥VDDVGRin; round up to a standard value.
  3. Then RG2=RG1VDD/VG−1; round to the nearest standard value.
  4. Recompute the built VG and Rin with the standard values.

Checks and mistakes

  • The built Rin must still meet the spec after rounding.
  • Rounding RG2 moves VG, and with it VS and ID; a few percent is normal (Ex. 1: 13.03 V designed, 13.04 V built).
  • No gate current flows, so the divider is unloaded; do not include the MOSFET in the divider calculation.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1, Step 11, p. 21-2.

A3. Finding the DC operating point of a MOSFET with a source resistor

Recognize it

The circuit and device are given, and you must find ID, VDS (or VD, VS). RS makes VGS depend on ID, so the square law becomes a quadratic.

Method

  1. Replace capacitors with opens; VG=VDDRG2/(RG1+RG2).
  2. Assume saturation. Write VGS=VG−IDRS (work in mA and kΩ so IDRS is in volts).
  3. Substitute: ID=12Kn(VG−IDRS−Vt)2.
  4. Expand to aID2+bID+c=0 and solve: ID=−b±b2−4ac2a
  5. Reject the root with VGS=VG−IDRS<Vt; it describes a device that is off.
  6. VOV=2ID/Kn (equal to VGS−Vt), VD=VDD−RDID, VS=RSID, VDS=VD−VS.
  7. Confirm the region (type A4).

Variants

  • Both roots give VGS<Vt: the device is off; ID = 0.
  • No RS: VGS=VG directly; no quadratic is needed.
  • Saturation check fails: redo with the triode equation (type A4).

Common mistakes

  • Dropping the "+1" when moving ID to the right-hand side (481, not 480, in Ex. 2).
  • Mixing units: with ID in mA, enter Kn in mA/V² and RS in kΩ.
  • Keeping both roots, or picking the larger one without checking VGS.
  • Including λ in the DC quadratic when the problem does not ask for it; the hand method ignores it.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 2, Steps 1 to 6, pp. 21-5, 21-6.

A4. Confirming the operating region

Recognize it

Any problem that says "assume … but confirm it", or any analysis that used the saturation equation.

Method

  1. Cutoff test: VGS>Vt (that is, VOV>0)? If not, ID=0.
  2. Saturation test: VDS≥VOV. If it holds, the square-law answer stands.
  3. If VDS<VOV, the device is in triode: redo the analysis with ID=Kn(VOVVDS−12VDS2).

Common mistakes

  • Comparing VDS with Vt or VGS instead of with VOV.
  • Skipping the check because the numbers "look fine".

Worked in these notes: Ex. 2, Step 7, p. 21-6.

A5. Small-signal gain of a common-source stage

Recognize it

After the operating point is known, find Avo or Av for a CS amplifier, often with RS bypassed and λ given.

Method

  1. Small-signal parameters at the Q-point: gm=2IDVOV,ro=1λID
  2. Short the coupling and bypass capacitors and the DC supplies.
  3. Open-circuit gain with RS bypassed: Avo=−gm(RD∥ro).
  4. With a load: Av=−gm(RD∥ro∥RL).
  5. From the signal source: multiply by Rin/(Rin+Rsig), with Rin=RG1∥RG2.
  6. Output resistance: Ro=RD∥ro.

Variants

  • RS not bypassed (ignoring ro): Avo=−gmRD1+gmRS.
  • λ not given: ro→∞, so Avo=−gmRD.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving RS in the gain when it is bypassed.
  • Dropping the minus sign; a CS stage inverts.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 2, Step 8, p. 21-6.

A6. Small-signal gain and output resistance of a common-drain stage

Recognize it

Checking a follower design, or finding how well a CD stage drives a load.

Method

  1. gm=2ID/VOV at the Q-point.
  2. Loaded gain: Av=gm(RS∥RL)1+gm(RS∥RL)
  3. Open-circuit gain: Avo=gmRS1+gmRS; output resistance Ro=RS∥1gm.
  4. Two-port check: Av=AvoRLRL+Ro gives the same answer.

Common mistakes

  • Using RS alone instead of RS∥RL when the load is connected.
  • Forgetting that a large gm(RS∥RL) is what pushes the gain toward 1; a light bias current gives a poor follower.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1 hand check, p. 21-3 (0.816 by hand, 0.817 simulated).

A7. Checking a design in simulation

Recognize it

The notes pair each hand result with a Multisim run. Lab work asks you to do the same.

Method

  1. DC operating point: compare node voltages and ID with the hand values. A few percent difference is expected from λ and model parameters.
  2. Transient gain: put one cursor on an output maximum and one on the next minimum; dy is Vo,pp. Then |Av|=Vo,pp/Vin,pp. Ignore dy/dx (a slope in V/s).
  3. Output swing: raise the input until the output meets the required Vpp, and confirm the waveform does not clip.
  4. Distortion: connect the distortion analyzer to the output, set the fundamental frequency, and read THD in percent.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing by the input's peak instead of its peak-to-peak value (Multisim sources are labeled in Vpk).
  • Reading cursors that are not on a peak and a trough.

Worked in these notes: pp. 21-3, 21-4 and 21-6.

A8. Choosing stages to meet a set of amplifier requirements

Recognize it

A spec lists input resistance, gain, output swing into a low-resistance load and distortion (Lab 3). You must decide what stages to use before designing any of them.

Method

  1. Model each candidate stage as a two-port: Rin, Avo, Ro.
  2. Check a single stage against the load: Av=AvoRL/(RL+Ro).
  3. A CS stage gives large |Avo| but large Ro≈RD∥ro, so its gain collapses into a small load. A CD stage has small Ro but Avo<1.
  4. Cascade: CS for gain into CD for drive. The overall gain is the product of each stage's loaded gain: Av=Avo1Rin2Rin2+Ro1·Avo2RLRL+Ro2
  5. Bias each stage so its Q-point does not depend strongly on Kn and Vt (source resistor, stiff divider), and check each ID and power against the constraints.

Worked in these notes: Lab 3 Introduction, p. 21-7 (numbers in the expansion note there).
