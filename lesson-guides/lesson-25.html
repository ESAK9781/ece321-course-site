Lesson 25: Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT) Operation, Biasing and Small-Signal Model

25-1

Just Enough Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT)

(*) For an npn BJT, we have three semiconductor regions

Expansion note: what the three regions are called and why the labels are n, p, n

The outer regions are the emitter (left) and the collector (right); the thin middle region is the base. In an npn device the emitter and collector are n-type (free electrons are the majority carriers) and the base is p-type (free holes are the majority carriers). A pnp device swaps every type, and every voltage and current direction, but works the same way.

which leads to two pn-junctions and, depending on how we bias them (set VBE and VCB), operating modes similar to the MOSFET

Expansion note: which two junctions, and why there are modes at all

The emitter-base junction (EBJ) sits between the emitter and the base; the collector-base junction (CBJ) sits between the collector and the base. Each junction can be forward or reverse biased independently, giving four combinations. Three of them are the useful operating modes in the table below; the fourth (EBJ reverse, CBJ forward) is the reverse-active mode, which is rarely used and is not part of these notes.

(*) Let's consider the minority carrier concentrations at equilibrium assuming

Expansion note: why the three doping levels are ordered this way, and how the levels in the plot follow

The emitter is doped most heavily, the base more lightly and the collector lightest, so ND,E≫NA,B≫ND,C. Only this ordering matters; the exponents shown are typical orders of magnitude. At equilibrium the minority-carrier density in each region is ni2 divided by that region's doping, so the most heavily doped region (the emitter) has the fewest minority carriers and the lightest doped region (the collector) has the most. The dashed lines mark the edges of each depletion region and the solid lines mark the metallurgical junctions; the depletion region extends mostly into the lighter-doped side.

Operating ModesEBJCBJ
(1) Cutoff Modereversereverse
(2) Active Modeforwardreverse
(3) Saturation Modeforwardforward
Expansion note: how to read the modes and what each one is used for

Cutoff: both junctions are reverse biased, so almost no current flows and the transistor acts as an open switch. Active: the EBJ is forward and the CBJ is reverse; the collector current is controlled by VBE (or by the base current), which is the mode used for amplification. Saturation: both junctions are forward biased; the transistor is fully on and acts like a closed switch with a small voltage across it. These three correspond to the MOSFET's cutoff, saturation (amplifier) and triode regions in that order of use.

25-2

Active Mode

Expansion note: how to read this plot

The horizontal axis runs through the emitter, base and collector. The vertical axis is the density of minority carriers: holes in the emitter and collector (pn), electrons in the base (np). Forward bias on the EBJ lifts the density at the base edge of that junction to np0eVBE/VT (and the hole density at its emitter edge to pn0eVBE/VT) above the equilibrium values np0 and pn0. Reverse bias on the CBJ pulls the electron density at the collector edge of the base to zero. W is the width of the neutral base between the two depletion regions.

Notes:

  1. 1:

    The concentration of minority electrons in the base goes to zero on the collector side because of the reverse bias on that side. The concentration is linear because W is smaller than Ln, the diffusion length

    Expansion note: why a short base gives a straight-line profile

    Electrons diffusing across the base can recombine with holes, which would bend the profile downward. The average distance an electron travels before recombining is the diffusion length Ln. When the base is much thinner than Ln (W≪Ln), almost none recombine on the way, so the same number of electrons crosses every point of the base. A constant diffusion current requires a constant slope, so the concentration falls in a straight line from np0eVBE/VT at the emitter side to zero at the collector side.

  2. 2:

    With the high concentration of electrons in the emitter, they will diffuse into the base. A much smaller number of holes will diffuse base → emitter

    Expansion note: why electrons win over holes at the EBJ

    Diffusion follows the concentration gradient. The heavily doped emitter holds far more mobile electrons than the lightly doped base holds holes, so the electron flow from emitter to base is far larger than the hole flow from base to emitter. Only the electron flow is useful, because those electrons go on to the collector.

  3. 3:

    The strong electric field pulls electrons to the collector

    Expansion note: which field this is

    It is the field inside the reverse-biased collector-base depletion region. Electrons that diffuse across the base reach its edge and are swept across it by the field into the collector, which is why the base electron density is pinned to zero there.

  4. 4:

    We want W as small as practical to get as much emitter current to the collector

(*) We see two components of current: Desire i1 ≫ i2

Some of “1” recombine in “B” but most gets to “C”

Expansion note: where the three terminal currents come from

The electron current i1 (emitter into base) is what we want: nearly all of it is swept into the collector and becomes the collector current. A small part recombines with holes in the base, and the base lead must supply those holes. The unwanted hole current i2 (base into emitter) is also supplied by the base lead. The base current is the sum of the recombination current and i2, so both being small keeps iB small compared with iC.

Expansion note: where the denominators NA,BW and ND,ELp come from

Both currents are diffusion currents, i=AEqD× (slope of the minority concentration). For electrons in the base the profile is a straight line from np0eVBE/VT to zero over the width W, with np0=ni2/NA,B, so the slope is ni2eVBE/VT/(NA,BW). For holes in the emitter the profile decays over the hole diffusion length Lp with pn0=ni2/ND,E, giving ni2eVBE/VT/(ND,ELp). Dividing the two shows why a thin, lightly doped base and a heavily doped emitter make i1≫i2: i1i2=DnDpND,ELpNA,BW

(*) By making the base thin and NA,B ≪ ND,E it follows

iB= iCβ 50† <β< 200†

Expansion note: what β is and why it lies between about 50 and 200

β is the ratio of collector current to base current, β=iC/iB. A small iB relative to iC means a large β. A thinner base and a more heavily doped emitter push β up; typical modern npn transistors fall between about 50 and 200, and some can exceed 1000.

Expansion note: how α, β and the three currents are connected

Kirchhoff's current law on the transistor gives iE=iB+iC. With iC=βiB this becomes iE=(β+1)iB, and dividing by β gives iE=β+1βiC. Solving the last equation for iC gives iC=ββ+1iE=αiE,α=ββ+1,β=α1−α Since β is large, α is just below 1 (for example, β=100 gives α=0.990), meaning nearly all of the emitter current reaches the collector.

α is the measure of how effectively we are moving electrons from the emitter to the collector

β is the current gain → how well we produce collector current from base current

(*) This leads to a large-signal model

Expansion note: what the diode and the controlled source in this model represent

The diode between base and emitter is the forward-biased EBJ, so the base current follows the diode law iB=(IS/β)evBE/VT. The diamond is a current-controlled current source: whatever base current flows, the collector terminal carries βiB down to the emitter. Together they say "a small base current sets a large collector current".

That we can simplify by using a diode Constant voltage-drop model to avoid the exponential nonlinearity

Expansion note: what the constant-voltage-drop model changes

The exponential evBE/VT makes hand analysis messy. Because vBE barely changes (about 0.6 V to 0.8 V) over a wide range of current, we replace the diode by a fixed drop Vo (about 0.7 V) whenever it conducts. The next page uses Vo for this drop.

Expansion note: tracing the loop

Start at the Thevenin source and go around the base loop: the source raises the potential by Vth; IB then drops RthIB across Rth; the base-emitter drop Vo follows; finally the whole emitter current IE=(β+1)IB flows through RE and drops (β+1)IBRE. The drops must add up to the source, so the sum of the rises and falls around the loop is zero.

Expansion note: why the collector current stops depending on β

In the exact result the factor β/(β+1) appears once the RE term dominates the denominator: IC=β(Vth−Vo)Rth+(β+1)RE≈ββ+1Vth−VoRE≈Vth−VoRE The first step uses (β+1)RE≫Rth and the second uses β≫1, so that β/(β+1)≈1. Since β varies a lot from one transistor to the next and with temperature, a bias point that does not depend on it is a robust design; the price is the emitter resistor RE and a stiff divider (small Rth).

The transconductance gm is the change in collector current per volt of vbe; with IC=1 mA and VT=25 mV it is 40 mA/V. Expansion note: why BJT current is diffusion and MOSFET current is drift

In the BJT the collector current is minority electrons diffusing across the base, driven by the concentration gradient set by VBE. In the MOSFET the gate voltage induces a channel of majority carriers, and the drain-source voltage pulls them along it with an electric field, which is a drift current. That is why a BJT uses both carrier types while a MOSFET uses only one.

Expansion note: reading items 3 and 4 (control and temperature)

The BJT is current controlled: the base lead must always supply a current iB=iC/β. The MOSFET gate is insulated by an oxide layer, so its DC gate current is zero and only the voltage matters. For temperature, a BJT's collector current rises with temperature at a fixed VBE, which raises dissipation and can run away. In a MOSFET a rise in temperature lowers carrier mobility, which raises the channel resistance and lowers the current, so the device tends to limit itself.

Expansion note: the threshold in item 5 and the r_o effect in item 6

A BJT starts to conduct noticeably near VBE≈0.5 V and is usually treated as on at about 0.7 V; a MOSFET turns on when VGS exceeds its threshold Vt. In item 6 the output current is not perfectly flat in saturation: in a BJT a larger collector voltage widens the CBJ depletion region and narrows the neutral base; in a MOSFET it shortens the channel. Either way the current rises slightly with the output voltage, which is modeled as a finite output resistance ro.

Expansion note: comparing the two g_m expressions

Both models have a control voltage (vbe or vgs) setting a current source. The BJT's gm=IC/VT grows in direct proportion to the bias current, while the MOSFET's gm=2knID grows only as its square root. For equal bias current the BJT therefore usually provides the larger transconductance. The BJT model also has the finite input resistance rπ; the MOSFET gate draws no current, so its gate is open in the model.

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Identifying the BJT operating mode from the junction biases

Recognize it

You are given the terminal voltages (or told how the base-emitter and collector-base junctions are biased) and must say whether the transistor is in cutoff, active or saturation, or what the minority-carrier profile looks like.

Method

  1. Decide the EBJ bias: forward if VBE is above about 0.7 V (npn), reverse if VBE is zero or negative.
  2. Decide the CBJ bias: reverse if VC>VB (that is VCB>0), forward if VC is below VB by more than a few tenths of a volt.
  3. Read the mode from the table: reverse/reverse = cutoff, forward/reverse = active, forward/forward = saturation.
  4. If the mode is active, sketch the base profile as a straight line from np0eVBE/VT at the emitter side to zero at the collector side.

Checks

  • Active mode requires IC=βIB to hold; if the computed VC lands below VB the assumption is wrong and the device is saturated.
  • No current at all means cutoff; check that VBE is below the turn-on value.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up the sign convention: VCB>0 is reverse bias for the CBJ of an npn transistor.
  • Assuming the active mode without checking the collector voltage.

Worked in these notes: Operating Modes table, p. 25-1; Active Mode, pp. 25-2 and 25-3.

A2. Explaining the current components and why β is large

Recognize it

"Why does most of the emitter current reach the collector?" or "What design choices make β large?"

Method

  1. Name the two injected currents: electrons from the emitter into the base (i1) and holes from the base into the emitter (i2).
  2. Write both as diffusion currents with the same eVBE/VT factor: i1∝Dn/(NA,BW), i2∝Dp/(ND,ELp).
  3. Take the ratio: i1/i2=(Dn/Dp)(ND,ELp)/(NA,BW). Make it large with a heavily doped emitter (ND,E≫NA,B) and a thin base (small W).
  4. Note that a thin base also keeps recombination in the base small, so almost all of i1 reaches the collector.

Variants

  • Wider base: recombination grows, iB rises, β falls.
  • Lighter emitter doping: i2 grows relative to i1, so β falls.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the collector current is made of holes; in an npn device the collector current is electrons.
  • Using the base doping in the numerator of the ratio instead of the denominator.

Worked in these notes: Active Mode notes 1 to 4, p. 25-2; current components and i1, i2, p. 25-3.

A3. Relating iE, iB, iC with α and β

Recognize it

Given one of iE, iB, iC and one of α, β, find the others.

Method

  1. KCL: iE=iB+iC.
  2. Use iC=βiB, so iE=(β+1)iB and iC=ββ+1iE=αiE.
  3. Convert between the two gains: α=ββ+1 and β=α1−α.

Checks

  • α must be just below 1 and β must be large and positive.
  • iE is always the largest of the three currents.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the "+1" in iE=(β+1)iB.
  • Using iC=αiB instead of αiE.

Worked in these notes: p. 25-4.

A4. DC bias analysis of a voltage-divider-biased BJT (Thevenin and KVL)

Recognize it

A BJT with a base divider RB1, RB2, a collector resistor RC and an emitter resistor RE, with a supply VCC; you must find IB, IC and usually the terminal voltages.

Method

  1. Replace the transistor by the DC model: a diode (constant drop Vo if conducting) from base to emitter and a source βDCIB from collector to emitter.
  2. Thevenize the divider: Vth=VCCRB2/(RB1+RB2), Rth=RB1∥RB2.
  3. Write KVL around the base loop: Vth−RthIB−Vo−(β+1)IBRE=0.
  4. Solve for the base current: IB=Vth−VoRth+(β+1)RE.
  5. Then IC=βIB and IE=(β+1)IB.
  6. Terminal voltages: VE=IERE, VB=VE+Vo, VC=VCC−ICRC.
  7. Check the active mode: VC≥VB (CBJ reverse biased).

Checks

  • IB must come out positive; if Vth<Vo the transistor is off and all currents are zero.
  • If VC<VB by more than a few tenths of a volt the device is saturated and the active-mode formulas do not apply.

Variants

  • If (β+1)RE≫Rth and β≫1, use the shortcut IC≈(Vth−Vo)/RE, which does not depend on β.
  • No emitter resistor (RE=0): IB=(Vth−Vo)/Rth and IC is proportional to β, so the bias is far less stable.
  • Different β: with the shortcut the answer is unchanged; with the exact formula only the (β+1)RE term moves.

Common mistakes

  • Dropping the (β+1) factor on RE in the base loop (the whole emitter current flows in RE).
  • Putting the divider resistors in the loop separately instead of using Rth.
  • Using the shortcut without checking that (β+1)RE≫Rth.

Worked in these notes: pp. 25-5 and 25-6.

A5. Finding the small-signal parameters gm and rπ

Recognize it

You have a BJT amplifier and need its small-signal model; the bias current IC and β are known or computed.

Method

  1. Find the DC collector current IC (type A4).
  2. Transconductance: gm=IC/VT, with VT≈25 mV at room temperature.
  3. Input resistance: rπ=β/gm (equivalently VT/IB).
  4. Draw the model and attach the external resistors; then find the voltage gain.

Checks

  • Units: gm in A/V (mA/V if IC is in mA and VT in V gives 40 mA/V per mA).
  • The signal must be small (vbe well below about 10 mV) for the linear model to hold.

Common mistakes

  • Using the total current iC instead of the DC value IC in gm=IC/VT.
  • Using rπ=VT/IC instead of VT/IB (or β/gm).

Worked in these notes: pp. 25-6, 25-7 and 25-9.

A6. Comparing the BJT and the MOSFET

Recognize it

"List the differences between a BJT and a MOSFET" or "which device would you choose and why".

Method

  1. Carriers: bipolar (majority and minority) against unipolar (majority only).
  2. Current mechanism: diffusion against drift.
  3. Control: a small base current against a gate voltage with zero DC gate current.
  4. Temperature: BJT current rises with temperature (positive coefficient); MOSFET current falls (negative coefficient).
  5. Turn-on: VBE above about 0.7 V against VGS>Vt.
  6. Output resistance: base-width modulation against channel-length modulation, both giving ro.
  7. Models: DC and small-signal pairs, with gm=IC/VT against gm=2knID.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a MOSFET draws current at the gate; the DC gate current is zero.
  • Mixing up the sign of the temperature coefficients.

Worked in these notes: Comparison, pp. 25-7 to 25-9.
