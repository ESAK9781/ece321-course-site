Lesson 16: Common-Source Gain, Small-Signal Models and the Biased CS Amplifier

16-1

Common Source Amplifier Results

Expansion note: how to read the voltage transfer curve

The curve plots the output vDS of the common-source stage against the gate-to-source voltage vGS. Left of Vt the transistor is in cutoff, so no current flows and vDS sits at VDD. Between Vt and point B the transistor is in saturation and vDS falls as vGS rises. Right of B the transistor is in triode, and the curve flattens at a small vDS. The crossed marks on the arrow above the plot are the two boundaries between these regions.

Small-Signal Voltage Gain: Av= dvDSdvGS

which is the slope of the VTC

Expansion note: why the slope of the curve is the small-signal gain

A small change ΔvGS at the gate moves the operating point a short distance along the curve, and the output changes by ΔvDS≈(dvDS/dvGS)ΔvGS. The ratio of the two small changes is the gain, so the gain at any bias point is the slope of the curve at that point. A steeper part of the curve means a larger gain.

Av= ddvGS [VDD−12Kn(vGS−Vt)2RD]

12Kn(vGS−Vt)2iD in saturation

Av= − KnRD(VGS−Vt) = −KnRDVOV

↖  FET to FET  ↗      ↑ phase shift

Expansion note: what varies from FET to FET and what the minus sign means

Kn depends on the device (its width, length and process), and Vt differs from one transistor to the next, so the gain of a given bias point differs between devices. The minus sign is a phase shift: when vGS rises the drain current rises and the drop across RD pulls the output down, so the output is an inverted copy of the input, shifted by 180°.

Q. Where in SAT is the slope (dvDSdvGS) the greatest?

Av,max= VDD−VDS,B12VOV,B = VDD−VOV,B12VOV,B

Expansion note: why the steepest part of saturation is point B

In saturation the gain size |Av|=KnRDVOV rises steadily with the overdrive voltage, that is with vGS. The largest overdrive that still keeps the transistor in saturation is VOV,B, reached at point B where VDS,B=VOV,B. The slope of the curve is therefore steepest at B. Beyond B the transistor is in triode and the saturation formulas no longer apply.

(*) We don't want to operate at B because there is no swing† but this gives us a maximum achievable

Expansion note: why point B is not a usable operating point

At B the transistor sits exactly on the edge of saturation, VDS=VOV. Any upward motion of the gate voltage pushes it into triode, where the gain collapses and the output flattens, so the signal is clipped at once. Point B therefore gives only an upper bound on the gain; a real bias point sits between A and B.

Expansion note: where the formula for VOV,B comes from

At B the transistor is at the edge of saturation, so VDS,B=VOV,B. Writing VDS=VDD−IDRD with ID=12KnVOV2 gives VOV,B=VDD−12KnRDVOV,B2, which rearranges to the quadratic

12KnRDVOV,B2+VOV,B−VDD=0⇒VOV,B=1+2KnRDVDD−1KnRD.

The positive root is kept because an overdrive voltage cannot be negative. In consistent units, mA/V² times kΩ is 1/V, so KnRD has units of 1/V and the expression comes out in volts.

|Av,max|= VDD−VOV,B12VOV,B = 1.8V−0.213V12(0.213V)† = 14.9† V/V

the gain will get smaller as we move away from pt B toward pt A of the VTC

Expansion note: why the gain falls on either side of B

Toward A the curve flattens at VDD, so its slope approaches zero. Past B, in triode, the curve also flattens, and the saturation gain formula stops applying. The slope is largest between those flat parts, at B, which is why |Av,max| is an upper limit that no bias point inside saturation reaches.

When the output must stay nearly distortion-free, use whichever of the two limits is smaller.

Expansion note: reading the notation iD, ID and id

A lowercase letter with an uppercase subscript (iD, vGS) is the total instantaneous value. An uppercase letter with an uppercase subscript (ID, VGS) is the DC bias value. A lowercase letter with a lowercase subscript (id, vgs) is the small ac signal. Each total is the sum of the other two: iD=ID+id and vGS=VGS+vgs.

The first term is constant, the DC current ID. The second grows in proportion to the signal vgs, so it is the useful linear part. The third grows as the square of vgs and is the distortion: it is the part that makes the output differ in shape from the input.

Differentiating iD=12Kn(vGS−Vt)2 with respect to vGS gives diD/dvGS=Kn(vGS−Vt), and evaluating it at the bias value VGS gives gm=Kn(VGS−Vt)=KnVOV. The unit is A/V (siemens), a current out per volt in, which is why it is called a transconductance.

Expansion note: which form of gm to use

All three forms are the same number: gm=KnVOV=2KnID=2ID/VOV. Use KnVOV when VOV is known, 2KnID when only the drain current is known, and 2ID/VOV when the current and overdrive are known but Kn is not.

Small-Signal (AC) NMOS Model

(*) Used to analytically determine: Av, Ri and Ro†

To use:   DC voltage supplies → short

DC current supplies → open

C → ∞ → short

Expansion note: why DC sources and large capacitors are replaced this way in the ac model

The small-signal model keeps only the changes around the bias point. An ideal DC voltage supply cannot change, so a change across it is zero, which is a short circuit. An ideal DC current source cannot change its current, so its signal current is zero, which is an open circuit. A capacitor large enough to be treated as C→∞ has negligible impedance at the signal frequency and acts as a short.

We need to impress our linear part of id equation

id= gmvgs

16-7

This is called the hybrid-π model

Expansion note: why the gate terminal is open in this model

The gate of a MOSFET is insulated from the channel, so no signal current flows into it (ig=0). The gate and source terminals are therefore drawn with nothing between them except the voltage vgs they carry, and the only current in the model is the dependent source gmvgs from the drain to the source.

(*) Let's massage it to get an equivalent† Form (start without ro). Since gmvgs= id we can add a connection and not change ig= 0

Expansion note: why adding the connection does not change the circuit

The controlled source passes the current gmvgs from the drain toward the source. Joining its lower end to the gate node gives that current a new path, but the same current leaves the node again toward the source, so no net current enters or leaves the gate and ig stays zero.

Now we have a current of gmvgs with the voltage across it being vgs → ratio → vgsgmvgs = 1gm

So

Expansion note: why a current gmvgs with vgs across it is a resistor 1/gm

A two-terminal element that carries a current i with a voltage v across it behaves as a resistance v/i. Here the voltage is vgs and the current is gmvgs, so the ratio is vgs/(gmvgs)=1/gm, independent of the signal size. The leg between the gate node and the source can therefore be drawn as a resistor 1/gm.

Since gmvgs is just the current thru 1/gm we can redraw as

which is called the T-model

(*) We can incorporate the channel-length modulation (ro) by connecting it between d & s

Expansion note: where ro goes and what it represents

With channel-length modulation the drain current rises slightly as vDS rises, so the drain-source path behaves like a finite resistance ro in parallel with the controlled source, about VA/ID (Equation Sheet 4). It is drawn directly between the drain and source terminals in both the hybrid-π and the T-model, and it is left out when λ=0.

We like to use the T-model when “source” has other components connected to it

Expansion note: why the T-model suits a resistor connected to the source

In the T-model the resistor 1/gm sits between the gate node and the source. Any component wired to the source terminal then lands in series with 1/gm and combines with it directly, with no need to account separately for vgs. The hybrid-π model is the better choice when the extra components are at the drain or gate.

ex. Consider a Common Source amplifier with gate biasing circuit. Find the gain.

  1. Set DC source to 0 V (ground)
  2. Replace C's by shorts
  3. Insert the small-signal model
Expansion note: the three steps of an ac analysis

First every DC supply is replaced by its ac equivalent: a voltage supply becomes ground, so both VDD rails become ground connections. Second, the coupling capacitors, treated as C=∞, become wires. Third, the transistor is replaced by its small-signal model, and the circuit that remains is a plain linear circuit that can be solved with the usual circuit rules.

Expansion note: why RG1 and RD both end at ground in the ac circuit

After the VDD supplies become ground, RG1 runs from the gate node to ground in parallel with RG2, and RD runs from the drain to ground in parallel with the load RL (the output capacitor is a wire). The source is connected straight to the gate node through its capacitor, so the full source voltage appears at the gate.

  • • Note   vgs= vs
  • • vo= −gmvgs(RD∥RL)

then   Av= vovs = −gm(RD∥RL)

If we consider no-load or open-circuit then

Avo= −gmRD   same as before!

Expansion note: why the loaded gain uses RD in parallel with RL

The controlled source pushes its current gmvgs into whatever is connected between the drain and ground, which is RD and RL in parallel. Disconnecting the load leaves only RD, which gives the open-circuit gain Avo=−gmRD, the same result as the unloaded stage on page 16-6. Connecting RL lowers the gain because RD∥RL<RD.

Appendix

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Gain of a common-source stage from the slope of its voltage transfer curve

Recognize it

You are given a common-source stage (an NMOS with drain resistor RD to VDD) and asked for its small-signal gain at a bias point, or for the largest gain it can reach.

Method

  1. Find the bias: VOV=VGS−Vt, ID=12KnVOV2, VDS=VDD−IDRD.
  2. Confirm saturation: VDS≥VOV.
  3. Gain: Av=−KnRDVOV=−gmRD, or Av=−VDD−VDS12VOV.
  4. For the largest gain, bias at point B: VOV,B=1+2KnRDVDD−1KnRD and |Av,max|=VDD−VOV,B12VOV,B.

Checks

  • |Av|≤|Av,max| for every bias point in saturation.
  • The two forms of Av agree.

Variants

  • VDS given instead of VGS: use VOV=2ID/Kn with ID=(VDD−VDS)/RD.
  • Larger RD raises the gain but lowers VOV,B and the available swing.

Common mistakes

  • Dropping the minus sign: the CS stage inverts.
  • Using the saturation gain formula when the transistor is in triode or cutoff.
  • Taking VOV,B as the bias point; it is only the upper limit.

Worked in these notes: pp. 16-1 to 16-2 (Ex. 1).

A2. Largest input amplitude without output distortion

Recognize it

A common-source stage has a known bias and gain; find the largest vgs peak that keeps the transistor in saturation (and out of cutoff).

Method

  1. DC step with vgs=0: VGS, VOV, ID.
  2. VDS=VDD−IDRD and verify VDS≥VOV.
  3. Gain Av=−KnVOVRD.
  4. Saturation at the positive peak: VDS−|Av|vgs,pk>VGS+vgs,pk−Vt, so vgs,pk<VDS−VOV1+|Av|.
  5. Cutoff at the negative peak: vgs,pk<VOV.
  6. Small-signal linearity: vgs,pk≤0.2VOV (Equation Sheet 4).

Checks

  • The limit is positive; if VDS<VOV the bias is already in triode.
  • Simulate and watch the output flatten near the limit.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the extra vgs,pk on the VGS side of the boundary (the factor is 1+|Av|, not |Av|).
  • Using the peak-to-peak value in place of the peak.

Worked in these notes: pp. 16-3 to 16-4 (Ex. 2).

A3. Finding gm and the gain in terms of gm

Recognize it

You know the bias current or overdrive voltage and need the transconductance, or you need the gain written with gm.

Method

  1. Pick the form that fits what is known: gm=KnVOV, 2KnID or 2ID/VOV.
  2. If Kn is missing, Kn=2ID/VOV2.
  3. Gain: vds=−RDid=−gmRDvgs, so Av=−gmRD.
  4. With channel-length modulation, ro≈VA/ID and Avo=−gm(RD∥ro) (Equation Sheet 4).

Checks

  • Units: mA/V² times V is mA/V, a conductance.
  • gm increases with ID and with VOV.

Common mistakes

  • Using VGS where VOV=VGS−Vt belongs.
  • Mixing mA and A with kΩ and Ω.

Worked in these notes: pp. 16-5 to 16-6.

A4. Choosing and drawing a small-signal model (hybrid-π or T)

Recognize it

An amplifier schematic must be turned into its ac circuit so the gain, Ri or Ro can be found.

Method

  1. Replace DC voltage supplies by short circuits (ground) and DC current sources by open circuits.
  2. Replace large coupling and bypass capacitors by short circuits.
  3. Replace the MOSFET by the hybrid-π model (ig=0, source gmvgs from drain to source) or the T-model (gmvgs into the gate node, then 1/gm to the source).
  4. Add ro between the drain and source when λ≠0.
  5. Use the T-model when other components connect to the source terminal; otherwise either model gives the same result.

Checks

  • No DC source remains in the ac circuit.
  • The gate carries no current in either model.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving VDD as a source instead of grounding it.
  • Putting 1/gm in the drain leg.

Worked in these notes: pp. 16-6 to 16-8.

A5. Gain of a biased common-source amplifier with a load

Recognize it

A common-source stage with a gate bias divider, coupling capacitors and a load RL; find Av or Avo.

Method

  1. Find gm from the DC bias (type A3).
  2. Draw the ac circuit (type A4): RG1∥RG2 at the gate, RD∥RL at the drain.
  3. vgs=vs for an ideal source.
  4. vo=−gmvgs(RD∥RL), so Av=−gm(RD∥RL).
  5. Remove the load for the open-circuit gain: Avo=−gmRD.

Checks

  • |Av|<|Avo| whenever RL is finite.
  • The sign is negative.

Variants

  • Source with its own resistance: the input divider Ri/(Ri+RS) multiplies the result.
  • With ro: replace RD by RD∥ro.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving RL out of the drain parallel combination.
  • Including the bias divider in the gain when the source is ideal.

Worked in these notes: p. 16-9.

A6. Checking that the small-signal approximation holds

Recognize it

You are told the signal amplitude and must decide whether the linear model is trustworthy.

Method

  1. Expand iD=12Kn(VOV+vgs)2 into a DC, a linear and a quadratic term.
  2. The linear term must dominate: KnVOVvgs≫12Knvgs2, that is vgs≪2VOV.
  3. Apply the working rule vgs,pk≤0.2VOV.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing vgs with VGS instead of VOV.
  • Confusing this limit with the saturation limit of type A2; both must be met.

Worked in these notes: pp. 16-4 to 16-5.
