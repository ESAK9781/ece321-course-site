Lesson 15: MOSFET Amplifier Models, Attributes and the Voltage Transfer Curve

15-1

Amplifiers

(*) Recall from circuits I and II

DC supplies provide extra power to achieve amplification

PDC = VCC ICC +VEE IEE

Expansion note: why an amplifier needs DC supplies

An amplifier delivers more signal power to the load than it takes from the source. Energy cannot be created, so the extra power comes from the DC supplies. The supplies deliver VCCICC+VEEIEE in total, and the signal power gain is paid for out of that.

(*) VCC & −VEE also impose saturation limits

Expansion note: what the output limits mean

The output voltage cannot go above the positive supply or below the negative supply (in practice it stops a little short of each). Outside the input range where the amplifier is linear, the output sits at one of the two limits and the amplifier is saturated. The dashed lines on the graph mark those two output limits and the source voltages at which they are reached.

Types of Gain

Av = vovi     Ai = ioii     Ap = voioviii

(*) We can express gain in ratios (V/V, A/A, W/W) or in decibels (dB)

Expansion note: converting a gain to decibels

Voltage and current gains in dB are 20log10|A|; power gain is 10log10Ap. A voltage gain of 10 V/V is 20 dB, 100 V/V is 40 dB, and a gain of 1 is 0 dB. Because Ap=AvAi, the power gain in dB is the average of the other two: Ap(dB)=12[Av(dB)+Ai(dB)].

Open-Loop Op Amp Model

ROUT ≈ 0† Ω

Rin ≈ ∞† Ω

A ≈ ∞†

Expansion note: how to read the three approximations

An ideal op amp has infinite input resistance (no current into the inputs), zero output resistance (the output is a perfect voltage source) and a very large open-loop gain. Real devices have Rin of megohms or more, Rout of tens of ohms and A of about 105, large enough that the ideal values work as approximations.

(*) We create an inverting amplifier by (1) grounding the + input (2) including RS & RL, then

VLoadVS ≈ −A provided Ri ≫ RS

Expansion note: why the gain is about −A

With the + input grounded, the signal drives the − input, so the output is −A times the voltage at the input. The source and load resistors make two voltage dividers (input and output). If Ri≫RS and RL≫Ro, the dividers pass nearly all of the signal, and the overall gain stays close to −A. The next page shows the dividers explicitly.

The input divider gives Vi=VSRi/(Ri+RS). The controlled source then produces AvoVi, and the output divider gives Vo=AvoViRL/(RL+Ro). Multiplying,

Cascaded Amplifiers

Q How do we analyze ?

VL = Vi Avo1 × Ri2Ri2+Ro1 × Avo2 × Ri3Ri3+Ro2 × Avo3RLRL+Ro3

Expansion note: why each stage is loaded by the next

The next stage's input resistance is the load of the stage before it. So between the controlled source of one stage and the input of the next sits a voltage divider formed by Ro of the first stage and Ri of the second. Each stage's gain is therefore its Avo times a divider factor, and the stage gains multiply. Because the circuits are linked this way, the stages cannot be analyzed as if each were unloaded.

For the stages shown: the factors are 10 × 0.990 (100 kΩ against 1 kΩ), 100 × 0.909 (10 kΩ against 1 kΩ) and 1 × 0.909 (100 Ω against 10 Ω).

  • An input stage may have large Rin but modest gain

Expansion note: why the stages are given different jobs

A large input resistance at the first stage keeps the source from being loaded down. The middle stage supplies most of the gain. The last stage has a low output resistance so that the load, which may be small, does not take much voltage from the divider RL/(RL+Ro).

Q. How experimentally determine Ri

  • Measure Vs
  • Adjust R until

Vi = VS/2

→ R = Ri

Expansion note: why a half-voltage reading gives Ri

With a variable resistor R in series with the source, the amplifier input sees a divider: Vi=VSRi/(R+Ri). When Vi falls to exactly half of VS, R=Ri.

Q. How Find Avo ?

  • Open circuit the load
  • Apply VS†
  • Measure Vo & Vi

→ Avo = VoVi

Q. How Find Ro ?

  • Ground the input†

→ AvoVi = 0

  • Apply VS & a variable R in series†
  • Measure Vo†
  • Adjust R until

Vo = VS/2†

→ R = Ro

Expansion note: why grounding the input isolates Ro

With the input grounded, Vi=0 and the controlled source is zero, so the only thing left between the output terminals is Ro. A source applied at the output through a variable resistor then forms a divider with Ro, and Vo=VS/2 when R=Ro.

(*) We will operate our MOSFET in saturation where it behaves as a voltage-controlled current source

VGS > Vt

VDS > VOV

VGD < Vt

ID = 12Kn ( VGS − Vt )2

Expansion note: three conditions, one region

VGS>Vt means a channel exists. VDS>VOV (with VOV=VGS−Vt) and VGD<Vt say the same thing about the drain end: the channel has pinched off, and the current no longer depends on VDS. That is why the device acts like a current source controlled by VGS.

15-7

Common Source Amplifier

KVL : VDD − ID RD − Vo = 0

Vo = VDD − ID RD

3 operating modes

(1) VGS < Vt → cutoff → ID = 0

Vo = VDD − IDRD = VDD

(2) VDS ≥ VOV → saturation → ID = 12Kn(VGS−Vt)2

Vo = VDD − 12KnRD(VGS−Vt)2

(3) VDS < VOV → triode → ID = Kn[(VGS−Vt)VDS−12VDS2]

Expansion note: reading the three modes

In cutoff no current flows, so there is no voltage across RD and the output sits at the supply. In saturation the current follows the square law, so Vo falls as VGS rises. When Vo has fallen so far that VDS drops below VOV, the transistor enters triode and the current is given by the triode equation. Both formulas for ID meet at VDS=VOV.

Voltage Transfer Characteristic

(*) We will want to operate between Vt and VGS,B

But this is nonlinear, how do we get amplification ?

Expansion note: why a curved characteristic can still amplify

Over a small enough stretch the curve is nearly straight. If the bias point sits on the steep part and the signal is small, the output follows the input with a nearly constant slope, which is the small-signal gain. The next pages find the steepest point and where to place the bias.

Point B Operation

VDS = VGS − Vt

But recall

VDS = VDD − RDID = VDD − 12KnRD ( VGS − Vt )2

so with VGS−Vt = VDS we get

VDS = VDD − 12KnRD VDS2

Rearranging

12KnRD VDS2 + VDS −VDD = 0

Expansion note: why VDS equals VGS−Vt at point B

Point B is the boundary between saturation and triode, where VDS=VOV=VGS−Vt. Substituting this into the saturation expression for VDS=VDD−RDID gives a quadratic in the single unknown VDS.

15-9

Quadratic Formula

VDS,B = −1 ± √( 1 − 4 (12KnRD)(−VDD) ) ⁄ 2(12KnRD)

Only the " + " solution is

VDS,B = 1+2KnRDVDD − 1 ⁄ KnRD = VOV,B

Then

VGS,B = Vt+VDS,B

Expansion note: why only the plus sign is kept

With a=12KnRD, b=1 and c=−VDD, the discriminant 1+4aVDD is larger than 1, so the square root is larger than 1. The minus sign would give a negative VDS, which a transistor in this circuit cannot have, so only the plus solution is physical.

VDS,B=−1+1+2KnRDVDDKnRD

(*) We don't want to operate at B because it borders triode but it will tell us the maximum gain achievable !

Expansion note: why point B sets the upper limit on gain

On the saturation part of the curve the slope dVDS/dVGS is steepest at its far end, point B, just before the device enters triode. The slope is the gain, so B marks the largest gain the circuit can give. Biasing exactly there would leave no room for the signal to swing downward in VDS, which is why it is only a reference.

Expansion note: gain, swing and linearity pull in different directions

The steepest part of the VTC (largest gain) is near point B, but that is also close to triode, so a downward signal swing is cut off. Placing Q near cutoff instead cuts off the upward swing. A Q in the middle of the sloping part leaves room for both swings, at a somewhat lower gain. And whatever the position, vgs has to stay small enough that the curve is nearly straight over its range, or the output is distorted.

Appendix

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Overall gain of an amplifier between a source and a load

Recognize it

A source VS with resistance RS drives an amplifier modeled by Ri, Ro and Avo, and the amplifier drives a load RL. You are asked for Vo/VS or for what the resistances must satisfy.

Method

  1. Input divider: Vi=VSRiRi+RS.
  2. Controlled source: AvoVi.
  3. Output divider: Vo=AvoViRLRL+Ro.
  4. Combine: G=VoVS=RiRi+RSAvoRLRL+Ro.

Checks

  • Each divider factor is less than 1, so |G|<|Avo|.
  • If Ri≫RS and RL≫Ro, G≈Avo.

Common mistakes

  • Using Ro in the input divider or RS in the output divider.
  • Forgetting that Avo is the open-circuit gain: it applies only before the output divider.

Worked in these notes: pp. 15-2, 15-3.

A2. Analyzing a cascade of amplifier stages

Recognize it

Several stages are connected output to input, each modeled by Ri, Avo and Ro. You need Av or Gv.

Method

  1. For each stage find its loaded gain: Avk=Avo,kRi,k+1Ri,k+1+Ro,k; for the last stage use RL.
  2. Multiply: Av=Av1Av2Av3 (from the input of stage 1 to the load).
  3. Include the source: Gv=Ri1Ri1+RSAv.

Checks

  • Removing a stage changes the loading on the one before it, so the gain is not simply divided by that stage's gain (for the notes' cascade, dropping stage 3 drops the gain by about a factor of 9).
  • Av never includes RS; Gv does.

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying the open-circuit gains and forgetting the loading dividers.
  • Taking the loading divider from the wrong side: it is the stage's own Ro against the next stage's Ri.

Worked in these notes: p. 15-4 (three-stage cascade).

A3. Measuring Ri, Avo and Ro experimentally

Recognize it

"How experimentally determine …": the amplifier is a black box and you may only apply sources and measure voltages.

Method

  1. Ri: put a variable resistor R in series with the source, adjust until Vi=VS/2; then R=Ri.
  2. Avo: remove the load, apply a signal, measure Vo and Vi; Avo=Vo/Vi.
  3. Ro: ground the input so the controlled source is zero, apply a source at the output through a variable resistor R and adjust until Vo=VS/2; then R=Ro.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring Avo with the load attached: that gives the loaded gain.
  • Leaving the input signal on while measuring Ro.

Worked in these notes: p. 15-5.

A4. Finding the region of a common-source stage and its output voltage

Recognize it

A MOSFET with drain resistor RD and supply VDD is biased by a given VGS. Find ID and Vo.

Method

  1. KVL: Vo=VDD−IDRD.
  2. If VGS<Vt: cutoff, ID=0, Vo=VDD.
  3. Otherwise assume saturation: ID=12Kn(VGS−Vt)2, Vo=VDD−12KnRD(VGS−Vt)2.
  4. Check VDS≥VOV. If not, use triode: ID=Kn[(VGS−Vt)VDS−12VDS2] with VDS=Vo.

Common mistakes

  • Using the saturation equation without checking VDS.
  • Comparing VDS with Vt instead of VOV.

Worked in these notes: pp. 15-6, 15-7.

A5. Locating point B on the voltage transfer characteristic

Recognize it

Find the largest gain a common-source stage can give, given VDD, Kn and RD.

Method

  1. At B, VDS=VGS−Vt and VDS=VDD−12KnRDVDS2.
  2. Rearrange: 12KnRDVDS2+VDS−VDD=0.
  3. Quadratic formula, keeping the plus sign: VDS,B=1+2KnRDVDD−1KnRD.
  4. VGS,B=Vt+VDS,B.

Checks

  • 0<VDS,B<VDD.

Common mistakes

  • Keeping the minus root, which is negative.
  • Forgetting that the coefficient of VDS2 is 12KnRD rather than 1.

Worked in these notes: pp. 15-8, 15-9.

A6. Choosing the Q-point

Recognize it

"Biasing: establishing the Q-point": pick the DC point on the VTC.

Method

  1. Restrict yourself to the sloping part of the VTC between A and B.
  2. Close to B: more gain but little room for the output to move toward triode.
  3. Close to A: less gain and little room toward cutoff.
  4. Check that vgs is small enough that the section is linear.

Common mistakes

  • Maximizing gain only and ignoring the swing.
  • Biasing at B itself.

Worked in these notes: pp. 15-9, 15-10.
