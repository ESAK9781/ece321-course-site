Lesson 29: Analyzing and Synthesizing Pull-Up and Pull-Down Networks

29-1

Implementation of Logic Gates using CMOS

(*) Recall of last time

PUN: Pulls Output up to VDD.

→ All PMOS

PDN: Pulls Output down to ground (0 V).

→ All NMOS

PUN & PDN must be complementary

Expansion note: what "complementary" means for the two networks

For every input combination exactly one network conducts. If both conducted, a direct path from VDD to ground would exist and the output would sit at some in-between voltage while the supply wastes current. If neither conducted, the output would float. Because exactly one conducts, the output is always pulled hard to VDD or to ground and a CMOS gate draws almost no DC current in either steady state.

VIN = 0 (low)

VIN = VDD (high)

Expansion note: reading the two switch pictures

With VIN = 0 the NMOS has VGS = 0, so it is off, while the PMOS has VSG = VDD and is on: the output connects to VDD and VOUT = VDD. With VIN = VDD the roles swap and VOUT = 0. This is why the inverter inverts.

BLUF

  • Switches in series act like logical AND
  • Switches in parallel act like logical OR
  • The PUN implements Y (the output function)
    The PDN implements Y¯ (the complement of the output function)
  • Since PMOS requires a low input (0) to turn on, PMOS inputs require a complement
Expansion note: why series means AND and parallel means OR

A series string passes current only if every switch is closed: switch 1 and switch 2 and so on. A parallel group passes current if any one switch is closed: switch 1 or switch 2. "Closed" means the transistor is on, which is a high gate for an NMOS and a low gate for a PMOS.

Expansion note: why the PDN implements the complement of the output

The PDN conducts when its logic expression is 1, and conducting pulls the output low. So the expression the PDN is built from is true exactly when the output is 0, which is Y¯. The PUN conducts, and pulls the output high, when the output is 1, so it is built from Y itself (written with complemented inputs because PMOS switch on a low gate).

Expansion note: reading the PUN and PDN lines

The PUN has P1 in parallel with P2. Each PMOS is on when its input is low, so the PUN conducts when A¯ or B¯ is 1, giving Y=A¯+B¯. DeMorgan's law turns this into Y=AB¯. The PDN has N1 in series with N2, so it conducts when AB = 1, and conducting pulls the output low: the output is the complement of that, Y=AB¯. Both networks give the same function, a NAND gate.

(*) Recall, RDS,p > RDS,n because μp < μn,
so having P1 & P2 in parallel together means their resistance is in parallel and will be the lowest, giving the smallest τ=RC and best condition for switching

Expansion note: why parallel PMOS give the best switching condition

Holes are less mobile than electrons (μp<μn), so a PMOS of the same size has a larger on-resistance than an NMOS. Putting the two PMOS in parallel halves the pull-up resistance when both are on (A = B = 0), which lowers the time constant τ=RC of charging the output. The worst case is a single PMOS conducting (one input high), where the resistance is that of one PMOS. The series NMOS pair, by contrast, always adds its two resistances, which is why NAND gates are preferred over NOR gates.

The two PMOS are in series, so the PUN conducts only when both are on, which needs A low and B low: YPUN=A¯B¯. DeMorgan's law says A¯B¯=A+B¯, the NOR function. The two NMOS are in parallel, so the PDN conducts when A+B = 1 and then pulls the output low, giving YPDN=A+B¯. The truth table agrees: Y is 1 only for A = B = 0.

(*) PMOS in series not as good because they have high RDS and low mobility. We try to avoid NOR gates when designing more complex circuits because of the PMOS and the larger time constants!

Expansion note: why NOR gates are avoided in complex circuits

In a NOR gate the weak PMOS devices are stacked in series, so the pull-up resistance is about twice that of one PMOS, and the pull-up time constant τ=RC is large. In a NAND gate the series devices are the stronger NMOS, so the stack costs much less. Designers therefore prefer NAND-based structures when a choice exists.

Notation Simplification

1 (high) to turn ON

0 (low) to turn ON

Expansion note: what the simplified symbols mean

The simplified NMOS is the plain transistor: it turns on when its gate is high. The simplified PMOS carries a small bubble on the gate, the usual sign of an active-low input: it turns on when its gate is low. The bubble replaces the source arrow, so from here on a PMOS is recognized by its bubble and an NMOS by its absence.

Determine the logic gate

(*) From PUN

Y = (A+B¯)(A¯+B)

Expansion note: reading the PUN of Ex. 3 into an expression

The upper pair of PMOS has gates A¯ and B in parallel. A PMOS is on when its gate is low, so the A¯ device is on when A = 1 and the B device is on when B = 0: the pair conducts when A+B¯. The lower pair has gates A and B¯, on when A = 0 or B = 1, so it conducts when A¯+B. The two pairs are in series (AND), giving Y=(A+B¯)(A¯+B).

expand

Y = AA¯ + AB + B¯A¯ + B¯B

simplify

Y = AB+A¯B¯

This gives an XNOR gate

Expansion note: why two of the four products vanish

A variable times its own complement is always 0, so AA¯=0 and B¯B=0. What remains is Y=AB+A¯B¯, which is 1 when A and B are equal: the XNOR (equivalence) function. Check: A = B = 0 gives (0+1)(1+0)=1 and A = 0, B = 1 gives (0+0)(1+1)=0.

(*) From PDN → Y = A¯B+AB¯¯ → Y = AB + A¯B¯†

DeMorgan: reverse input, change AND ↔ OR, reverse total

Expansion note: getting the same answer from the PDN

The PDN has two series branches in parallel: the left branch (A¯, B) conducts when A¯B = 1 and the right branch (A, B¯) when AB¯ = 1. When either conducts the output is pulled low, so Y=A¯B+AB¯¯. Applying DeMorgan's law as the notes say (complement each input, swap AND and OR, complement the total) gives Y=(A+B¯)(A¯+B)=AB+A¯B¯, the same XNOR function the PUN produced. That the two networks agree is the check that the gate is a proper complementary pair.

Ex. 4

Realize the PUN & PDN for Y=A(B+CD)¯

STEP 1: First work with the PUN

  • Apply DeMorgan →
    1. Complement each input
    2. Swap AND & OR (inside to outside)
    3. Complement

① A(B+CD)¯ → A¯(B¯+C¯D¯)¯

② A¯+B¯(C¯+D¯)¯

③ A¯+B¯(C¯+D¯)

Expansion note: what each of the three DeMorgan moves does

Move 1 puts a bar on every input letter and leaves the big bar alone. Move 2 swaps every AND for an OR and every OR for an AND, working from the innermost group outward: the product C¯D¯ becomes a sum, the sum with B¯ becomes a product, and the outer product with A¯ becomes a sum. Move 3 complements the whole expression, which cancels the big bar and leaves Y=A¯+B¯(C¯+D¯). A direct check with the textbook's step-by-step DeMorgan gives the same result: A(B+CD)¯=A¯+B+CD¯=A¯+B¯(C¯+D¯).

  • Remember, PUN works with PMOS and complemented inputs
Expansion note: how the expression becomes the PUN

Read Y=A¯+B¯(C¯+D¯) as a switch network: a plus is a parallel connection and a product is a series connection. So the PMOS with gate A is in parallel with a branch made of the PMOS with gate B in series with the parallel pair C and D. Each PMOS turns on when its gate is low, which is exactly the "complemented" letter in the expression. The gates therefore receive the plain signals A, B, C and D; no inverters are needed here.

STEP 2: Synthesize the PDN

If Y=A(B+CD)¯ then Y¯ = A(B+CD)

  • Remember, PDN works with NMOS and uncomplemented inputs
Expansion note: how the expression becomes the PDN

The PDN conducts when Y¯ = 1, that is when A(B+CD) = 1. Product means series, sum means parallel: NMOS A in series with the parallel combination of NMOS B and the series pair C, D. Each NMOS is on when its gate is high, so the gates take the plain letters.

(*) Recall, we can synthesize the PDN as the logic dual of the PUN

Y=A¯+B¯(C¯+D¯)

  • Complements vars
  • Work inside out, swap AND ↔ OR
Expansion note: how the dual gives the PDN from the PUN

Swap AND and OR in A¯+B¯(C¯+D¯) to get A¯(B¯+C¯D¯), then complement the variables to get A(B+CD), the PDN expression. In circuit terms every series branch of the PUN becomes a parallel branch of the PDN and vice versa, and PMOS become NMOS.

Expansion note: minterms and maxterms for this table

A minterm is a product that contains every input (complemented or not) and is 1 for exactly one row. Rows 01 and 10 have Y = 1, so the minterms are A¯B and AB¯. A maxterm is a sum that is 0 for exactly one row, used where Y = 0: rows 00 and 11 give A+B and A¯+B¯. The notes use sum of products (SOP), so the minterms are what matter here.

STEP 1: Express Y & Y¯ using SOP

Y = A¯B+AB¯

Y¯ = A¯B¯+AB

STEP 2: Create PUN (use Y, PMOS, input† complements)

Expansion note: how the PUN is drawn from the minterms

Each product term is a series branch and the sum is the parallel of the branches. The term A¯B needs a PMOS that is on when A = 0, so its gate gets A, and a PMOS that is on when B = 1, so its gate gets B¯. The term AB¯ needs gates A¯ and B. The PUN therefore needs the complemented signals A¯ and B¯, produced by two extra inverters.

STEP 3: Create PDN (use Y¯, NMOS, no† complements)

Expansion note: how the PDN is drawn from the complement

The PDN conducts when Y¯ = A¯B¯+AB = 1. NMOS turn on for a high gate, so each letter in the expression goes straight to a gate: the branch A¯B¯ has NMOS with gates A¯ and B¯ in series, and the branch AB has gates A and B in series, the two branches in parallel. The letters appear exactly as written in the expression, with no extra complementing of the gate signal.

Note: This is NOT the dual of the PUN

29-8

Q. How create PDN using dual? [swap AND/OR]

Start from the PUN: Y=A¯B+AB¯. Swap AND and OR: (A¯+B)(A+B¯). Complement the variables: Y¯=(A+B¯)(A¯+B). In the circuit: swap every series for a parallel and every parallel for a series, and change PMOS to NMOS with the same gate signals.

Expansion note: why the dual PDN is a different but valid network

The dual PDN conducts when (A+B¯)(A¯+B) = 1. Expanding gives AB+A¯B¯ (the cross terms AA¯ and B¯B are 0), the same condition as the direct PDN of Step 3. The two networks have different shapes but the same function and the same number of transistors. The textbook notes that a PDN and PUN need not be duals, and that the direct synthesis is often the easier route.

Q. How many transistors required?

Every input signal drives one PMOS and one NMOS, so a gate with N transistor inputs needs 2N. Here the PUN has 4 and the PDN has 4, which is 8, plus two inverters (2 transistors each) to make A¯ and B¯: 12 transistors in all.

Expansion note: why this count matters

An exclusive-OR gate is not cheap in CMOS: 12 transistors against 4 for a NAND or a NOR. More transistors mean more chip area and more capacitance to charge, so designers avoid XOR where a simpler gate will do, and share the inverters that make complemented inputs.

Noise Margins

DEF: How much positive or negative noise can be added to a signal before the receiving gate misreads the value.

(*) Let's propose an idealized logic inverter

Expansion note: how to read the idealized inverter plot

The horizontal axis is the input, the vertical axis the output. For any input up to VIL the output sits at the high level VOH, and for any input above VIH it sits at the low level VOL; in between it makes its transition. The output barely depends on the exact input value inside the two flat regions, which is what lets digital circuits ignore small noise. The textbook's perfect inverter has VIL = VIH = VDD/2 with a vertical transition and VOH = VDD, VOL = 0.

29-9

where

VOH = Voltage Output High

VOL = Voltage Output Low

VIL = Maximum value of VIN while still being interpreted as a logic 0

VIH = Minimum value of VIN while still being interpreted as a logic 1

then it follows that

For low input   NML = VIL−VOL

For high input   NMH† = VOH−VIH

Expansion note: where the two noise-margin formulas come from

Gate 1 drives gate 2 and noise vN is added on the wire between them, so gate 2 sees VOL+vN or VOH+vN. If gate 1 outputs a low, gate 2 still reads a 0 as long as its input stays below VIL, so the noise can rise by VIL−VOL: that is NML. If gate 1 outputs a high, gate 2 still reads a 1 as long as its input stays above VIH, so the noise can fall by VOH−VIH: that is NMH. Both margins want to be as large as possible; for a symmetric inverter they are equal.

(*) So, these four parameters define the VTC of an inverter and thus determine its noise margins, which provide a measure of the inverter's ability to tolerate variations in the input signal levels

Expansion note: how VIL and VIH are located on a real curve

A real inverter does not switch at a single point. The textbook defines VIL and VIH as the two points on the VTC where the slope is −1 V/V. Between them the gain magnitude exceeds 1 and the circuit is in its transition region; outside them the gain is below 1 and small input changes shrink at the output. The next lessons find these points for the CMOS inverter.

(*) We will need to explore the mathematics of this for a "real" inverter!

Appendix

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Identifying a logic gate from its transistor schematic

Recognize it

You are shown a PUN of PMOS devices and a PDN of NMOS devices (Ex. 1, 2, 3) and asked what gate they make.

Method

  1. Mark which transistors are PMOS (the PUN, between VDD and the output) and which are NMOS (the PDN, between the output and ground).
  2. For the PDN, write when it conducts: series is AND, parallel is OR, using the gate signals as they are. That expression is Y¯.
  3. Complement it to get the output: Y=PDNexpression¯.
  4. For the PUN, write when it conducts using the complement of each gate signal (a PMOS is on when its gate is low). That expression is Y.
  5. Use DeMorgan's law to confirm the two forms are equal, then name the function.

Checks

  • Where the PDN has series devices the PUN should have parallel ones, and vice versa (dual networks).
  • Fill the ON/OFF truth table: for every row exactly one network should conduct.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that a PMOS conducts for a low gate and writing its gate letter uncomplemented in the PUN expression.
  • Reading the PDN expression as the output instead of its complement.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1 (NAND), Ex. 2 (NOR), Ex. 3 (XNOR), pp. 29-2 to 29-4.

A2. Filling a switch-state truth table (ON and OFF for each transistor)

Recognize it

A table lists A, B, one column per transistor and the output Y (Ex. 1 and Ex. 2).

Method

  1. For each row, look at the gate signal of every transistor.
  2. NMOS: gate 1 is ON, gate 0 is OFF. PMOS: gate 0 is ON, gate 1 is OFF.
  3. Decide whether the PUN conducts (series needs all ON, parallel needs any ON) and whether the PDN conducts.
  4. PUN conducts: Y = 1. PDN conducts: Y = 0. Exactly one should conduct.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a PMOS gate of 1 as ON.
  • Calling the output 1 because a transistor is ON without checking that it is in the pull-up path.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1 and Ex. 2 tables, pp. 29-2 and 29-3.

A3. Synthesizing the PUN and PDN from a Boolean expression

Recognize it

You are given Y as an expression, such as Y=A(B+CD)¯, and asked for the transistor networks (Ex. 4).

Method

  1. PDN: take the expression inside the big bar, Y¯=A(B+CD). Product is series, sum is parallel, NMOS, true inputs on the gates.
  2. PUN: apply DeMorgan until Y has no big bar and every variable is complemented: complement each input, swap AND and OR from the inside out, complement the whole. Here Y=A¯+B¯(C¯+D¯).
  3. Draw the PUN with PMOS: sum is parallel, product is series, and feed the plain letters to the gates.
  4. Check the networks are duals (series branches in one are parallel in the other).

Checks

  • Pick two input rows (one with Y = 0, one with Y = 1) and trace which network conducts.
  • Count transistors: one PMOS and one NMOS per input literal, 8 for four inputs.

Common mistakes

  • Complementing only part of the expression, or skipping the final complement of the whole.
  • Putting the original letters, not their complements, in the PUN expression before reading it into PMOS.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 4, pp. 29-5 and 29-6.

A4. Synthesizing the PUN and PDN from a truth table

Recognize it

A truth table gives Y for every input combination (Ex. 5).

Method

  1. Add a column Y¯.
  2. Write Y as a sum of minterms (an AND of every input for each row where Y = 1), and Y¯ the same way for rows where Y = 0.
  3. PUN: use Y with PMOS and complemented gate signals: a minterm letter written plain needs a PMOS whose gate takes that letter, a complemented letter needs its complement on the gate.
  4. PDN: use Y¯ with NMOS and the letters exactly as written.
  5. Generate the complemented signals with inverters.

Checks

  • For every row exactly one network conducts.
  • The two networks need not be duals; if they are not, it is still correct.

Common mistakes

  • Using the maxterms where minterms are wanted.
  • Forgetting the inverters in the transistor count.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 5, pp. 29-7 and 29-8.

A5. Getting one network from the other with the dual

Recognize it

The notes ask "How create PDN using dual?" (p. 29-8).

Method

  1. Take the PUN expression Y (the one written in complemented-gate form).
  2. Swap AND and OR, working inside out.
  3. Complement the variables. The result is Y¯, the PDN expression.
  4. As a drawing: swap every series for parallel and every parallel for series, and replace PMOS by NMOS with the same gate signals.

Checks

  • Expanding the dual expression must give the same function as the direct synthesis.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 4, p. 29-6; Q&A on p. 29-8.

A6. Counting transistors and comparing gates

Recognize it

"How many transistors required?" (p. 29-8) and the remarks on NAND versus NOR (pp. 29-2, 29-3).

Method

  1. Count the gate signals that appear in the PUN; the PDN has the same number of NMOS.
  2. Add two transistors for each inverter that makes a complemented input.
  3. Compare speed by looking at the worst-case series stack: NAND stacks the (stronger) NMOS, NOR stacks the (weaker) PMOS.

A7. Finding noise margins from the four inverter levels

Recognize it

You are given or can read VOH, VOL, VIL and VIH and asked for NML and NMH (p. 29-9; applied in the next lesson to a CMOS inverter).

Method

  1. Low-input margin: NML=VIL−VOL.
  2. High-input margin: NMH=VOH−VIH.
  3. The worst-case margin is the smaller of the two.

Checks

  • Both margins must be positive; a negative margin means a gate cannot reliably drive another identical gate.
  • Ordering must be VOL<VIL<VIH<VOH.

Common mistakes

  • Swapping the pairs (using VIL−VIH or VOH−VIL).
  • Mixing which output level pairs with which input threshold: low output goes with VIL, high output with VIH.

Worked in these notes: p. 29-9 (definitions only).

B. Topics Covered by the Lesson 29 Notes

B1. Complementary CMOS gates (PUN and PDN)

A static CMOS gate has a PMOS pull-up network from VDD to the output and an NMOS pull-down network from the output to ground, driven by the same inputs. One network conducts for each input combination, so the output is driven hard to VDD or 0 and no DC current flows from supply to ground in steady state.

B2. Transistors as switches: series, parallel and active-low

An NMOS is a switch that closes for a high gate and a PMOS one that closes for a low gate (drawn with a bubble). Series switches form an AND of their conditions and parallel switches an OR. A PMOS gate therefore receives the complement of the signal that appears in the expression.

B3. Boolean tools: DeMorgan, SOP, minterms and maxterms, duality

DeMorgan: AB¯=A¯+B¯ and A+B¯=A¯B¯. SOP: a sum of minterms, one product per row with Y = 1. Dual: swap AND and OR (series and parallel). The PUN expression comes out in complemented variables; the PDN expression in the uncomplemented ones.

B4. Gate speed: resistance, mobility and the time constant

PMOS have higher on-resistance than NMOS of the same size because hole mobility is lower than electron mobility. The delay is set by the time constant τ=RC, so stacking PMOS in series (NOR) is slower than stacking NMOS (NAND); parallel PMOS give the best pull-up.

B5. Logic levels, the VTC and noise margins

An inverter's voltage transfer curve is described by four numbers: VOH, VOL, VIL and VIH. The noise margins NML and NMH measure how much noise a signal can carry without the next gate misreading it. The ideal inverter switches sharply at VDD/2 with VOH = VDD and VOL = 0.
