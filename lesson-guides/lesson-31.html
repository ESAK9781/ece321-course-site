Lesson 31: CMOS Inverter Dynamic Operation: Propagation Delay

31-1

ECE 321  ·  Lecture #31

CMOS Inverter Dynamic Operation: Propagation Delay

(*) Let's start with a semi-ideal input pulse

Expansion note: why the output edges are rounded and late

The input pulse is called semi-ideal because its edges are perfectly sharp. The output cannot follow that sharply: the capacitance on the output node must be charged or discharged through a transistor that supplies only a finite current, so the output moves along an exponential-like curve and passes its midpoint a short time after the input edge. The input edge is the reference, and the delay is measured to the output's midpoint.

Here, the propagation delay is measured from the midpoint of the transition of the input to when it goes through the midpoint, 12(VOL+VOH), of the output swing.

(*) Why this is too idealized is that more generically, the input of one inverter is the output of another (the previous stage)

Expansion note: why a real input is not a sharp step

In a chain of gates every input is the output of the stage before it, so every input edge is itself rounded and delayed by the same mechanism. That is why the formal definition on the next page uses the 50% points of the input and output edges, and measures transition times between the 10% and 90% points.

31-2

Definitions

DEF: Rise time (tr) -- time for input signal to rise
From 10% of Final value to 90%

DEF: Fall time (tf) -- time for input signal to fall
From 90% of Final value to 10%

Expansion note: why 10% and 90% instead of 0% and 100%

An exponential edge only approaches its final value and never quite arrives, so the 0% and 100% points are not well defined. The 10% and 90% points are sharp, repeatable markers on the curve, so rise, fall and transition times are measured between them. For a single-time-constant edge, the 10% to 90% time is about 2.2RC.

DEF: Propagation Delay times

tPHL -- time between when input transitions
and output transitions through its midpoint heading low (high to low)

tPLH -- time between when input transitions
and output transitions through its midpoint heading high (low to high)

Expansion note: reading the subscripts P, H and L

P stands for propagation, and the two letters after it give the output's direction: PHL is high to low and PLH is low to high. Both are timed from the 50% point of the input edge to the 50% point of the output edge. Because an inverter inverts, a rising input produces the falling output tPHL, and a falling input produces the rising output tPLH. The two are generally unequal.

DEF: Transition times -- output goes from 90% to 10%
(tTHL) or from 10% to 90% (tTLH)

Expansion note: transition time versus propagation delay

A transition time is how long the output edge itself takes (its steepness). A propagation delay is how late the output edge is relative to the input edge. They are different measurements on the same waveform, and a gate can have a short delay with a slow edge or the reverse.

METHOD 2: Model FET as an equivalent resistor

Expansion note: what the equivalent circuits say

While the output falls, the NMOS is replaced by a resistor RN that discharges C toward ground, so vo decays exponentially from VDD. While the output rises, the PMOS is replaced by a resistor RP that charges C from VDD, so vo climbs exponentially from 0. In both pictures the delay is read where the curve crosses VDD/2. The arrows show the direction of the current in the equivalent resistor.

(*) Let's see how this works! Recall from first-order RC circuits

Vc = vc(t), the capacitor voltage after the step†

if    IV = the initial value of vc

FV = the final value of vc

τ = RC

then

Vc = FV − (FV − IV) e−t/τ

Expansion note: the first-order step response

A capacitor charging or discharging through a resistor starts at the initial value IV and moves toward the final value FV. The gap FV − IV between where it is going and where it started shrinks exponentially with time constant τ=RC, so vc(t)=FV−(FV−IV)e−t/τ. At t=0 this equals IV, and as t→∞ it equals FV.

Expansion note: why resistance scales inversely with W/L

The channel is a slab of conducting material. Making it wider (larger W) puts more parallel paths for the current, which lowers the resistance, and making it longer (larger L) lengthens the path, which raises it. So the on-resistance is proportional to L/W. If a unit-sized transistor of shape Weff/Leff has resistance Reff, a transistor of shape (W/L) has R=Reff(Weff/Leff)/(W/L).

CINT = 2Cgd2+2Cgd1+Cdb2+Cdb1

CEXT = (Cwire+Cg4+Cg3)×Fan\_Out

CTOT = CINT+CEXT

Expansion note: where each term of the load capacitance comes from

The gate-drain capacitances Cgd1 and Cgd2 count twice because of the Miller effect: when the output falls by VDD the input rises by VDD, so the voltage across each of them changes by 2VDD and the output node sees twice the capacitance. The drain-body capacitances Cdb1 and Cdb2 have one end at a fixed voltage and count once. The wire and the gate capacitances of the driven inverter (Cg3 and Cg4) are external to the driving stage, and each driven gate adds its own pair, so they are multiplied by the fan-out. The driven inverter does not switch during the delay interval, so its input capacitance stays roughly constant.

The tPLH values agree closely, while the resistance method gives a larger tPHL and so a somewhat larger tP. The average-current formulas assume long-channel square-law behavior and underestimate delay in deep-submicron processes, where velocity saturation lowers the current. The effective resistances come from simulation of the real technology, so they capture that effect. Hand results from either method are for design insight, not precision.

Appendix

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Reading delays and transition times from a waveform

Recognize it

You are given (or must sketch) the input and output waveforms of an inverter and asked for tPHL, tPLH, tP, a rise or fall time, or a transition time.

Method

  1. Mark the three levels on each waveform: VOL, VOH and the midpoint 12(VOL+VOH), and the 10% and 90% levels of the swing VOH−VOL.
  2. For tPHL, find where the input edge crosses its midpoint and where the falling output edge crosses its midpoint; subtract the times. For tPLH, use the rising output edge.
  3. For a transition time, read the time the output edge takes between its 90% and 10% points (tTHL) or its 10% and 90% points (tTLH). For the input, the same 10% to 90% times are tr and tf.
  4. Average the two delays: tP=12(tPHL+tPLH).

Checks and variants

The output edge must be inverted relative to the input, and the delays must be positive. With a sharp input pulse (semi-ideal), the reference is simply the instant of the input step.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring a delay between 10% or 90% points instead of 50% points.
  • Mixing up PHL and PLH: the letters give the output direction, not the input's.
  • Confusing a transition time (edge duration) with a propagation delay (lateness).

Worked in these notes

Pages 31-1 and 31-2.

A2. Finding the equivalent load capacitance C

Recognize it

A question asks for the total capacitance an inverter drives, given the transistor capacitances, the wire and the fan-out.

Method

  1. Internal part: CINT=2Cgd2+2Cgd1+Cdb2+Cdb1. The gate-drain capacitances are doubled by the Miller effect; the drain-body capacitances count once.
  2. External part per driven gate: Cwire+Cg4+Cg3.
  3. Multiply the external part by the fan-out: CEXT=(Cwire+Cg4+Cg3)×Fan\_Out.
  4. Add: CTOT=CINT+CEXT, and use it as C in either delay method.

Checks and variants

If the load is not another inverter, replace Cg3 and Cg4 with the input capacitance of whatever is driven. Wire capacitance often dominates in modern processes.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the factor 2 on the gate-drain capacitances.
  • Not multiplying the external capacitance by the fan-out.

Worked in these notes

Page 31-10.

A3. Propagation delay by the average-current method

Recognize it

You know VDD, Vtn, Vtp, the transconductance parameters k′n, k′p, the shapes (W/L)n, (W/L)p, and the load C, and want tPHL, tPLH and tP from the device equations.

Method

  1. Compute Kn=k′n(W/L)n and Kp=k′p(W/L)p.
  2. Compute an=274−3VtnVDD+(VtnVDD)2 and ap the same way with |Vtp|.
  3. tPHL=anCKnVDD and tPLH=apCKpVDD.
  4. tP=12(tPHL+tPLH).

Checks and variants

an and ap should land between about 1 and 3. If Kn=Kp and Vtn=−Vtp the two delays are equal. To see the effect of a different supply, recompute a and the delays with the new VDD.

Common mistakes

  • Using k′ instead of K=k′(W/L).
  • Putting a signed Vtp into ap instead of its magnitude.
  • Mixing units: μA/V^2 with fF gives ps only if converted consistently.

Worked in these notes

Derivation on pages 31-3 to 31-7; numbers on page 31-11.

A4. Propagation delay by the equivalent-resistance method

Recognize it

You are given effective resistances Reff,N, Reff,P measured at some reference shape Weff/Leff, plus the actual shapes and C.

Method

  1. Scale the resistances: RN=Reff,NWeff/Leff(W/L)n and RP=Reff,PWeff/Leff(W/L)p.
  2. tPHL=0.693RNC and tPLH=0.693RPC.
  3. tP=12(tPHL+tPLH).

Checks and variants

Typical resistances are tens of ohms to several kilohms, so with femtofarad loads the delays come out in picoseconds. A wider transistor has a lower resistance. The effective resistances belong to one supply voltage, so this method cannot predict a change in VDD.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing by the reference shape instead of the actual shape (inverting the scaling).
  • Using the same R for both edges.

Worked in these notes

Pages 31-8 to 31-10 (theory) and 31-12 (numbers).

A5. Time for an RC step response to reach a fraction of its final value

Recognize it

A capacitor charges or discharges through a resistor, and you need the time to reach the half-way point (or another fraction).

Method

  1. Identify IV, FV and τ=RC.
  2. Write vc(t)=FV−(FV−IV)e−t/τ.
  3. Set vc equal to the target value and solve: e−t/τ=FV−vcFV−IV, so t=−τlnFV−vcFV−IV.
  4. For the half-way point the ratio is 12 and t=τln2=0.693τ.

Checks and variants

At t=τ the gap has shrunk to 37% of its start. The 10% to 90% time of a single-time-constant edge is about 2.2τ.

Common mistakes

  • Getting the sign of the exponent wrong, which makes the log of a number larger than 1.
  • Using IV when the problem gives the gap, or the reverse.

Worked in these notes

Pages 31-8 and 31-9.

A6. Comparing methods and the effect of the supply voltage

Recognize it

You are asked to compare the two methods, or to find by what factor tP changes when VDD is lowered.

Method

  1. Compute tP with both methods at the original supply and tabulate tPHL, tPLH, tP.
  2. For the new supply, use only the average-current method, since the effective resistances were measured at the old supply: recompute a (it grows as VDD falls toward Vt) and the delays.
  3. Take the ratio tP,new/tP,old using the average-current tP values from both supplies, so the comparison uses the same method.

Checks and variants

Lower VDD always lengthens the delay: KnVDD falls and a rises. The trade-off is lower dynamic power.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing a resistance-method tP by an average-current tP.
  • Keeping a at its old value.

Worked in these notes

Pages 31-11 and 31-12 (Ex. 17-2).
