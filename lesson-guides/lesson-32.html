Lesson 32: Sizing Transistors in CMOS Gates and Power Dissipation

32-1

Review of Propagation Delay

Expansion note: how to read the delay figure

The 10%, 50% and 90% po∫s are measured between VOL and VOH. The rise and fall times tr and tf are the 10% to 90% times of the input; tTHL and tTLH are the same times on the output. The propagation delays tPHL and tPLH run from the 50% po∫ of the input edge to the 50% po∫ of the output edge. A falling output (high to low, HL) is produced by a rising input, and a rising output (LH) by a falling input.

Average Current

an=274−3(VtnVDD)+(VtnVDD)2

ap=274−3|VtpVDD|+|VtpVDD|2

tPHL=anCKnVDD     tPLH=apCKpVDD

Equivalent Resistance

RN=Reff,N(Weff/Leff)(W/L)n

RP=Reff,P(Weff/Leff)(W/L)p

tPHL=0.693RNC

tPLH=0.693RPC

Expansion note: the two ways to get the delay and why both are here

Method 1 (average current) divides the charge CVDD/2 moved in a half swing by the average current of the transistor, which gives tPHL=anC/(KnVDD); the factor an collects the saturation and triode portions of the swing. Method 2 treats the on-transistor as a resistor RN charging or discharging C in a single time constant, and the 50% po∫ of an exponential is reached at 0.693RC. Both give delays that are proportional to C and inversely proportional to the transistor width, which is why sizing matters in the rest of this lesson.

tP= 12 tPHL + tPLH

Expansion note: why t_P is the average of the two delays

An inverter has two propagation delays, one for each output direction. The single number tP quoted for a gate is their average, tP=12(tPHL+tPLH). For a matched inverter the two are equal and tP=tPHL=tPLH.

32-2

Transistor Sizing: the logical inverter

RN≈1μnCox(WL)n[VGS−Vtn]

Expansion note: why the on-resistance falls as W/L grows

In the triode region near vDS=0 the channel behaves as a resistor of value 1/[μnCox(W/L)nVOV]. A wider channel (larger W) is like paralleling resistors, and a longer channel (larger L) is like putting them in series. So R∝L/W: widening a device lowers R and speeds the delay, but it also enlarges the gate and drain capacitance that R must charge.

  1. 1.
    Choose L to be the minimum length permitted by the technology (process).
    Expansion note: why every channel uses the minimum length

    Area is W×L per device and a shorter channel conducts more current for the same width, so using the smallest drawn length that the process allows gives the smallest, fastest device. All sizing is then done with the width W alone.

  2. 2.
    If we wish to minimize inverter area† above all else and fit contacts† to access, choose 1 < (W/L)n < 1.5.
    Expansion note: the smallest allowed widths and what newer processes do

    With minimum area as the only goal, (W/L)n is taken between 1 and 1.5, which also gives the least driving current. In more recent technologies, values closer to 2 and above are common (the examples in this lesson use (W/L)n=2).

  3. 3.
    In order to match Kn and Kp to optimize noise margins† and tP_LH†, we would like (W/L)p ≈ μnμp (W/L)n but to avert significant increases in area and capacitance C† a compromise is (W/L)p ≈ 2 (W/L)n
    Expansion note: why matching the transistors is not always worth it

    Electrons are more mobile than holes, so kn′=μnCox has historically been 3 to 5 times kp′. Matching Kp=Kn would need a PMOS 3 to 5 times wider, which costs area and adds capacitance, so the delay can end up higher than with a smaller PMOS. A PMOS twice as wide as the NMOS is the usual compromise. In emerging processes (the 28 nm node) kn′ and kp′ are closer, and (W/L)p=(W/L)n is more common.

  4. 4.
    Revisit (W/L)n to reduce tP† and allow higher speed†
    Expansion note: the trade between speed and area when scaling W/L

    Write the load as C=C∫+Cext. Scaling both (W/L)n and (W/L)p by a factor S gives C=SC∫0+Cext and Req=Req0/S, so tP=0.69Req0C∫0+0.69Req0SCext. Scaling does not change the part of the delay caused by the inverter's own capacitance; it only shrinks the part caused by wiring and the gates it drives, at the price of S times the area.

32-3

Transistor Sizing in CMOS Logic Gates

DEF. The ratios for an optimized inverter† design are defined as

n = (W/L)n,     p = (W/L)p

So, if we are told n = 2† and p = 4†. For a foundry using L = 65 nm then

Expansion note: how the widths follow from n, p and L

The width is the ratio times the length: W=(W/L)L. With L=65nm, Wn=2×65=130nm and Wp=4×65=260nm. These are the values used in Examples 3 and 4 below (the 1040 nm of Example 4 is 16 times 65 nm).

(*) We desire for more complex logic gates that the PUN provide a capacitor charging current similar to the inverter

→ worst-case path has similar resistance

and the PDN provide a capacitor discharging current similar to the inverter

→ worst-case path has similar resistance

Expansion note: what the worst case means for a gate

A gate's delay is set by its weakest conducting path. The worst case is the input combination that leaves the fewest transistors conducting, or the most in series. Size each device so that this weakest path matches the inverter's single transistor and every other input combination is at least as fast.

(*) The PUN resistances will add (4 in series) so widen each PMOS to make them the same as the inverter   (W/L)p,NOR = 4 (W/L)p,inv. So each should be 4p

Expansion note: why four series PMOS each need 4p

Series resistances add, so four equal PMOS in series have four times the resistance of one. To get back to the resistance of the inverter's single PMOS, every one of them must have one quarter of that resistance, that is four times the width: (W/L)p,NOR=4(W/L)p,inv=4p. Equivalently, the series connection of four devices of ratio 4p has (W/L)eq=4p/4=p.

(*) Since the longest series path through the PDN is 1, to make those resistances match the inverter   (W/L)n,NOR = (W/L)n,inv So each should be n

Expansion note: why the NOR PDN needs no widening

In the PDN the worst case is when only one NMOS is on (one input high, the others low), so the discharge path is a single transistor. Any other input combination puts more transistors in parallel and discharges faster. Each NMOS can therefore have the inverter ratio n.

Expansion note: why the sized NAND is smaller than the sized NOR

Both gates need a factor of 4 on the four-transistor series stack, but the NAND stacks NMOS (ratio 4n) while the NOR stacks PMOS (ratio 4p). Because p is usually two to three times n, the NOR needs much more area, which is why NAND gates are preferred for implementing logic in CMOS.

Power Dissipation

Q. What happens when we switch a capacitively loaded† CMOS inverter?

  • Static dissipation is zero (≈ 0)
  • Dynamic dissipation is nonzero: a spike at each transition†
Expansion note: why there are spikes only at the transitions

When the input is steady, one of the two transistors is off, so no current flows from VDD to ground and there is no static dissipation. While the input switches, the output capacitor must be charged or discharged through an on-transistor, and current flows through that resistance for a short time. That is the dynamic dissipation, drawn as a spike at every edge of vIN.

Expansion note: where the half goes on each edge

Charging: the supply delivers CVDD2, half is stored on the capacitor and half is lost as heat in the on-resistance of the PMOS (whatever that resistance is). Discharging: the supply delivers nothing, and the 12CVDD2 stored on the capacitor is lost as heat in the on-resistance of the NMOS.

so

= frac12CVDD2+frac12CVDD2T

Expansion note: why P = f C V_DD squared does not depend on the transistor resistance

One full cycle (one turn-on and one turn-off) dissipates 12CVDD2+12CVDD2=CVDD2, independent of the on-resistances. With f=1/T cycles per second, Pave=fCVDD2. So the dynamic power falls with a smaller C, a lower f, and especially a lower supply, because it varies with VDD2.

ex. 5   A CMOS logic chip is clocked at 500† MHz. Each gate sees 3† fF/gate. The supply is 1.0† V and there are 1,000,000† gates. Estimate Pdiss

Pdiss = NfCVDD2=(106)(500×106)(3×10−15)(1.0)2=1.5W†

Expansion note: how to evaluate the chip power

Convert every quantity to base units first: 500 MHz = 5×108 Hz, 3 fF = 3×10−15 F. The power per gate is fCVDD2=(5×108)(3×10−15)(1)2=1.5μW, and a million gates dissipate N times that, 1.5 W.

Probably over†-estimated since each gate not switching† each clock cycle. Need to apply an activity† factor out front

Expansion note: the activity factor

fCVDD2 assumes the gate output makes a full charge and discharge every clock cycle. Most gates switch in only a fraction of the cycles, so the estimate is multiplied by an activity factor α<1: P=αNfCVDD2. With α=0.1 the example would give 0.15 W.

Expansion note: why the supply falls with the channel length

The electric field across the gate oxide is about VDD/tox and along the channel about VDD/L. Appendix

A. Problem Types in This Notetaker and How to Solve Them

A1. Choosing the inverter ratios (n, p) and the transistor widths

Recognize it

You are told the process (channel length L) and the inverter ratios n and p, or asked to pick them, and must give Wn and Wp (pp. 32-2 and 32-3).

Method

  1. Set L to the minimum channel length of the process.
  2. Choose n=(W/L)n: 1 to 1.5 for minimum area, about 2 or more in recent technologies.
  3. Choose p=(W/L)p: p=μnn/μp for a matched inverter (tPHL=tPLH, symmetric noise margins), p=2n as the usual compromise, p=n for minimum area.
  4. Convert to widths with W=(W/L)L.

Checks

  • p≥n in nearly every process, because holes are less mobile than electrons.
  • Widths come out as multiples of L.

Common mistakes

  • Giving the ratio when the question asks for the width in nm.
  • Choosing p=μnn/μp without noticing the area and capacitance penalty.

Worked in these notes: pp. 32-2 and 32-3.

A2. Sizing a uniform gate (NOR or NAND) from its longest series path

Recognize it

A gate in which every input drives one PMOS and one NMOS and all transistors in a network are alike; the question gives the inverter ratios and asks for the transistor sizes (Ex. 1 and Ex. 2).

Method

  1. For the PUN, find the longest path of series PMOS (the worst case for charging). Call its length kp.
  2. For the PDN, find the longest series path of NMOS. Call its length kn.
  3. Series resistances add, so make each PMOS on that path kp times the inverter PMOS: (W/L)p=kpp. Likewise (W/L)n=knn.
  4. Parallel-only networks (one device in the worst path) keep the inverter ratio.
  5. Convert to widths with W=(W/L)L.

Checks

  • The worst path's equivalent ratio is (W/L)eq=(W/L)device/k and should equal p (PUN) or n (PDN).
  • The gate whose stack is made of PMOS (NOR) comes out larger than the NAND, since p>n.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing the parallel network for its number of devices; parallel devices only help, so the worst case is one device on.
  • Applying the factor to the wrong network (PUN instead of PDN).

Worked in these notes: Ex. 1 (NOR) and Ex. 2 (NAND), pp. 32-4 and 32-5.

A3. Sizing a mixed series-parallel gate path by path

Recognize it

A complex gate (Ex. 3 and Ex. 4) whose PUN and PDN contain both series and parallel parts, so different transistors lie on paths of different lengths.

Method

  1. List every conducting path from the supply (or ground) to the output and count the devices on each.
  2. Take the longest path of the network. Each device on it gets k times the inverter ratio (kp for PMOS, kn for NMOS).
  3. For a device that is not on the longest path, size it so that every path through it has the same total resistance as the longest. A single device in parallel with a string of m devices of the longest-path width w0 has width w0/m when it replaces the whole string; two devices in series that are replaced by one get half the width.
  4. A device alone in its path (a one-device path) can take the inverter ratio.
  5. Round widths to the nearest 10 nm if the problem asks, and convert with W=(W/L)L.

Checks

  • Add the resistances (∝1/(W/L)) along each path: all paths from the PUN should come to 1/p or less, all PDN paths to 1/n or less.

Variants

  • If the problem gives a dual network, derive it first (A4) and then repeat the method on the new network.

Common mistakes

  • Giving every device in the PUN the same width after finding only the longest path.
  • Forgetting that a shared device (like Ē in Ex. 4) belongs to every path.

Worked in these notes: Ex. 3 (pp. 32-6, 32-7) and Ex. 4 (pp. 32-8, 32-9).

A4. Finding the logic function and the dual network from a transistor network

Recognize it

"What logical expression is realized?" or "How create the dual PDN?" (Ex. 3 and Ex. 4).

Method

  1. Dual: swap series and parallel, change PMOS to NMOS (or the reverse), and keep each gate label.
  2. Write when the PDN conducts: series is AND, parallel is OR, using the gate signals as labeled.
  3. The output is the complement of that expression. Apply DeMorgan to push the bars onto single inputs.
  4. Check with the PUN: PMOS conduct for low gates, so each PMOS contributes the complement of its gate label.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that the PDN gives the complement of Y.
  • Reading a gate labeled with a bar as the plain input.

Worked in these notes: pp. 32-7 and 32-9.

A5. Dynamic power and energy per transition

Recognize it

Given a clock frequency, load capacitance, supply and number of gates, estimate the power (Ex. 5), or find the energy for one charge or discharge.

Method

  1. Per cycle the supply delivers EDD=VDDQ=CVDD2 while charging (Q=CVDD).
  2. The capacitor stores 12CVDD2, so 12CVDD2 is lost in the PMOS; discharging loses the stored 12CVDD2 in the NMOS.
  3. Per gate, P=fCVDD2. For N gates, P=NfCVDD2, multiplied by an activity factor α if not every gate switches each cycle.
  4. Convert units before multiplying (MHz, fF).

Checks

  • The result is an upper bound when gates are idle part of the time.
  • Halving VDD quarters the power.

Common mistakes

  • Using 12CV2 per cycle (that is one edge only).
  • Leaving the supply voltage unsquared.

Worked in these notes: pp. 32-9 to 32-11.

A6. Propagation delay from average current or equivalent resistance

Recognize it

Find tPHL, tPLH and tP for an inverter (review, p. 32-1).

Method

  1. Method 1: compute an and ap from Vt/VDD, then tPHL=anC/(KnVDD) and tPLH=apC/(KpVDD).
  2. Method 2: find RN and RP from the equivalent-resistance relation, then tPHL=0.693RNC and tPLH=0.693RPC.
  3. Average: tP=12(tPHL+tPLH).

Common mistakes

  • Using |Vtp| without absolute value in ap.
  • Quoting only one of the two delays as tP.

Worked in these notes: p. 32-1.
