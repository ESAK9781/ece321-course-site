Problem 4.2 Lesson 5 · p. 230 · §4.1

Ideal-diode states in resistor circuits with positive and negative supplies.

Problem statement

circuit rule

Check a proposed diode state against its polarity and current direction. Then verify KVL, KCL, and the indicated reference directions. An ideal diode is modeled as a short only while forward conducting; when reverse biased it is an open circuit.

Expansion note: why check the state instead of assuming it?

The ideal-diode model is piecewise: its voltage-current constraint depends on whether the device is on or off. A consistent assumed state must satisfy both the circuit equations and the corresponding diode condition.

Reading · ideal-diode states and circuit equivalents Quoted and summarized · pp. 175–179
Quoted · p. 175

Quoted · p. 175

Summarized · pp. 176–179

For each possible diode state, replace it with its ideal equivalent and solve the resulting linear circuit. Check that the solved voltage/current agrees with the assumed forward or reverse condition. The chapter uses this process to analyze rectifier and limiter circuits.

Expansion note: how a node gets its voltage when current is zero

An open branch carries no current. If the node still has a resistor path to a fixed rail, zero current means zero resistor voltage drop, so the node settles at that rail's potential.

Compare source levels through their diode orientations, propose a consistent on/off combination, and then check each diode against the solved node voltage. The sources do not simply average together; a forward-biased path may establish the node and turn another path off.

Expansion note: why the higher source may dominate

A diode permits current in one direction. When one source drives a shared node high enough, the node can make a lower-voltage branch reverse biased; that branch then stops contributing current in the ideal model.

Reading · the ideal model in networks Summarized · pp. 176–179
Summarized · pp. 176–179

For networks with several diodes, analyze a candidate set of conducting and nonconducting branches. Replace on devices by shorts and off devices by opens, solve for node voltages and currents, then reject any candidate whose device polarities contradict the assumed state. The book's ideal-diode examples use this approach to explain rectification and limiting.

Expansion note: a node voltage is a circuit-wide result

Each connected branch constrains the node through its source, diode direction, and resistance. The final node value must satisfy all branch conditions at once.

Problem 4.4 Lesson 5 · pp. 230–231 · §4.1

Waveforms through ideal-diode series, opposing, and shunt networks.

Problem statement

In each of the ideal-diode circuits shown in Fig. P4.4, vi is a 1-kHz, 5-V peak sine wave. Sketch the waveform resulting at vo. What are its positive and negative peak values?

Assigned parts: (c), (e), (h). Syllabus notation: (MS).

Similar to 4.4(c) opposing series diodes, new signal and resistor

Similar problem (new numbers)

For the topology in Fig. P4.4(c), let vi be a 2-kHz, 4-V peak sine wave and replace the 1-kΩ resistor with 2 kΩ. Using ideal diodes, describe vo(t) and give its positive and negative peaks.

Step · test both input polarities

The two series diodes face opposite directions, so one of them blocks for either input polarity. The output node remains connected to ground through the resistor, with no conducting input path. Thus the output is zero throughout the cycle.

vo(t)=0V,vo,max=0V,vo,min=0V
ideal-diode model

Similar to 4.4(e) opposed parallel paths, new signal and resistor

Similar problem (new numbers)

For the topology in Fig. P4.4(e), let vi be a 2-kHz, 4-V peak sine wave and replace the 1-kΩ resistor with 2 kΩ. Using ideal diodes, describe vo(t) and give its positive and negative peaks.

Step · follow the conducting direction

The antiparallel diode pair provides a conducting path for either sign of the input. For a positive half-cycle, one diode conducts; for a negative half-cycle, the other conducts. An ideal conducting diode has zero drop, so the output tracks the input for both halves.

vo(t)=vi(t),vo,max=+4V,vo,min=−4V
ideal-diode model

The resistor changes the current drawn but does not change the ideal short's voltage transfer.

Step · check positive and negative excursions

Each polarity forward-biases one of the two shunt diodes to ground. The conducting ideal diode clamps the output node to ground in that half-cycle; the opposite diode is off. The output is therefore held at ground for both polarities.

vo(t)=0V,vo,max=0V,vo,min=0V
ideal-diode model

The 2-kΩ resistor limits current; it does not move the ideal clamp voltage from ground.

Analyze the positive and negative half-cycles separately. Determine which diode paths are forward-biased, replace them with shorts, and replace reverse-biased paths with opens. Translate the resulting circuit behavior into a piecewise waveform and then identify its extreme values.

Expansion note: waveforms are piecewise circuits

The diode states can change as the input crosses zero or a clamp threshold. Each interval has its own equivalent circuit; together these intervals define the full output waveform.

Reading · half-wave behavior and ideal equivalents Summarized · pp. 176–179
Summarized · pp. 176–179

In the ideal rectifier example, the diode conducts during the input polarity that forward-biases it and blocks during the opposite polarity. In the conducting interval the output follows the input; in the blocking interval the load establishes the output through its connection to the reference node. Reversing diode orientation reverses which interval conducts.

Summarized · pp. 178–179

The limiter example uses a diode to constrain a node when the input attempts to exceed a reference. Its piecewise analysis likewise replaces the diode by an open or short in each operating interval.

Expansion note: peak values and sign

For a periodic input, “positive peak” is the greatest output value and “negative peak” is the least output value. Keep the algebraic sign when reporting the negative extreme.

Expansion note: why iteration converges quickly here

The resistor relation lowers current when the trial diode voltage rises, while the exponential relation adjusts voltage in response to the current. For the textbook's one-resistor/one-diode example, a few alternating updates bring the estimates close together.

Expansion note: operating point

The operating point, or Q point, is the pair of dc values (ID,VD) that satisfies both the diode characteristic and the surrounding circuit equation.

Reading · rapid diode models Quoted and summarized · pp. 193–196

The constant-voltage-drop model replaces a conducting silicon diode with a fixed voltage source and a nonconducting diode with an open circuit. The exponential model retains the device's nonlinear current-voltage relation and is more precise. The ideal model neglects forward drop when a rapid, rough estimate or an on/off test is sufficient.

Reading · reverse breakdown and the zener model Quoted and summarized · pp. 196–197
Quoted · p. 197

“The incremental resistance of the zener, rz, relates changes in current, ΔIZ, to the resulting change in reverse voltage, ΔVZ=rzΔIZ.”

Quoted · p. 197

“The almost-linear i-v characteristic of the zener diode suggests it can be modeled” by a voltage intercept and a resistance; the book gives VZ=VZ0+rzIZ as Eq. (4.8).

Summarized · pp. 196–197

A zener is intended to operate in reverse breakdown, where its voltage changes relatively little as current changes. External circuitry must limit current so that the device's power rating is respected. The linear model is local to the neighborhood of its rated operating point.

Expansion note: reconstructing the model intercept

The rated voltage-current pair lies on the model's straight line. Subtracting the resistor-like increment rzIZT from the rated voltage gives the line's voltage-axis intercept VZ0.
