Lesson 1 Intrinsic semiconductors

Lesson 1 · p. 171 Problem 3.1

Quoted · p. 171

Find values of the intrinsic carrier concentration ni for silicon at −55°C, 0°C, 20°C, 75°C, and 125°C. At each temperature, what fraction of the atoms is ionized? Recall that a silicon crystal has approximately 5×1022 atoms/cm^3.

Similar to 3.1 new temperatures · silicon

Similar problem (new values)

For silicon, use B=7.3×1015cm^{-3}K^{-3/2}, Eg=1.12eV, and k=8.62×10−5eV/K. Find ni at −30°C, 10°C, 40°C, 85°C, and 105°C. For each temperature, find the fraction of silicon atoms represented by ni, using 5×1022 atoms/cm^3.

Step 1 · convert temperature

Use T=t°C+273.15 so the exponential law uses kelvins.

Step 2 · intrinsic concentration

From Eq. (3.2), ni=BT3/2e−Eg/(2kT). Substitute the constants and each converted temperature; the Python record worked/p3_1.py prints the unrounded values and fractions.

Step 3 · fraction

Compare the carrier concentration with the atomic density: f=ni/(5×1022cm^{-3}). The units cancel, leaving a dimensionless fraction.

Results (similar problem)

The evaluation gives:

Temperatureni (cm^−3)Fraction f
-30 degrees C6.896×1071.379×10−15
10 degrees C3.776×1097.552×10−14
40 degrees C3.956×10107.911×10−13
85 degrees C6.557×10111.311×10−11
105 degrees C1.857×10123.713×10−11
The saved output preserves full precision and shows the strong temperature dependence from the exponential term.

Formula toolkit

Eq. (3.2) ni=BT3/2e−Eg/(2kT); use T in K, Eg in eV, and k in eV/K.

counting relation f=ni/NSi, where the book gives NSi≈5×1022 atoms/cm^3 (p. 140). This counts thermally generated electron–hole pairs per silicon atom.

units T=t°C+273.15 K.

Reading · intrinsic silicon, pp. 138–140

Quoted · p. 139 “Thermal generation results in free electrons and holes in equal numbers and hence equal concentrations.” In equilibrium, n=p=ni (Eq. 3.1).

Quoted · p. 139 The textbook defines Eg as the minimum energy required to break a covalent bond and generate an electron–hole pair. Equation (3.2) gives the temperature law; its exponential factor is why temperature changes matter greatly.

Summarized · pp. 138–140 A broken covalent bond creates a mobile electron and a hole. At room temperature the example gives ni=1.5×1010 cm^−3, against approximately 5×1022 silicon atoms/cm^3.

Expansion note: Why does the atom fraction use ni once? Each thermally generated pair contributes one free electron and one hole, and each event begins with one broken bond.

Calculate the value of ni for gallium arsenide (GaAs) at T=300K. The constant B=3.56×1014cm^{-3}K^{-3/2} and the bandgap voltage Eg=1.42eV. Compare with that of silicon at the same temperature.

Similar to 3.2 hypothetical material · changed constants

Similar problem (new values)

A hypothetical semiconductor has B=1.8×1015cm^{-3}K^{-3/2} and Eg=0.78eV. At T=315K, calculate its ni and compare it with silicon at the same temperature. For silicon, use B=7.3×1015 and Eg=1.12eV; use k=8.62×10−5eV/K for both.

Step 1 · evaluate each material

Apply Eq. (3.2) separately, keeping each material’s own B and Eg: ni=BT3/2e−Eg/(2kT).

Step 2 · compare

Use the ratio ni,X/ni,Si after evaluating both concentrations. The ratio is unitless. See worked/p3_2.py for the recomputed results.

Results (similar problem)

At 315 K, the hypothetical material gives ni=5.820×1012 cm^−3, while silicon gives 4.508×1010 cm^−3; the ratio is ni,X/ni,Si=129.1. The lower bandgap dominates through the exponential factor.

Reading · intrinsic semiconductors, pp. 139–140

Quoted · p. 139 For intrinsic material in thermal equilibrium, electrons and holes have equal concentration; that common value is ni.

Summarized · p. 139 Equation (3.2) ties ni to three material/temperature inputs: a fitted material constant, the bandgap energy, and absolute temperature. The temperature is shared in a comparison, but the material constants are not.

Summarized · p. 140 Example 3.1 substitutes silicon’s material data into Eq. (3.2); the surrounding text emphasizes the enormous difference between intrinsic carrier density and atomic density.

Expansion note: Why is the temperature shared? A fair material comparison evaluates both materials at the same operating condition, while preserving each material’s own parameters.

Lesson 2 Doping and current flow

Lesson 2 · p. 171 Problem 3.4

Quoted · p. 171

For a p-type silicon in which the dopant concentration NA=2×1018/cm^3, find the hole and electron concentrations at T=300K.

Similar to 3.4 p-type silicon · changed doping

Similar problem (new values)

A p-type silicon sample has NA=6×1017cm^{-3} at T=310K. If ni=2.0×1010cm^{-3} at that temperature, find its hole and electron concentrations. Assume complete ionization and thermal equilibrium.

Step 1 · majority carriers

For p-type silicon with NA≫ni, Eq. (3.6) gives pp≈NA.

Step 2 · minority carriers

Use the mass-action relation, Eq. (3.3): ppnp=ni2, so np=ni2/pp=(2.0×1010)2/(6.0×1017)=6.67×102 cm^−3. Substitution is recorded in worked/p3_4.py.

Step 3 · check

Verify ppnp=ni2 and confirm the hole concentration is the majority concentration.

Results (similar problem)

The saved calculation gives pp≈6.0×1017cm^{-3} and np≈6.67×102cm^{-3}.

Formula toolkit

Eq. (3.6) pp≈NA for p-type material with acceptors fully ionized and NA≫ni.

Eq. (3.3) ppnp=ni2.

from Eq. (3.3) np=ni2/pp.

units Carrier concentration is cm^−3; the product in Eq. (3.3) is cm^−6.

Reading · doped semiconductors, pp. 141–143

Quoted · p. 141 “To increase the concentration of holes, p, silicon is doped with an element having a valence of 3, such as boron”; the material is then p type.

Quoted · p. 142 The acceptor doping concentration sets the majority-hole concentration (Eq. 3.6). The minority-electron concentration follows from Eq. (3.7), which uses the equilibrium product relation from Eq. (3.3).

Summarized · p. 143 Doping changes which carrier is the majority, but the material remains electrically neutral: mobile majority charge is balanced by bound impurity charge.

Expansion note: Why can a heavily doped sample still contain minority carriers? Thermal generation continues; the equilibrium product constrains the minority concentration as the majority concentration rises.

Lesson 2 · p. 171 Problem 3.5

Quoted · p. 171

For a silicon crystal doped with phosphorus, what must ND be if at T=300K the hole concentration drops below the intrinsic level by a factor of 108?

Similar to 3.5 n-type silicon · changed factor and temperature

Similar problem (new values)

For phosphorus-doped silicon at T=315K, how large must ND be for the equilibrium hole concentration to be 106 times smaller than ni? Use Eq. (3.2) with silicon constants B=7.3×1015cm^{-3}K^{-3/2}, Eg=1.12eV, and k=8.62×10−5eV/K.

Step 1 · find ni

Use Eq. (3.2) at 315 K. The calculation is saved in worked/p3_5.py.

Step 2 · translate the target ratio

The target is pn=ni/106. For n-type material, Eqs. (3.3) through (3.5) give nn≈ND and nnpn=ni2, hence ND≈ni2/pn=106ni.

Step 3 · units and approximation

The result has units cm^−3. Check that ND≫ni, consistent with the n-type approximation used.

Results (similar problem)

At 315 K, ni=4.508×1010 cm^−3, so the target is pn=4.508×104 cm^−3. Then ND=ni2/pn=4.508×1016 cm^−3, which is 106ni and satisfies ND≫ni.

Formula toolkit

Eq. (3.4) nn≈ND for n-type material when donors are fully ionized and ND≫ni.

Eq. (3.3) nnpn=ni2.

Eq. (3.5) pn≈ni2/ND.

from Eq. (3.3) ND≈ni2/pn.

target ratio “pn is F times below ni” means pn=ni/F; therefore ND≈Fni.

Reading · n-type silicon, pp. 141–143

Summarized · p. 141 A pentavalent donor such as phosphorus contributes a mobile electron. When the donor concentration greatly exceeds ni, electrons are the majority carriers and nn is approximately ND.

Quoted · pp. 142–143 The textbook applies the equilibrium product relation to doped material (Eqs. 3.3–3.5) to determine the minority holes.

Expansion note: What does “below by a factor” mean? A concentration smaller by a factor F equals the reference concentration divided by F.

Lesson 2 · p. 172 Problem 3.8

Quoted · p. 172

Contrast the electron and hole drift velocities through a 10-μm layer of intrinsic silicon across which a voltage of 3 V is imposed. Let μn=1350cm^2/(V\cdot s) and μp=480cm^2/(V\cdot s).

Similar to 3.8 uniform silicon layer · changed values

Similar problem (new values)

Compare the electron and hole drift velocities in intrinsic silicon across a 16-μm layer with 2.4 V across it. Use μn=1280cm^2/(V\cdot s) and μp=460cm^2/(V\cdot s).

Step 1 · electric field

For a uniform layer, E=V/L. Convert 16μm to cm before dividing.

Step 2 · signed drift velocities

From Eqs. (3.8)–(3.9), vp=μpE and vn=−μnE. The saved calculation in worked/p3_8.py reports both signed values and magnitudes.

Step 3 · compare

Both velocities have units cm/s; the negative electron sign means motion opposite the field. Their magnitude ratio is |vn|/|vp|=μn/μp.

Results (similar problem)

Here E=2.4/(16×10−4)=1.50×103 V/cm. Thus vn=−1.92×106 cm/s and vp=6.90×105 cm/s; |vn|/|vp|=2.783.

Formula toolkit

Eq. (3.8) vp=μpE.

Eq. (3.9) vn=−μnE.

circuit rule Uniform field magnitude E=V/L.

comparison |vn|/|vp|=μn/μp in the same field.

Reading · carrier drift, pp. 144–145

Quoted · p. 144 Holes accelerate in the direction of the electric field; free electrons accelerate in the opposite direction.

Quoted · p. 144 The drift-velocity relations are vp,drift=μpE and vn,drift=−μnE (Eqs. 3.8–3.9). Mobility measures how readily a carrier moves in response to the field.

Summarized · pp. 144–145 For intrinsic silicon at room temperature, the chapter gives electron and hole mobilities of 1350 and 480 cm^2/(V·s), respectively. Both carrier types produce conventional drift current in the field direction even though their particle motions differ.

Expansion note: Why is the electron velocity negative? The sign records direction relative to the chosen positive field direction; it does not mean the speed is negative.

Lesson 2 · p. 172 Problem 3.9

Quoted · p. 172

Find the length of a silicon bar having a 5-μm×4-μm cross section and having free-electron and hole densities of 104/cm^3 and 1016/cm^3, respectively, so that 0.2 mA current flows when 1 V is applied end-to-end. Use μn=1200cm^2/(V\cdot s) and μp=500cm^2/(V\cdot s).

Similar to 3.9 conducting silicon bar · changed values

Similar problem (new values)

Find the length of a silicon bar with a 6-μm×3-μm cross section, electron density 2×104/cm^3, and hole density 8×1015/cm^3, if 0.30 mA flows when 1.5 V is applied end-to-end. Use μn=1100cm^2/(V\cdot s) and μp=450cm^2/(V\cdot s).

Step 1 · conductivity

From Eqs. (3.13)–(3.15), sigma=q(nμn+pμp) and rho=1/sigma. Use q=1.602×10−19 C.

Step 2 · resistance and geometry

Ohm’s law gives R=V/I; Eq. (3.17) gives R=rhoL/A. Rearranging, L=RA/rho. Convert the cross-sectional area from μm2 to cm^2.

Step 3 · compute and check

The Python record worked/p3_9.py computes the length and checks that rhoL/A=V/I.

Results (similar problem)

The inputs give sigma=0.57672 S/cm and rho=1.73394 ohm cm. Then R=1.5/0.30mA=5.00 kohm and L=RA/rho=5.190×10−4 cm =5.190 micrometers. Recomputing rho L/A gives 5.00 kohm, recovering 0.30 mA.

Eqs. (3.13)–(3.15) sigma=q(nμn+pμp) and rho=1/sigma.

Eq. (3.17) R=rhoL/A.

from Eq. (3.17) L=RA/rho.

circuit rule R=V/I.

units 1μm=10−4 cm; hence 1μm2=10−8 cm^2.

Reading · drift current and resistivity, pp. 145–146

Summarized · p. 145 Electron and hole drift-current densities add. The chapter writes the result in terms of total carrier density, mobility, charge magnitude, and electric field, then defines resistivity as the reciprocal conductivity (Eqs. 3.13–3.16).

Quoted · p. 146 “We can find the resistance of the single-crystal silicon bar … in terms of its resistivity, cross-sectional area, and length L” as Eq. (3.17), R=rhoL/A.

Summarized · pp. 145–146 If both carrier types are present, both contributions belong in conductivity. The dimensions of rho are Ω·cm when concentrations and mobility use cm-based units.

Expansion note: Why convert area before using Eq. (3.17)? Resistivity is in Ω·cm, so length and cross-sectional area must use cm and cm² for the dimensions to match.
