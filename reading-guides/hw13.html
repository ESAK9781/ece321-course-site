Similar to 18.27 a stored 1 with a half-supply pre-charge · §18.3.1

Sketch the conducting part of the cell, describe the read, and find where node Q‾ settles.

Similar problem (different stored bit, pre-charge level and process)

Consider the read operation of the 6T SRAM cell of Fig. 18.22 in a 0.13-µm process (VDD=1.2 V, Vtn=0.4 V) when the cell is storing a 1, that is, VQ=VDD and VQ‾=0 V. Assume that the bit lines are precharged to VDD/2 before the word-line voltage is raised to VDD, and that the access transistors have (W/L)a=3(W/L)n. Sketch the relevant part of the circuit, describe the operation, and find the voltage at which VQ‾ settles. Is the read non-destructive?

Step 1 · Which devices conduct

With W=VDD both access transistors turn on. On the B side the stored node Q is at VDD and the line is at VDD/2, so only a small current flows from the cell into the B line; that side is not the critical one. On the B‾ side, node Q‾ is at 0 V and the line is at VDD/2, so current flows from the B‾-line capacitance through the access transistor Q5 into CQ‾ and through the NMOS Q1 (gate at VQ=VDD, so on) to ground.

Step 2 · Operating modes at the settling point

Call the settling voltage V (it is VQ‾). Q5 has gate VDD, drain at the line voltage VDD/2=0.6 V and source V, so VDS=0.6−V and VOV=VDD−V−Vtn=0.8−V. Since 0.6−V<0.8−V for any V, Q5 is in triode (with a VDD pre-charge it would be in saturation). Q1 has VGS=VDD and VDS=V so VDS<0.8 V, also triode.

Step 3 · Equate the currents
3[(0.8−V)(0.6−V)−12(0.6−V)2]=0.8V−12V2

Both currents carry the same factor μnCox(W/L)n, which cancels, leaving the equation above with the factor 3 from (W/L)a=3(W/L)n. Multiplying out gives a quadratic in V whose root between 0 and 0.6 V is V≈0.364 V.

Check: with a VDD pre-charge the same ratio of 3 gives exactly V=Vtn=0.4 V, the limit of the text's design rule, so the half-supply pre-charge is the gentler case. A script solved both quadratics.

Step 4 · Conclusion

VQ‾≈0.364 V is below Vtn=0.4 V, so Q3 stays off, the inverter Q3-Q4 does not switch and the read is non-destructive. The B‾ line drops slightly while B stays up, a small difference the sense amplifier detects.

The 6T SRAM cell and the read operation §18.3.1, pp. 1217–1218
Quoted · p. 1217

Figure 18.22 shows a typical static memory cell in CMOS technology. The circuit, which we encountered in Section 18.2, is a flip-flop comprising two cross-coupled inverters and two access transistors, Q5 and Q6. The access transistors are turned on when the word line is selected and its voltage raised to VDD, and they connect the flip-flop to the column (bit or B) line and column (bit or B‾) line. … The access transistors act as transmission gates allowing bidirectional current flow between the flip-flop and the B and B‾ lines. This circuit is known as the six-transistor or 6T SRAM cell.

The Read Operation Consider first a read operation, and assume that the cell is storing a 1. In this case, Q will be high at VDD, and Q‾ will be low at 0 V. Before the read operation begins, the B and B‾ lines are raised to a voltage in the range VDD/2 to VDD. This process, known as precharging, is performed using circuits we will discuss in Section 18.4, where we consider sense amplifiers. To simplify matters, let's assume here that the precharge voltage of B and B‾ is VDD.

Quoted · p. 1218

When the word line is selected and the access transistors Q5 and Q6 are turned on, we can see that the only portion of the circuit that will be conducting is what is shown in Fig. 18.23. Noting that the initial value of vQ‾ is 0 V, we can see that current will flow from the B‾ line (actually, from the B‾-line capacitance CB‾) through Q5 and into capacitor CQ‾, which is the small equivalent capacitance between the Q‾ node and ground. This current charges CQ‾ and thus vQ‾ rises and Q1 conducts, sinking some of the current supplied by Q5. Equilibrium is reached when CQ‾ is charged to a voltage VQ‾ at which I1 equals I5, and no current flows through CQ‾. Here it is extremely important to note that to avoid changing the state of the flip-flop, that is, for our read operation to be nondestructive, VQ‾ must not exceed the threshold voltage of the inverter Q3–Q4. In fact, SRAM designers usually impose a more stringent requirement on the value of VQ‾, namely, that it should be lower than the threshold voltage of Q3, Vtn. Thus, the design problem we shall now solve is as follows: Determine the ratio of (W/L)5/(W/L)1 so that VQ‾≤Vtn.

Noting that Q5 will be operating in saturation and neglecting, for simplicity, the body effect, we can write

I5=12(μnCox)(WL)5(VDD−Vtn−VQ‾)2
(18.19)

Transistor Q1 will be operating in the triode region, and its current I1 can be written as

I1=(μnCox)(WL)1[(VDD−Vtn)VQ‾−12VQ‾2]
(18.20)

Equating I5 and I1 gives a quadratic equation in VQ‾, which can be solved to obtain

VQ‾VDD−Vtn=1−11+(W/L)5(W/L)1
(18.21)

Waveforms during a read and the bit-line change p. 1220
Quoted · p. 1220

Observe that as we have already discussed, vQ‾ rises from zero to a voltage VQ‾≤Vtn. Correspondingly, the change in vQ will be very small, justifying the assumption implicit in the analysis above that vQ remains constant at VDD. Most important, note that the voltage of the B‾ line, vB‾, decreases by a small amount ΔV. This is a result of the discharge of the capacitance of the B‾ line, CB‾, by the current I5. Assuming that I5 reaches its equilibrium value in Eq. (18.19) relatively quickly, capacitor CB‾ is in effect discharged by a constant current I5 and the change in its voltage, ΔV, obtained in a time interval Δt, can be found by writing a charge-balance equation, I5Δt=CB‾ΔV. Thus, ΔV=I5Δt/CB‾ (18.24).

Similar to 18.26 a 0.13-µm cell · §18.3.1

Find the largest (W/L)5/(W/L)1 for a limit on VQ‾, then choose the widths that give the smallest combined area.

Similar problem (new process and limit)

Consider a 6T SRAM cell fabricated in a 0.13-µm CMOS process for which Vtn=|Vtp|=0.4 V and VDD=1.2 V. If during a read-1 operation it is required that VQ‾ not exceed 0.15 V, determine the maximum allowable value of the ratio (W/L)5/(W/L)1. For L1=L5=130 nm, select values for W1 and W5 that minimize the combined areas of Q1 and Q5. Assume that the minimum width allowed is 130 nm.

Step 1 · Normalize the limit

The graph's vertical axis is VQ‾/(VDD−Vtn). Here VDD−Vtn=0.8 V, so the limit is 0.15/0.8=0.1875.

Step 2 · The ratio

Reading the graph at 0.1875 gives a ratio of about 0.5. The exact value from Eq. (18.21) solved for the ratio is

(W/L)5(W/L)1=1(1−0.1875)2−1=0.515

Check: substituting 0.515 back into Eq. (18.21) returns 1−1/1.515=0.1875. A script confirmed it.

Step 3 · Widths that minimize the area

The gate area of each transistor is W×L and both lengths are 130 nm, so the combined area is proportional to W1+W5. The ratio limit with equal lengths is W5/W1≤0.515. Making W5 as small as allowed (the 130 nm minimum) lets W1 be as small as 130/0.515=252.5 nm. A smaller W1 would break the ratio; a larger W5 would force W1 larger still.

W5=130 nm,W1≥1300.515≈253 nm

Expansion note: what "minimize the combined areas" asks you to balance

The ratio fixes only the relative size of the two transistors; many width pairs satisfy it. The minimum allowed width puts a floor under both. Because a smaller combined area means smaller widths, look at which of the two transistors is limited by the floor and which then follows from the ratio.

Design constraint on the access transistor §18.3.1, pp. 1218–1219
Quoted · pp. 1218–1219

This is an attractive relationship, since it provides VQ‾ in normalized form and thus always applies, independent of the process technology utilized. Figure 18.24 shows a universal plot of [VQ‾/(VDD−Vtn)] versus (W/L)5/(W/L)1. For a given process technology, VDD and Vtn are determined, and the plot in Fig. 18.24 can be used to determine the maximum value permitted for (W/L)5/(W/L)1 while keeping VQ‾ below a desired value. Alternatively, we can derive a formula for this purpose. For instance, if VQ‾ is to be kept below Vtn, the ratio of (W/L)5 to (W/L)1 must be kept below the value obtained from Eq. (18.21), that is,

(W/L)5(W/L)1≤1(1−VtnVDD−Vtn)2−1
(18.22)

This is an important design constraint that can be expressed in a slightly more general form by replacing (W/L)5 with (W/L)a, where the subscript a denotes access transistors Q5 and Q6, and (W/L)1 with (W/L)n, which is the W/L ratio of QN in each of the two inverters; thus,

(W/L)a(W/L)n≤1(1−VtnVDD−Vtn)2−1
(18.23)

Summarized from the Lesson 35 and 36 notetakers

The notetaker works out the same bound by finding the modes of the access transistor and the pull-down NMOS when VQ‾=Vtn, then requiring the pull-down current to be at least the access current.
