The DC bias point §7.2.1, p. 381
Quoted · p. 381

We can find the dc bias current ID by setting the signal vgs to zero; thus,

ID=12kn(VGS−Vt)2=12knVOV2
(7.25)

where we have neglected channel-length modulation (i.e., we have assumed λ=0). Here VOV=VGS−Vt is the overdrive voltage at which the MOSFET is biased to operate.

Expansion note: what the bias current and the signal current are

The total drain current is the steady (dc) bias current plus a small signal current on top of it. ID is the steady part, found with no signal. The parameters gm and ro describe how the transistor reacts to small changes around that bias point, which is why they depend on it.

Small-signal models and the output resistance ro §7.2.1, pp. 385–386
Quoted · p. 385

… Such dependence was modeled by a finite resistance ro between drain and source, whose value was given by Eq. (5.27) in Section 5.2.4, which we repeat here (with the prime on ID dropped) as

ro=|VA|ID
(7.37)

where the Early voltage VA=1/λ is a MOSFET parameter that either is specified or can be measured. …

Quoted · p. 386

The current ID is the value of the dc drain current without the channel-length modulation taken into account; that is,

ID=12knVOV2
(7.38)

Typically, ro is in the range of 10 kΩ to 1000 kΩ. … It is important to note that the small-signal model parameters gm and ro depend on the dc bias point of the MOSFET.

Expansion note: how λ, VA and ro are connected

In saturation the drain current rises slightly with vDS; λ measures that slope, and VA=1/λ is the same information as a voltage. A larger VA means a flatter characteristic and a larger ro. When λ=0 the characteristic is flat and ro is infinite, which is why the first model omits it.

The transconductance gm: three expressions §7.2.1, pp. 386–387
Quoted · p. 386

… the MOSFET transconductance given by Eq. (7.32), which we rewrite with kn=kn′(W/L) as follows:

gm=kn′(W/L)(VGS−Vt)=kn′(W/L)VOV
(7.40)

This relationship indicates that gm is proportional to the process transconductance parameter kn′=μnCox and to the W/L ratio of the MOS transistor … We can find another useful expression for gm by substituting 2ID/(kn′(W/L)) [from Eq. (7.25)] for VOV in Eq. (7.40):

gm=2kn′W/LID
(7.41)
Quoted · p. 387

Yet another useful expression for gm of the MOSFET can be obtained by substituting for kn′(W/L) in Eq. (7.40) by 2ID/(VGS−Vt)2:

gm=2IDVGS−Vt=2IDVOV=ID(VOV/2)
(7.42)

… In summary, there are three different relationships for determining gm—Eqs. (7.40), (7.41), and (7.42)—and there are three design parameters—(W/L), VOV, and ID, any two of which can be chosen independently. …

Expansion note: why three expressions give the same gm

All three come from gm=knVOV with kn=kn′(W/L), using ID=12knVOV2 to eliminate one of the two quantities each time. They are interchangeable whenever the quantities they contain are known and consistent.

Step 1 · Signal circuit

For the signal, the supplies +VDD and −VSS are shorts to ground (p. 385). In the T model of Fig. 7.17(d) the gate current is zero, so the gate sits at vi; a resistance 1/gm lies between the gate node and the source, and RS connects the source to ground.

Step 2 · Source current

The signal current i flows down through 1/gm and then through RS. Circuit rule: KVL around the loop from vi to ground, with Ohm's law across each resistor.

vi=i(250Ω+2000Ω)⇒i=vi2250Ω=0.444mA per volt of vi
Step 3 · Source voltage gain

Circuit rule: Ohm's law across RS.

vs=iRS=(0.444mA)(2kΩ)per volt⇒vsvi=0.889
Step 4 · Drain voltage gain

In the T model id=is=i (Fig. 7.17(d)). This current is drawn through RD from the supply, which is a signal ground, so the drain signal falls below zero by iRD.

vd=−idRD=−(0.444mA)(6kΩ)per volt⇒vdvi=−2.67

Check: vgs=vi−vs=0.111vi, and gmvgs=(4mA/V)(0.111)=0.444 mA per volt, equal to i.

Small-signal equivalent-circuit models §7.2.1, p. 385
Quoted · p. 385

From a signal point of view, the FET behaves as a voltage-controlled current source. It accepts a signal vgs between its gate and source and provides a current gmvgs at the drain terminal. The input resistance of this controlled source is very high—ideally, infinite. … In analyzing a MOSFET amplifier circuit, we can replace the transistor with the equivalent-circuit model shown in Fig. 7.13(a). The rest of the circuit remains unchanged except that ideal constant dc voltage sources are replaced by short circuits. This is because the voltage across an ideal constant dc voltage source does not change, and thus there will always be a zero voltage signal across a constant dc voltage source. …

The T equivalent-circuit model §7.2.1, pp. 393–394
Quoted · p. 393

Through a simple circuit transformation it is possible to develop an alternative equivalent-circuit model for the MOSFET. The development of such a model, known as the T model, is illustrated in Fig. 7.17. …

Summarized (p. 393)

Fig. 7.17(a) is the model without ro. In (b) a second current source gmvgs is added in series with the original one; this does not change the terminal currents. The new node X is joined to the gate in (c); the gate current stays zero. The controlled source across its own control voltage vgs then draws gmvgs and is replaced by a resistance vgs/(gmvgs)=1/gm, giving (d). In (d) ig is still zero, id=gmvgs, and is=vgs/(1/gm)=gmvgs, as in (a).

Quoted · p. 393

The model of Fig. 7.17(d) shows that the resistance between gate and source looking into the source is 1/gm. This observation and the T model prove useful in many applications. Note that the resistance between gate and source, looking into the gate, is infinite.

In developing the T model we did not include ro. If we want, we can do this by incorporating in the circuit of Fig. 7.17(d) a resistance ro between drain and source, as shown in Fig. 7.18(a). …

Expansion note: how to read the T model

In the T model the gate terminal attaches to the node between the current source and the resistor, so the voltage between the gate and that node does not exist; the gate-to-source voltage vgs appears across the resistor 1/gm.

Worked example: Example 7.4, an amplifier analyzed with the T model pp. 395–396
Quoted · p. 395 (Example 7.4)

Figure 7.19(a) shows a MOSFET amplifier biased by a constant-current source I. Assume that the values of I and RD are such that the MOSFET operates in the saturation region. The input signal vi is coupled to the source terminal by a large capacitor CC1. Similarly, the output signal at the drain is taken through a large coupling capacitor CC2. Find the input resistance Rin and the voltage gain vo/vi. Neglect channel-length modulation.

Quoted · p. 395, Solution

Replacing the MOSFET with its T equivalent-circuit model results in the amplifier equivalent circuit shown in Fig. 7.19(b). Notice that the dc current source I is replaced with an open circuit and the dc voltage source VDD is replaced by a short circuit. The large coupling capacitors have been replaced by short circuits. From the equivalent-circuit model we determine

Rin=vi−i=1/gm

and, p. 396,

vo=−iRD=(vi1/gm)RD=gmRDvi

Thus, Av≡vo/vi=gmRD. This amplifier, known as the common-gate amplifier because the gate at ground potential is common to both the input and output ports, has a low input resistance (1/gm) and a noninverting gain. …

Expansion note: the sign of the current i in the example

In Fig. 7.19(b) the arrow for i is drawn into the T-model resistor from the gate node, while the input source pushes current into the source terminal; that is why the book writes Rin=vi/(−i). When you set up a circuit of your own, choose a current direction and keep to it through every equation.
