Lesson 3 Equilibrium pn-junction structure

Lesson 3 · p. 171 Problem 3.14

Quoted · p. 171

If, for a particular junction, the acceptor concentration is 1017/cm^3 and the donor concentration is 1016/cm^3, find the junction built-in voltage. Assume ni=1.5×1010/cm^3. Also, find the width of the depletion region (W) and its extent in each of the p and n regions when the junction terminals are left open. Calculate the magnitude of the charge stored on either side of the junction. Assume that the junction area is 10μm^2.

Similar to 3.14 abrupt silicon junction · changed values

Similar problem (new values)

An abrupt silicon junction at 300 K has NA=6×1016cm^{-3}, ND=2×1016cm^{-3}, ni=1.1×1010cm^{-3}, and area A=8μm^2. Find V0, total depletion width W, the distances xp and xn, and the magnitude of depletion charge on either side. Use VT=0.02585 V, q=1.602×10−19 C, and epss=11.7(8.854×10−14) F/cm.

Step 1 · built-in potential

From Eq. (3.21), V0=VTln(NAND/ni2).

Step 2 · depletion width and partition

Use Eqs. (3.24)–(3.26): W=(2epssV0/q)(1/NA+1/ND), xp=WND/(NA+ND), and xn=WNA/(NA+ND). The saved script converts the area and lengths to cm-based units before calculating charge.

Step 3 · charge balance

From Eqs. (3.22) and (3.23), Q=qANAxp=qANDxn. Equality of the two magnitudes is the charge-neutrality check.

Results (similar problem)

V0=0.7736 V, W=0.2583 micrometers, xp=0.06456 micrometers, and xn=0.1937 micrometers. Each depletion-charge magnitude is 4.965×10−15 C; the two charge expressions agree. Full precision is in worked/p3_14.txt.

Formula toolkit

Eq. (3.21) V0=VTln(NAND/ni2), with VT=kT/q.

Eqs. (3.22) and (3.23) Q=qANAxp=qANDxn (equal magnitudes on each side).

Eq. (3.24) Charge neutrality: NAxp=NDxn.

Eq. (3.25) W=2epssV0q(1NA+1ND).

Eqs. (3.26) and (3.27) xp=WNDNA+ND; xn=WNANA+ND.

units Convert junction area from μm^2 to cm^2 when using concentration in cm^−3.

Reading · pn junction at equilibrium, pp. 151–156

Summarized · pp. 151–154 Joining p-type and n-type regions lets majority carriers diffuse across the boundary and recombine. The uncovered ionized dopants leave a depletion region and an internal electric field. At equilibrium, the field-driven drift balances diffusion.

Quoted · p. 155 The textbook relates the depletion-region charge on the p side and n side by equal magnitude and opposite sign. The doping concentrations therefore set how far the depletion region extends into each side.

Summarized · pp. 155–156 With the junction open, the built-in potential is associated with the carrier-concentration ratio. The depletion width follows from the potential drop and the charge distribution; area converts charge density per length into total charge.

Expansion note: Why does most depletion extend into the lighter-doped side? Charge neutrality requires NAxp=NDxn; the smaller doping density must occupy the longer distance.

Lesson 3 · p. 171 Problem 3.16

Quoted · p. 171

In a pn junction for which NA≫ND, and the depletion layer exists mostly on the lightly doped side with W=0.2μm, find V0 if ND=1016/cm^3. Also calculate QJ for the case A=10μm^2.

Similar to 3.16 one-sided junction approximation · new values

Similar problem (new values)

For an abrupt pn junction with NA≫ND, take the depletion width on the lightly doped side as W=0.16μm and ND=2×1016cm^{-3}. Estimate V0 using the one-sided approximation and find the depletion charge magnitude when A=8μm^2. Use q=1.602×10−19 C and epss=11.7(8.854×10−14) F/cm.

Step 1 · relate potential and width

With the depletion layer almost entirely in the n side, Eq. (3.25) reduces to W≈2epssV0/(qND). Rearrange to V0≈qNDW2/(2epss).

Step 2 · charge

Use QJ=qANDW. Convert W to cm and A to cm^2. The saved computation checks the charge from both qANDW and the potential-width relation.

Results (similar problem)

For the new values, V0=0.3959 V and QJ=4.101×10−15 C. The width recovered from the one-sided relation equals 0.16μm.

Formula toolkit

Eq. (3.25) W=2epssV0q(1NA+1ND).

one-sided approximation If NA≫ND, then W≈xn and 1/NA is negligible: W≈2epssV0/(qND).

from Eq. (3.25) V0≈qNDW2/(2epss).

Eq. (3.22) QJ=qANDxn≈qANDW.

Reading · depletion width and charge, pp. 155–156

Summarized · p. 155 The depletion approximation treats the mobile carriers as removed from the junction region, leaving fixed ionized donors and acceptors. Charge neutrality balances the total uncovered dopant charge on either side.

Summarized · p. 156 When one side is much more lightly doped, its depletion width dominates. In this limit the more heavily doped side contributes little to the total width; the one-sided form follows from the full depletion-width relation, Eq. (3.25).

Expansion note: Why is W approximately the n-side width here? The depletion widths are inversely related to the doping concentrations through charge neutrality.

Lesson 4 pn junction under forward bias

Lesson 4 · p. 172 Problem 3.23

Quoted · p. 172

Calculate Is and the current I for V=780mV for a pn junction for which NA=1017/cm^3, ND=1016/cm^3, A=20μm^2, ni=1.5×1010/cm^3, Lp=5μm, Ln=10μm, Dp=10cm^2/s, and Dn=18cm^2/s.

Similar to 3.23 long-base diode model · changed values

Similar problem (new values)

At 300 K, calculate Is and I for V=0.72 V in an ideal pn junction with NA=6×1016cm^{-3}, ND=8×1015cm^{-3}, A=12μm^2, ni=1.2×1010cm^{-3}, Lp=4μm, Ln=8μm, Dp=12cm^2/s, and Dn=22cm^2/s. Use VT=0.02585 V and q=1.602×10−19 C.

Step 1 · diffusion saturation current

From Eq. (3.40), Is=qAni2(Dp/(LpND)+Dn/(LnNA)). Convert A to cm^2 and diffusion lengths to cm.

Step 2 · forward current

From Eq. (3.39), I=Is(eV/VT−1). The saved script evaluates the exponential and prints the component terms and total.

Step 3 · check

Each D/L term is cm/s; dividing by concentration gives cm^4/s. Multiplying by qAni2 then gives amperes. Confirm forward bias gives positive I.

Results (similar problem)

The hole and electron terms are 1.038×10−17 A and 1.269×10−18 A, giving Is=1.165×10−17 A. At 0.72 V, I=1.455×10−5 A =0.01455 mA. The inverse diode relation recovers 0.72 V; full precision is in worked/p3_23.txt.

Formula toolkit

Eq. (3.40) Is=qAni2(DpLpND+DnLnNA).

Eq. (3.39) I=Is(eV/VT−1).

definition in Sec. 3.5.2 VT=kT/q; at room temperature VT≈25.9 mV.

units 1μm=10−4 cm and 1μm^2=10−8 cm^2.

Reading · diode current, pp. 157–163

Summarized · pp. 157–160 Forward bias reduces the junction barrier and raises minority-carrier injection at the depletion edges. The injected carriers diffuse into the quasi-neutral regions and recombine.

Summarized · pp. 160–163 Under the ideal long-base assumptions, electron and hole injection contributions add to the saturation current. The exponential diode relation then maps applied voltage to current; VT is the thermal voltage.

Quoted · p. 163 The chapter identifies the ideal-junction current as the difference between the forward injection component and the reverse saturation component, yielding the familiar exponential form (Eq. 3.39).

Expansion note: Why are there two terms in Is? Minority carriers are injected into both sides of the junction, so both diffusion contributions enter unless one side dominates.

Lesson 4 · p. 172 Problem 3.25

Quoted · p. 172

A p+n junction is one in which the doping concentration in the p region is much greater than that in the n region. In such a junction, the forward current is mostly due to hole injection across the junction. Show that

I≃Ip=Aqni2DpLpND(eV/VT−1)

For the specific case in which ND=1016/cm^3, Dp=10cm^2/s, Lp=10μm, and A=104μm^2, find Is and the voltage V obtained when I=0.2mA. Assume operation at 300 K where ni=1.5×1010/cm^3.

Similar to 3.25 p+n junction · changed parameters

Similar problem (new values)

For a p+n junction where hole injection dominates, use ND=1.8×1016cm^{-3}, Dp=8cm^2/s, Lp=12μm, A=8×103μm^2, and ni=1.2×1010cm^{-3} at 300 K. Find Is and the voltage when I=0.15 mA. Use VT=0.02585 V and q=1.602×10−19 C.

Step 1 · simplify the saturation current

Because hole injection dominates, retain only the hole term in Eq. (3.40): Is≃Aqni2Dp/(LpND).

Step 2 · solve the diode equation

From Eq. (3.39), I=Is(eV/VT−1). Rearranging gives V=VTln(1+I/Is). Use the computed Is, not a rounded intermediate.

Step 3 · verify

Substitute the resulting voltage into Eq. (3.39) and confirm the current is 0.15 mA. Full precision is preserved in worked/p3_25.py.

Results (similar problem)

The result is Is=6.8352×10−16 A and V=0.67506 V. Back-substitution into Eq. (3.39) returns 0.150 mA.

Formula toolkit

Eq. (3.40) Is=qAni2(DpLpND+DnLnNA).

p+n limit from Eq. (3.40) When NA≫ND and hole injection dominates, Is≃Aqni2Dp/(LpND).

Eq. (3.39) I=Is(eV/VT−1).

from Eq. (3.39) V=VTln(1+I/Is).

thermal-voltage definition VT=kT/q.

Reading · asymmetric junction injection, pp. 160–163

Summarized · pp. 160–162 The two sides of a forward-biased junction contribute minority-carrier injection currents. Their relative sizes depend on dopant concentrations, diffusion coefficients, and diffusion lengths.

Summarized · p. 163 In a p+n junction, the p side is much more heavily doped. Hole injection into the n side dominates, allowing the total current relation to be simplified to the hole component.

Quoted · p. 172 Problem 3.25 states this one-sided-injection limit and supplies the reduced current expression as the relation to show.

Expansion note: Why can one injection component dominate? The minority-carrier concentrations and transport properties on the two sides need not be comparable; the heavily doped side suppresses its own minority contribution.
