Problem 16.13 Lesson 32 · p. 1146 · §16.1

Design a CMOS logic circuit from a verbal specification: write the Boolean function, sketch the pull-down network, reduce it by inspection, and build the pull-up network as its dual.

Problem statement

D*16.13 It is required to design a CMOS logic circuit that realizes a three-input, even-parity checker. Specifically, the output Y is to be low when an even number (0 or 2) of the inputs A, B, and C are high.

  1. Give the Boolean function Y‾.
  2. Sketch a PDN directly from the expression for Y‾. Note that it requires 12 transistors in addition to those in the inverters.
  3. From inspection of the PDN circuit, reduce the number of transistors to 10 (not counting those in the inverters).
  4. Find the PUN as a dual of the PDN in (c), and hence the complete realization.

No figure is referenced by this problem; you draw the networks. The "D" before the number marks a design problem and the asterisk a difficult problem (see the key at the foot of the book's problem pages).

Similar to 16.13 same four questions, a different checker · §16.1

Write Y‾, draw the PDN directly, reduce it by inspection, then find the dual PUN and the complete circuit.

Similar problem (new function)

It is required to design a CMOS logic circuit that realizes a three-input "exactly-one" checker. Specifically, the output Y is to be low when exactly one of the inputs A, B, and C is high.

  1. Give the Boolean function Y‾.
  2. Sketch a PDN directly from the expression for Y‾. Note that it requires 9 transistors in addition to those in the inverters.
  3. From inspection of the PDN circuit, reduce the number of transistors to 8 (not counting those in the inverters).
  4. Find the PUN as a dual of the PDN in (c), and hence the complete realization.
Step 1 · (a) The function Y‾

Y is low for exactly three input rows: A high only, B high only, C high only. Y‾ is 1 on those rows. Each row is an AND of the inputs (high inputs as they are, low inputs complemented), and the rows are ORed:

Y‾=AB‾C‾+A‾BC‾+A‾B‾C
Step 2 · (b) PDN directly from Y‾

The PDN conducts when Y‾=1 (§16.1.9, item 1): series NMOS devices form each AND term and the three terms are in parallel (p. 1114). Every term has three devices, so the PDN has 3×3=9 transistors. Complemented variables (A‾, B‾, C‾) need three inverters, which are counted separately.

Step 3 · (c) Reduction by inspection

Terms 1 and 2 share the factor C‾, and the rest of each is AB‾ and A‾B:

Y‾=C‾(AB‾+A‾B)+A‾B‾C

The shared device C‾ is drawn once, in series with two parallel two-device branches, and a three-device branch is in parallel with that. Count: 1+2+2+3=8 transistors.

Step 4 · (d) PUN as the dual, complete circuit

By duality (§16.1.7) every series connection becomes parallel and every parallel connection series, with PMOS devices fed by the same gate signals. The dual of "C‾ in series with [(A series B‾) parallel (A‾ series B)], all in parallel with (A‾, B‾, C in series)" is "[C‾ parallel ((A parallel B‾) in series with (A‾ parallel B))] in series with (A‾, B‾, C in parallel)". It has 8 PMOS transistors.

Check: a script confirmed over all 8 input rows that exactly one network conducts and that the PUN conducts exactly when Y=1. The total is 8+8=16 transistors, plus 6 in the three inverters (22 in all).

Answers (similar problem)
(a)Y‾=AB‾C‾+A‾BC‾+A‾B‾C
(b)9 NMOS transistors (three series triples in parallel)
(c)Y‾=C‾(AB‾+A‾B)+A‾B‾C, 8 NMOS transistors
(d)Dual PUN of 8 PMOS transistors; 16 transistors plus 3 inverters

Expansion note: what "even parity" means as a specification

Parity counts how many inputs are high. "Even" means that count is an even number (0, 2, ...). The problem tells you which counts make Y low, so the specification is already a list of input combinations; you decide which combinations that list contains.

Expansion note: why the bar sits over Y in part (a)

A PDN conducts when its output should be low, so it is described by the condition for Y=0. That condition is the same as Y‾=1, and it is written directly in the inputs as they are, which is why the book asks for Y‾ first.

General structure of CMOS logic §16.1.3, pp. 1112–1114
Quoted · p. 1113

… the pull-down network (PDN) constructed of NMOS transistors, and the pull-up network (PUN) constructed of PMOS transistors (see Fig. 16.3). The two networks are operated by the input variables, in a complementary fashion. Thus, for the three-input gate represented in Fig. 16.3, the PDN will conduct for all input combinations that require a low output (Y=0) and will then pull the output node down to ground, causing a zero voltage to appear at the output, vY=0. Simultaneously, the PUN will be off, and no direct dc path will exist between VDD and ground. On the other hand, all input combinations that call for a high output (Y=1) will cause the PUN to conduct, and the PUN will then pull the output node up to VDD …

Quoted · pp. 1113–1114

Now, since the PDN comprises NMOS transistors, and since an NMOS transistor conducts when the signal at its gate is high, the PDN is activated (i.e., conducts) when the inputs are high. In a dual manner, the PUN comprises PMOS transistors, and a PMOS transistor conducts when the input signal at its gate is low; thus the PUN is activated when the inputs are low.

The PDN utilizes devices in parallel to form an OR function and devices in series to form an AND function; the same is true of the PUN. Here, the OR and AND notations refer to current flow or conduction.

Examples of pull-down and pull-up networks §16.1.3, pp. 1114–1115
Quoted · pp. 1114–1115

For the circuit in Fig. 16.4(a), QA will conduct when A is high (vA=VDD) and will then pull the output node down to ground (vY=0 V, Y=0). Similarly, QB conducts and pulls Y down when B is high. Thus Y will be low when A is high or B is high, which can be expressed as Y‾=A+B, or equivalently Y=A+B‾.

The PDN in Fig. 16.4(b) will conduct only when A and B are both high simultaneously. Thus Y will be low when A is high and B is high, Y‾=AB, or equivalently Y=AB‾.

As a final example, the PDN in Fig. 16.4(c) will conduct and cause Y to be 0 when A is high or when B and C are both high, thus Y‾=A+BC …

Next consider the PUN examples shown in Fig. 16.5. The PUN in Fig. 16.5(a) will conduct and pull Y up to VDD (Y=1) when A is low or B is low, thus Y=A‾+B‾. The PUN in Fig. 16.5(b) will conduct and produce a high output (vY=VDD, Y=1) only when A and B are both low, thus Y=A‾B‾. Finally, the PUN in Fig. 16.5(c) will conduct and cause Y to be high (logic 1) if A is low or if B and C are both low; thus, Y=A‾+B‾C‾.

Expansion note: reading a bar over a letter in these figures

A‾ is "not A": it is high when A is low. In a PUN every PMOS conducts when its gate is low, so a PMOS whose gate carries A contributes A‾ to the condition that makes Y high.

A complex gate §16.1.6, p. 1117
Quoted · p. 1117

Consider next the more complex logic function

Y=A(B+CD)‾
(16.3)

Since Y‾=A(B+CD), we see that Y should be low for A high and simultaneously either B high or C and D both high, from which the PDN is directly obtained. To obtain the PUN, we need to express Y in terms of the complemented variables. We do this through repeated application of DeMorgan's law, as follows:

Y=A(B+CD)‾=A‾+B+CD‾=A‾+B‾CD‾=A‾+B‾(C‾+D‾)
(16.4)

Thus, Y is high for A low or B low and either C or D low. The corresponding complete CMOS circuit will be as shown in Fig. 16.9.

Obtaining the PUN from the PDN and vice versa §16.1.7, pp. 1117–1118
Quoted · pp. 1117–1118

From the CMOS gate circuits considered thus far (e.g., that in Fig. 16.9), we observe that the PDN and the PUN are dual networks: Where a series branch exists in one, a parallel branch exists in the other. Thus, we can obtain one from the other, a process that can be simpler than having to synthesize each separately from the Boolean expression of the function. For instance, in the circuit of Fig. 16.9, we found it relatively easy to obtain the PDN, simply because we already had Y‾ in terms of the uncomplemented inputs. On the other hand, to obtain the PUN, we had to manipulate the given Boolean expression to express Y as a function of the complemented variables, the form convenient for synthesizing PUNs. Alternatively, we could have used this duality property to obtain the PUN from the PDN. …

We should, however, mention that at times it is not easy to obtain one of the two networks from the other using the duality property. For such cases, we would have to resort to a more rigorous process, which is beyond the scope of this book …

Expansion note: what "dual" asks you to do to a drawing

Go through the PDN branch by branch. Wherever devices are in series, put the corresponding PMOS devices in parallel, and wherever they are in parallel, put them in series. Each PMOS keeps the same gate signal as the NMOS it replaces; the active-low behavior of PMOS is what makes the two networks complementary.

The exclusive-OR function §16.1.8, pp. 1118–1119
Quoted · pp. 1118–1119

An important function that often arises in logic design is the exclusive-OR (XOR) function,

Y=AB‾+A‾B
(16.5)

We observe that since Y (rather than Y‾) is given, it is easier to synthesize the PUN. Unfortunately, however, Y is not a function of the complemented variables only (as we would like it to be). Thus, we will need additional inverters. The PUN obtained directly from Eq. (16.5) is shown in Fig. 16.10(a). Note that the Q1, Q2 branch realizes the first term (AB‾), whereas the Q3, Q4 branch realizes the second term (A‾B). Note also the need for two additional inverters to generate A‾ and B‾.

As for synthesizing the PDN, we can obtain it as the dual network of the PUN in Fig. 16.10(a). Alternatively, we can develop an expression for Y‾ and use it to synthesize the PDN. … DeMorgan's law can be applied to the expression in Eq. (16.5) to obtain Y‾ as

Y‾=AB+A‾B‾
(16.6)

The corresponding PDN will be as in Fig. 16.10(b), which shows the CMOS realization of the exclusive-OR function except for the two additional inverters. Note that the exclusive-OR requires 12 transistors for its realization, a rather complex network.

Another interesting observation follows from the circuit in Fig. 16.10(b). The PDN and the PUN here are not dual networks. Indeed, duality of the PDN and the PUN is not a necessary condition. Thus, although a dual of PDN (or PUN) can always be used for PUN (or PDN), the two networks are not necessarily duals.

Expansion note: why the book gets a PDN from Y‾ here but not a dual

The book shows two valid routes to the second network: take the dual of the first, or write the other function (Y or Y‾) and read the network off it. Both give circuits that behave the same. The route that avoids a dual is useful when the dual is hard to draw.

Summary of the synthesis method §16.1.9, pp. 1119–1120
Quoted · pp. 1119–1120
  1. The PDN can be most directly synthesized by expressing Y‾ as a function of the uncomplemented variables. If complemented variables appear in this expression, additional inverters will be required to generate them.
  2. The PUN can be most directly synthesized by expressing Y as a function of the complemented variables and then applying the uncomplemented variables to the gates of the PMOS transistors. If uncomplemented variables appear in the expression, additional inverters will be needed.
  3. The PDN can be obtained from the PUN (and vice versa) using the duality property.

Worked example: Example 16.1 pp. 1120–1121
Quoted · Example 16.1, pp. 1120–1121

Synthesize a CMOS logic circuit that implements the Boolean function Y=A+B(C+D)‾.

Solution. To obtain the PDN we use Y‾=A+B(C+D) and note that Y will be low when A is high or when [B(C+D)] is high. Thus we have two parallel networks. One consists of a single transistor with A supplied to its gate, and the second is a network composed of a transistor controlled by B in series with two parallel transistors controlled by C and D. The resulting PDN is shown in Fig. 16.11(a).

A question arises: Should the B transistor be placed closer to ground (with the C and D transistors closer to Y) or closer to Y? From a logic standpoint, both are valid solutions.

To obtain the PUN we use deMorgan's law on the given expression for Y to obtain Y=A‾(B‾+C‾D‾), which leads to the circuit in Fig. 16.11(b). Here again, we may exchange the positions of the C and D transistors with no effect on the logic behavior. Finally, the complete circuit can be obtained by combining the PDN and the PUN shown in Fig. 16.11.

Step 1 · Inverter ratios and channel length

With L=60 nm for every transistor (the basic inverter's length), the ratios are n=(W/L)n=90/60=1.5 and p=(W/L)p=210/60=3.5 (p. 1167). Each additional input of a gate adds one NMOS and one PMOS transistor (p. 1170).

Step 2 · Rule for sizing a gate

Sizing is set by the worst case (p. 1167). Series devices add their resistances, so k equal devices in series have an equivalent ratio (W/L)eq=(W/L)/k (Eq. 17.27); parallel devices add their ratios (Eq. 17.28). Figures 17.8 and 17.9 apply this: in a k-input NOR the k series PMOS are kp each and the parallel NMOS are n each; in a k-input NAND the k series NMOS are kn each and the parallel PMOS are p each.

Step 3 · Circuit (a): four-input NOR plus inverter

NOR4: 4 PMOS of 4p=14 and 4 NMOS of n=1.5. Inverter: one PMOS of p=3.5 and one NMOS of n=1.5. Transistors: 8+2=10. Sum of ratios:

∑WL=4(14)+4(1.5)+3.5+1.5=67
Step 4 · Circuit (b): two NOR2 plus one NAND2

Each NOR2: 2 PMOS of 2p=7 and 2 NMOS of n=1.5, so 2(7)+2(1.5)=17. The NAND2: 2 PMOS of p=3.5 and 2 NMOS of 2n=3, so 2(3.5)+2(3)=13. Transistors: 2(4)+4=12. Sum of ratios:

∑WL=2(17)+13=47
Step 5 · Total area

All channels have the same length, so area is proportional to total width (the book compares gate areas this way in Exercise 17.9). Total width Wtot=L∑(W/L) and area A=L∑W:

(a)Wtot=67(60)=4020 nm,A=4020×60=2.412×105 nm2=0.241 μm2
(b)Wtot=47(60)=2820 nm,A=2820×60=1.692×105 nm2=0.169 μm2

Area here means the sum of W×L over all transistors, ignoring contacts and wiring. Circuit (b) has two more transistors but about 30% less area (ratio of areas 1.43).

Expansion note: what "fan-in" means

Fan-in is the number of inputs of a gate. A gate with more inputs has longer transistor stacks, so each device in a stack must be made wider to keep the same current-driving capability, which costs area and adds capacitance.

Expansion note: "total area" and the channel length

The book keeps every channel at the same length L, so the area of a transistor is proportional to its width. Comparing total width is then the same as comparing total area; a transistor count alone does not tell you which circuit is smaller.

Inverter sizing §17.2.1, p. 1165
Quoted · p. 1165

In this section, we are concerned with selecting appropriate values for the channel length L and the (W/L) ratios for the two transistors QN and QP in an inverter. Our reasoning can be summarized as follows.

  1. To minimize area, the length of all channels is usually made equal to the minimum length permitted by the given technology.
  2. In a given inverter, if our interest is strictly to minimize area, (W/L)n can be selected in the range from 1 to 1.5, which minimizes driving current. However, in more recent technologies it has become common to use slightly higher values, closer to 2 and above.
  3. The selection of (W/L)p relative to (W/L)n affects the noise margins and tPLH. Both are optimized by matching QP and QN. … In emerging technologies (e.g., the 28-nm process), kn′ and kp′ are closer in value, and it has become more common to use (W/L)p=(W/L)n.
  4. Having settled on an appropriate ratio of (W/L)p to (W/L)n, we still have to select (W/L)n to reduce tP and thus allow higher speeds of operation. Any increase in (W/L)n and proportionally in (W/L)p will of course increase area, and hence the inverter contribution to the value of the equivalent capacitance C. …
Summarized (items 3 and 4)

Historically kn′ has exceeded kp′ by a factor of 3 to 5, so matching QP and QN can be wasteful of area and capacitance. The book notes (W/L)p=(W/L)n is a possibility and (W/L)p=2(W/L)n a frequent compromise. Scaling both ratios of an inverter by a factor S changes tP through Eqs. (17.21) to (17.26); this lesson's problems use the sizing of §17.2.2.

Transistor sizing in CMOS logic gates §17.2.2, pp. 1167–1169
Quoted · p. 1167

Once a CMOS gate circuit has been generated, the only significant step remaining in the design is to decide on W/L ratios for all devices. These ratios usually are selected to provide the gate with current-driving capability in both directions equal to that of the basic inverter. For the basic inverter design, denote (W/L)n=n and (W/L)p=p, where n is usually 1 to 3, depending on technology (using larger numbers for newer technologies), and, for a matched design, p=(μn/μp)n; it should be noted, however, that often p=2n and for minimum area p=n. Thus, we wish to select individual W/L ratios for all transistors in a logic gate so that the PDN should be able to provide a capacitor discharge current at least equal to that of an NMOS transistor with W/L=n, and the PUN should be able to provide a charging current at least equal to that of a PMOS transistor with W/L=p. This will guarantee a worst-case gate delay equal to that of the basic inverter.

In the preceding description, the idea of “worst case” should be emphasized. It means that in deciding on device sizing, we should find the input combinations that result in the lowest output current and then choose sizes that will make this current equal to that of the basic inverter. Before we consider examples, we need to address the issue of determining the current-driving capability of a circuit consisting of a number of MOS devices. In other words, we need to find the equivalent W/L ratio of a network of MOSFETs. To that end, we consider the parallel and series connection of MOSFETs and find the equivalent W/L ratios.

The derivation of the equivalent W/L ratio is based on the fact that the on-resistance of a MOSFET is inversely proportional to W/L (see Eqs. 17.14 and 17.15). Thus, if a number of MOSFETs having ratios of (W/L)1, (W/L)2, …, are connected in series, the equivalent series resistance obtained by adding the on-resistances will be

Rseries=RN1+RN2+⋯=constant(W/L)1+constant(W/L)2+⋯=constant(W/L)eq

resulting in the following expression for (W/L)eq for transistors connected in series:

(W/L)eq=11(W/L)1+1(W/L)2+⋯
(17.27)

Similarly, we can show that the parallel connection of transistors with W/L ratios of (W/L)1, (W/L)2, …, results in an equivalent W/L of

(W/L)eq=(W/L)1+(W/L)2+⋯
(17.28)
Quoted · p. 1168 (end of page)

As an example, two identical MOS transistors with individual W/L ratios of 4 result in an equivalent W/L of 2 when connected in series and of 8 when connected in parallel. We can think about this another way. Connecting MOS transistors in series is equivalent to adding the lengths of their channels while the width does not change; connecting MOS transistors in parallel does not change the channel length but increases the width to the sum of the W's.

Expansion note: why equivalent W/L moves opposite to resistance

A larger W/L means a lower on-resistance. Resistances in series add, so series devices have a larger total resistance and therefore a smaller equivalent W/L. Conductances in parallel add, so parallel devices behave like one wider transistor and their ratios add.

Quoted · p. 1169

As an example of proper sizing, consider the four-input NOR in Fig. 17.8. Here, the worst case (the lowest current) for the PDN is obtained when only one of the NMOS transistors is conducting. We therefore select the W/L of each NMOS transistor to be equal to that of the NMOS transistor of the basic inverter, namely, n. For the PUN, however, the worst-case situation (and indeed the only case) occurs when all inputs are low and the four series PMOS transistors are conducting. Since the equivalent W/L will be one-quarter of that of each PMOS device, we should select the W/L ratio of each PMOS transistor to be four times that of QP of the basic inverter, that is, 4p.

As another example, we show in Fig. 17.9 the proper sizing for a four-input NAND gate. Comparison of the NAND and NOR gates in Figs. 17.8 and 17.9 indicates that because p is usually two to three times n, the NOR gate will require much greater area than the NAND gate. For this reason, NAND gates are generally preferred for implementing combinational-logic functions in CMOS.

Quoted · Example 17.4, p. 1169

Provide transistor W/L ratios for the logic circuit shown in Fig. 17.10. Assume that for the basic inverter n=2 and p=4 and that the channel length is 65 nm. Round all values of W to the nearest 10 nm.

Quoted · Solution, p. 1170

Refer to Fig. 17.10, and consider the PDN first. Note that the worst case occurs when QNB is on and either QNC or QND is on. That is, in the worst case, we have two transistors in series. Therefore, we select each of QNB, QNC, and QND to have twice the width of the n-channel device in the basic inverter: W/L=2n=4=260/65. For transistor QNA, select W/L to be equal to that of the n-channel device in the basic inverter: W/L=n=2=130/65.

Next, consider the PUN. Here, we see that in the worst case, we have three transistors in series: QPA, QPC, and QPD. Therefore, we select the W/L ratio of each of these to be three times that of QP in the basic inverter, that is, 3p: W/L=3p=12=780/65.

Finally, the W/L ratio for QPB should be selected so that the equivalent W/L of the series connection of QPB and QPA should be equal to p. It follows that for QPB the ratio should be 1.5p, QPB: W/L=1.5p=6=390/65.

Effects of fan-in and fan-out on propagation delay §17.2.3, pp. 1170–1171
Quoted · pp. 1170–1171

Each additional input to a CMOS gate requires two additional transistors, one NMOS and one PMOS. These additional transistors not only increase the chip area but also increase the total capacitance per gate, which in turn increases the propagation delay. The size-scaling method described earlier compensates for some (but not all) of the increase in tP. Specifically, by increasing device size, we are able to preserve the current-driving capability. However, the capacitance C increases because of both the increased number of inputs and the increase in device size. Thus tP will still increase with fan-in, a fact that imposes a practical limit on the fan-in of, say, the NAND gate to about 4. If a higher number of inputs is required, then “clever” logic design should be adopted to realize the given Boolean function with gates of no more than four inputs. This would usually mean an increase in the number of cascaded stages and thus an increase in delay. However, such an increase in delay can be less than the increase due to the large fan-in.

Power dissipation §17.3, pp. 1174–1176
Summarized (also covered in Lesson 32; not needed to state the assigned problem)

The CMOS inverter has no static power dissipation. Charging and discharging its load capacitance C each cycle dissipates, in the on-resistances of the switches, a total energy CVDD2 per cycle (12CVDD2 in each switch), independent of the resistance values. At switching frequency f the dynamic power is:

Pdyn=fCVDD2
(17.39)

It falls with a smaller C and especially a lower supply voltage.
