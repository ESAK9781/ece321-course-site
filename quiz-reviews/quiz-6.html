1. Transistors as Switches, Pass Levels and the Logic Inverter

A logic gate uses each transistor as a switch: cutoff is open, and triode with a large overdrive is a small resistor rDS. The output of a gate drives other gates, so it is modelled as a capacitor Co. The question in every case is what voltage the capacitor can reach and what it costs in power.

General method.

  1. Find the source of each device: for an NMOS the lower-voltage channel terminal, for a PMOS the higher one.
  2. Write the gate-source voltage (VGS for an NMOS, VSG for a PMOS) in terms of the output voltage Vo.
  3. The device conducts only while VGS≥Vtn (NMOS) or VSG≥|Vtp| (PMOS). The output stops moving where this becomes an equality.
  4. If the device stays on all the way, it is in triode at the end and passes the rail it is connected to exactly (a good level). Otherwise the level is degraded.
  5. For a resistor-load inverter with the input high, the on-device and RD form a divider: VOL=VDDrDS,n/(RD+rDS,n), with a static current VDD/(RD+rDS,n).
  6. For a CMOS gate one device is off in each steady state, so static power is zero; power is spent only when the output changes: P=CVDD2f.

Adapting the method. If the question gives μnCox instead of kn′, use kn′=μnCox. If the device is a PMOS used as a pull-up, VSG=VDD−0 stays at VDD and it passes a full VDD; the degraded cases are always the wrong-polarity placements. If RD is not much larger than rDS,n, VOL is not small: either enlarge RD (more area) or the NMOS W/L (more capacitance). If the computed VOL exceeds Vov=VDD−Vtn the triode formula no longer applies.

Question 1.1. What is the highest voltage an NMOS pull-up can deliver and the lowest voltage a PMOS pull-down can deliver?

Given
VDD=1.0V, Vtn=|Vtp|=0.35V (chosen). Both devices have their gate at the rail that turns them on.
Find
Vo,max for the NMOS in the pull-up network, Vo,min for the PMOS in the pull-down network.
  1. Step 1

    Identify the source of the NMOS.

    VG=VD=VDD,VS=Vo

    The source is the lower channel terminal. The output node is below the drain, so it is the source and its voltage is the unknown.

  2. Step 2

    Write the turn-on condition.

    VGS=VG−VS=VDD−Vo≥Vtn

    As Co charges, Vo rises and VGS shrinks. The channel disappears when VGS falls to Vtn.

  3. Step 3

    Solve for the stopping voltage.

    Vo,max=VDD−Vtn=1.0V−0.35V=0.65V

    The NMOS cannot pass a full "1" by itself. This is a degraded voltage level, 0.35V below the rail.

  4. Step 4

    Repeat for the PMOS pull-down: its source is the output node.

    VSG=VS−VG=Vo−0≥|Vtp|,⇒,Vo,min=|Vtp|=0.35V

    A PMOS conducts only while Vo≥|Vtp|, so it cannot discharge the output below 0.35V. It cannot pass a clean "0".

  5. Step 5

    State the placement rule.

    NMOS: pull-down network,PMOS: pull-up network

    An NMOS pulling down keeps VGS=VDD as Vo→0, and a PMOS pulling up keeps VSG=VDD as Vo→VDD, so both pass full levels.

Question 1.2. What are the output low level, static current and static power of an NMOS inverter with a resistor load when the input is high?

Given
VDD=1.0V, Vtn=0.35V, kn′=540µA/V², (W/L)n=1.5, RD=19kΩ (chosen), Vin=VDD.
Find
rDS,n, VOL, the static current I and the static power P.
  1. Step 1

    Find the on-resistance of the NMOS switch.

    rDS,n=1kn′(W/L)n(VDD−Vtn)=1(540µA/V²)(1.5)(0.65V)=1.90kΩ

    With Vin=VDD the overdrive is Vov=VDD−Vtn=0.65V. A wider device or larger overdrive lowers rDS.

  2. Step 2

    Treat RD and rDS,n as a voltage divider for the output low level.

    VOL=VDDrDS,nRD+rDS,n=(1.0V)1.90kΩ19kΩ+1.90kΩ=0.091V

    The output is small but not zero, because the NMOS is a resistor and not an ideal short. A larger RD gives a lower VOL.

  3. Step 3

    Check the triode assumption.

    VDS=VOL=0.091V≤Vov=0.65V✓

    The formula for rDS holds only in triode, and the check confirms it.

  4. Step 4

    Find the static current through the stack.

    I=VDDRD+rDS,n=1.0V19kΩ+1.90kΩ=47.8µA

    The same current flows for as long as the input stays high, even though nothing is switching.

  5. Step 5

    Find the static power.

    P=VDDI=(1.0V)(47.8µA)=47.8µW

    For input low the NMOS is off, I=0 and Vo=VDD, so this loss appears only for one logic state, and a chip with millions of gates adds it up quickly.

Why the switching is asymmetric. The output rises through RD with τ=RDCo and falls through rDS,n with τ=rDS,nCo. Here RD/rDS,n=19/1.90=10, so the rising edge is ten times slower. RD must also be large in area. The complementary (CMOS) inverter removes the resistor, the static current and the asymmetry at once.

Question 1.3. How much power does a CMOS inverter dissipate when its output switches?

Given
Co=5fF, VDD=1.0V, the output switches through one full cycle (low to high and back) at f=500MHz (chosen).
Find
The energy taken from the supply per cycle and the dynamic power.
  1. Step 1

    Confirm there is no static power.

    Vin=VDD:QNon,QPoff,i=0,Vo=0,Vin=0:QNoff,QPon,i=0,Vo=VDD

    The two series transistors carry the same current and one is always off, so i=0 and P=VDDi=0 in either steady state.

  2. Step 2

    Find the charge and energy taken from the supply for a low-to-high change.

    Esupply=QVDD=(CoVDD)VDD=CoVDD2

    Charging Co from 0 to VDD draws charge CoVDD from the supply at voltage VDD. Half of Esupply is stored on the capacitor, 12CoVDD2, and half is dissipated in rDS,p, whatever its value.

  3. Step 3

    Account for the high-to-low change.

    Ecycle=CoVDD2=(5fF)(1.0V)2=5fJ

    During discharge the stored 12CoVDD2 is dissipated in rDS,n and nothing more is taken from the supply, so one full cycle costs CoVDD2.

  4. Step 4

    Multiply by the switching rate.

    P=CoVDD2f=(5fJ)(500MHz)=2.5µW

    Power is energy per cycle times cycles per second. It does not depend on the transistor resistance, only on Co, VDD2 and f. Millions of gates switching millions of times a second make this the dominant loss.

2. Analyzing and Synthesizing Pull-Up and Pull-Down Networks

A static CMOS gate has a PMOS pull-up network (PUN) from VDD to the output and an NMOS pull-down network (PDN) from the output to ground, driven by the same inputs. For every input combination exactly one of them conducts. In the simplified symbols a bubble on the gate marks a PMOS (on for a low input) and a plain gate marks an NMOS (on for a high input).

General method for analyzing a gate.

  1. Mark the PMOS network (between VDD and the output) and the NMOS network (between the output and ground).
  2. Write when the PDN conducts: transistors in series give AND, in parallel give OR, using the inputs as written. Call this f.
  3. When the PDN conducts the output is pulled to 0, so Y=f‾.
  4. Check with the PUN: it conducts when the complemented inputs satisfy its series (AND) and parallel (OR) structure, and this must equal f‾.
  5. Confirm with a truth table: exactly one network should conduct in every row.

General method for synthesizing a gate.

  1. Write Y as the complement of a sum of products (from the expression, or from the rows where Y‾=1 in a truth table).
  2. PDN: use the expression for Y‾ as written with NMOS devices. A product (AND) is a series path and a sum (OR) is a parallel pair of paths.
  3. PUN: apply DeMorgan’s theorem until every bar sits on a single input: complement each input and swap AND and OR. Build it from PMOS with the same rule: product is series, sum is parallel.
  4. Check that the PUN is the dual of the PDN (series becomes parallel, parallel becomes series) and count 2N transistors for N inputs.

Adapting the method. If an input is needed in complemented form (for example the XOR gate in Question 2.3), add an inverter for it: 2 more transistors. If you are only given the truth table, write the sum of products from the rows with Y‾=1 for the PDN and from the rows with Y=1 for the PUN; this PDN is then not the series-parallel dual of the PUN as written, but swapping AND and OR and complementing the variables of one network gives the other. If the PUN and PDN conduct together for some row, one of them was built from the uncomplemented or complemented form by mistake. Series stacks of PMOS are slow because μp<μn, so a NAND gate, whose series devices are NMOS, is preferred over a NOR gate.

Question 2.1. What logic function does this CMOS gate implement?

Given
PUN: PMOS A in parallel with PMOS B, that pair in series with PMOS C. PDN: NMOS A in series with NMOS B, that branch in parallel with NMOS C.
Find
The output Y as a Boolean expression and its truth table.

    Mark the networks.

    PUN: 3 PMOS between VDD and Y,PDN: 3 NMOS between Y and ground

    Each input drives one PMOS and one NMOS, so the six transistors belong to three inputs.

  2. Step 2

    Write when the PDN conducts.

    fPDN=A·B+C=AB+C

    NMOS devices turn on for a high input. Series devices all must be on (AND) and parallel branches need only one path (OR).

    Find the output.

    Y=fPDN‾=AB+C‾

    Check the PUN gives the same function.

    fPUN=(A‾+B‾)C‾=AB‾C‾=AB+C‾✓

    PMOS devices turn on for a low input, so each input enters complemented: the parallel pair needs A‾ or B‾, in series with C‾. DeMorgan’s theorem shows this equals Y.

    Tabulate and confirm only one network conducts.

    Y=1only whenAB+C=0

    See the table: the PDN is on exactly in the rows with Y=0 and the PUN exactly in the rows with Y=1, never both.

Question 2.2. Create the PUN and the PDN for Y=A·B+C·D‾.

Given
The output expression Y=A·B+C·D‾ (Lesson 30, page 30-1).
Find
The PDN and PUN circuits, and the number of transistors.
  1. Step 1

    Build the PDN from the function it conducts for.

    Y‾=A·B+C·D

    The PDN pulls the output low exactly when Y‾=1, and it uses uncomplemented inputs with NMOS devices.

  2. Step 2

    Turn the expression into a network: AND is series, OR is parallel.

    A·B⇒NMOSAin series withB,C·D⇒series pair+⇒the two pairs in parallel

    Either series path to ground is enough to pull the output low.

  3. Step 3

    Apply DeMorgan’s theorem so the PUN can be read directly.

    Y=AB+CD‾=AB‾·CD‾=(A‾+B‾)(C‾+D‾)

    The expression as given is the complement of a sum of products, which is awkward to read as a network. Pushing the bars onto single inputs gives one in terms of complemented inputs.

  4. Step 4

    Build the PUN with PMOS devices, using the same rule.

    (A‾+B‾)⇒PMOSA∥B,(C‾+D‾)⇒PMOSC∥Dproduct⇒the pairs in series

    A PMOS turns on for a low gate input, so a complemented input in the equation becomes a PMOS driven by the true input.

  5. Step 5

    Check the result.

    Ntransistors=2×4inputs=8,PUN is the dual of PDN

    Series pairs in the PDN became parallel pairs in the PUN and vice versa. Test the row A=B=1,C=D=0: the series pair AB conducts to ground (Y=0) and the PMOS pairs are both open. Test A=C=0: one PMOS in each pair is on, so the PUN conducts, and neither NMOS pair is complete, so Y=1.

3. The CMOS Voltage Transfer Characteristic and Noise Margins

The voltage transfer characteristic (VTC) is the steady output for each steady input. The four levels VOH, VOL, VIL and VIH define it, where VIL and VIH are the two points where the slope is −1. The noise margins measure how much noise a signal can carry before the next gate misreads it.

General method.

  1. Levels: for CMOS VOH=VDD and VOL=0.
  2. Convert to device voltages: VGSN=VIN, VDSN=VOUT, VSGP=VDD−VIN, VSDP=VDD−VOUT.
  3. Modes: NMOS is off for VIN<Vtn, saturated for VOUT>VIN−Vtn, triode below that. PMOS is off for VIN>VDD−|Vtp|, saturated for VOUT<VIN+|Vtp|, triode above that.
  4. Switching point VM (where VIN=VOUT, both saturated): set 12Kn(VM−Vtn)2=12Kp(VDD−VM−|Vtp|)2 and solve, giving the VM formula above.
  5. Matched (Kn=Kp, Vtn=|Vtp|): VIH and VIL from the formulas, VM=VDD/2; sizing: Wp=(μn/μp)Wn for equal channel lengths.
  6. Noise margins: NML=VIL−VOL and NMH=VOH−VIH. For a small mismatch the curve slides by ΔVM=VM−VDD/2: NML changes by about +ΔVM and NMH by about −ΔVM. Area is A=L(Wn+Wp).

Adapting the method. The VIH and VIL formulas hold only for the matched inverter; for |Vtp|≠Vtn or Kn≠Kp use VM to estimate the shift or work from measured levels with the definitions. A weaker PMOS (r<1) moves VM below VDD/2 (the output reaches the input level earlier); a stronger PMOS moves it above. Checks: the order VOL<VIL<VM<VIH<VOH must hold and both noise margins must be positive; a negative margin means a gate cannot reliably drive another identical gate. The shift estimate is best for modest changes in r.

Question 3.1. For a matched CMOS inverter, find VIL, VIH and both noise margins, and mark points A to D on its VTC.

Given
VDD=1.0V, Vtn=|Vtp|=Vt=0.35V (the 65-nm values of Lesson 30, page 30-11), Kn=Kp, λ=0.
Find
VOH, VOL, VM, VIL, VIH, NML, NMH and the corner points.
  1. Step 1

    Write the output levels.

    VOH=VDD=1.0V,VOL=0

    With the input low the PMOS is on and the NMOS is off, and with the input high the roles reverse, so each rail is reached fully.

  2. Step 2

    Find the corner points from the region conditions.

    A: VIN=Vtn=0.35V,D: VIN=VDD−|Vtp|=0.65V

    Left of A the NMOS is off; right of D the PMOS is off.

  3. Step 3

    Find the steep segment and its end points.

    VM=VDD2=0.5V,B: VOUT=VIN+Vt=0.85V,C: VOUT=VIN−Vt=0.15V

    Between B and C both devices are saturated and the gain is very large (no λ), so the curve drops almost vertically at VM. B is where the PMOS leaves triode and C is where the NMOS enters triode.

  4. Step 4

    Compute VIH from the matched-inverter formula.

    VIH=18(5VDD−2Vt)=18(5(1.0)−2(0.35))=0.5375V

    At VIH the NMOS is in triode and the PMOS is in saturation; setting the currents equal and requiring slope −1 gives this result.

  5. Step 5

    Get VIL from symmetry.

    VIL=VDD−VIH=18(3VDD+2Vt)=18(3(1.0)+2(0.35))=0.4625V

    The matched VTC is symmetric about (VDD/2,VDD/2), so the two unity-gain points are mirror images.

  6. Step 6

    Compute the noise margins.

    NMH=VOH−VIH=1.0−0.5375=0.4625V,NML=VIL−VOL=0.4625−0=0.4625V

    Both equal 0.46V, about 93% of the ideal VDD/2=0.5V. Order check: 0<0.4625<0.5<0.5375<1.0 ✓.

Question 3.4. What are the noise margins of an inverter with the following measured levels?

Given
VOL=0.10V, VIL=0.40V, VIH=0.55V, VOH=0.95V (chosen).
Find
NML, NMH and the worst case.
  1. Step 1

    Pair each output level with its input threshold.

    NML=VIL−VOL,NMH=VOH−VIH

    A low driving a receiver is read as 0 until the input exceeds VIL; a high is read as 1 as long as it stays above VIH. The low output goes with VIL and the high output with VIH.

  2. Step 2

    Substitute.

    NML=0.40−0.10=0.30V,NMH=0.95−0.55=0.40V

    Noise up to 0.30V can be added to a low and noise down to −0.40V can be added to a high without a misread.

  3. Step 3

    Check and interpret.

    VOL<VIL<VIH<VOH(0.10<0.40<0.55<0.95)✓,NMworst=min(0.30,0.40)=0.30V

    Both margins are positive, so the gate can drive an identical gate. The worst case is the smaller margin.

4. Propagation Delay

The output of an inverter charges or discharges the capacitance C at its output through a transistor that supplies a finite current. The propagation delay is measured from the midpoint of the input edge to the midpoint of the output edge, and the two directions are generally different.

General method.

  1. Definitions: tPHL and tPLH are measured between the 50% points of the input and output edges (the subscript gives the output direction). Rise, fall and transition times use the 10% and 90% points. tP=12(tPHL+tPLH).
  2. Load: CINT=2Cgd2+2Cgd1+Cdb2+Cdb1 and CEXT=(Cwire+Cg4+Cg3)×extFanOut; C=CINT+CEXT.
  3. Method 1, average current: K=k′(W/L); a=2/[74−3(Vt/VDD)+(Vt/VDD)2]; tPHL=anC/(KnVDD); tPLH=apC/(KpVDD) with |Vtp| in ap.
  4. Method 2, equivalent resistance: scale RN=Reff,N(Weff/Leff)/(W/L)n and RP=Reff,P(Weff/Leff)/(W/L)p; tPHL=0.693RNC and tPLH=0.693RPC from vc=FV−(FV−IV)e−t/RC at the half-way point.
  5. Average: tP=12(tPHL+tPLH).

Adapting the method. The resistances Reff are measured at a reference supply, so to change VDD you must use Method 1, which contains VDD explicitly. a should fall between about 1 and 3. If Kn=Kp and Vtn=|Vtp| the two delays are equal. If the load is not another inverter, replace Cg3 and Cg4 by the input capacitance of what is driven. A transition time is not a propagation delay: the first is the steepness of the edge, the second is how late it is. If a question asks for tPLH only, do only the pull-up half. Units: μA/V^2 with fF gives picoseconds only if converted consistently.

Question 4.1. What are tPHL, tPLH, tP and the fall transition time read from these waveforms?

Given
Cursor readouts (chosen, from an RC model). Input rises through 50% at 20.0ps and falls through 50% at 120.0ps. Output falls through 90%, 50%, 10% at 22.7, 38.0, 79.9ps and later rises through 50% at 155.0ps.
Find
tPHL, tPLH, tP and tTHL.
  1. Step 1

    Mark the levels on each waveform.

    midpoint=12(VOL+VOH),10%and90%ofVOH−VOL

    The input rises, so the output falls; the subscript PHL names the output direction (high to low).

  2. Step 2

    Compute tPHL from the 50% points of the rising input and falling output.

    tPHL=tout,50%↓−tin,50%↑=38.0−20.0=18.0ps

    Subtract the time the input edge crosses its midpoint from the time the output crosses its midpoint.

  3. Step 3

    Compute tPLH from the falling input and rising output.

    tPLH=tout,50%↑−tin,50%↓=155.0−120.0=35.0ps

    The output is slower in this direction, a sign that the pull-up is weaker than the pull-down.

  4. Step 4

    Average the two delays.

    tP=12(tPHL+tPLH)=12(18.0+35.0)=26.5ps

    The average is the single delay figure used to compare gates.

  5. Step 5

    Compute the transition time of the falling output.

    tTHL=t10%−t90%=79.9−22.7=57.2ps

    This is the edge duration, not a delay. For a single time constant the 10% to 90% time is about 2.2RC: 18.0/0.693=26ps=RC and 2.2×26=57, consistent.

Question 4.2. Find tPLH, tPHL and tP by both methods, and the effect of lowering VDD to 0.8V (Example 17-2).

Given
65-nm process: VDD=1.0V, Vtn=−Vtp=0.35V, kn′=5.4kp′=540µA/V², (W/L)n=1.5, (W/L)p=3, C=5fF. Devices with W/L=1.5 have Reff,N=8kΩ and Reff,P=24kΩ.
Find
tPHL, tPLH, tP by average current and by equivalent resistance, then tP at VDD=0.8V.
  1. Step 1

    Method 1: compute the transconductance parameters.

    Kn=kn′(W/L)n=540×1.5=810µA/V²,Kp=kp′(W/L)p=100×3=300µA/V²

    kp′=540/5.4=100µA/V². The PMOS is weaker per unit width because μp<μn.

  2. Step 2

    Compute the factor a.

    an=ap=274−3VtVDD+(VtVDD)2=21.75−3(0.35)+(0.35)2=20.8225=2.43

    |Vtp|=Vtn, so ap=an. The value lies between 1 and 3 as expected.

  3. Step 3

    Compute the delays.

    tPHL=anCKnVDD=2.43(5fF)(810µA/V²)(1V)=15.0ps,tPLH=apCKpVDD=2.43(5fF)(300µA/V²)(1V)=40.5ps

    The PMOS delay is longer because Kp<Kn. Then tP=12(15.0+40.5)=27.8ps.

  4. Step 4

    Method 2: scale the effective resistances to the actual shapes.

    RN=Reff,N(Weff/Leff)(W/L)n=8kΩ×1.51.5=8kΩ,RP=24kΩ×1.53=12kΩ

    Resistance is proportional to L/W, so the PMOS, twice the reference width, has half the reference resistance.

  5. Step 5

    Compute the delays from the RC step response.

    tPHL=0.693RNC=0.693(8kΩ)(5fF)=27.7ps,tPLH=0.693(12kΩ)(5fF)=41.6ps

    From 12=1−e−t/RC we get t=RCln2=0.693RC. Then tP=12(27.7+41.6)=34.7ps.

  6. Step 6

    Lower the supply to 0.8V (Method 1 only).

    a=21.75−3(0.4375)+(0.4375)2=3.18,tPHL=3.18(5fF)(810µA/V²)(0.8V)=24.5ps,tPLH=3.18(5fF)(300µA/V²)(0.8V)=66.3ps

    Vt/VDD=0.35/0.8=0.4375. The resistances were measured at 1.0V, so Method 2 cannot be reused. tP=12(24.5+66.3)=45.4ps.

  7. Step 7

    Compare.

    tP(0.8V)tP(1.0V)=45.427.8=1.63

    Lowering the supply to save power slows the inverter by about 63%.

The resistance method gives a longer tPHL because the effective resistances come from simulation of the real technology, while the average-current formulas assume long-channel square-law behavior, which underestimates delay in deep-submicron processes. Question 4.3. What is the total load capacitance of an inverter with fan-out 2, and what tPHL follows from it?

Given
Cgd1=0.2, Cgd2=0.3, Cdb1=0.5, Cdb2=0.7fF for the driver; each driven inverter has Cg3=0.9 and Cg4=1.5fF; Cwire=0.8fF per branch; Fan_Out =2; RN=8kΩ (all chosen).
Find
CINT, CEXT, CTOT and tPHL by Method 2.
  1. Step 1

    Find the internal capacitance.

    CINT=2Cgd2+2Cgd1+Cdb2+Cdb1=2(0.3)+2(0.2)+0.7+0.5=2.2fF

    The gate-drain capacitances count twice because of the Miller effect: when the output falls by VDD the input rises by VDD, so the voltage across each changes by 2VDD. The drain-body capacitances count once.

  2. Step 2

    Find the external capacitance.

    CEXT=(Cwire+Cg4+Cg3)×extFanOut=(0.8+1.5+0.9)(2)=6.4fF

    Each driven gate adds its own wire and gate capacitances, so the sum is multiplied by the fan-out.

  3. Step 3

    Add them.

    CTOT=CINT+CEXT=2.2+6.4=8.6fF

    This single capacitor replaces every capacitance on the output node.

  4. Step 4

    Use CTOT in the delay formula.

    tPHL=0.693RNCTOT=0.693(8kΩ)(8.6fF)=47.7ps

    Compared with 27.7ps for 5fF in Question 4.2, the delay scales directly with C: more fan-out means a slower gate.

Summary of the Methods

  • Switches and pass levels. Find the source; write VGS (or VSG) in terms of Vo; the output stops where it equals the threshold. NMOS pulls down well and pulls up to VDD−Vtn; PMOS pulls up well and pulls down to |Vtp|. Resistor load: VOL=VDDrDS/(RD+rDS) and a static current; CMOS: no static current and P=CVDD2f.
  • Networks. Series is AND, parallel is OR. Analyze: Y=fPDN‾ and check with the PUN. Synthesize: PDN from Y‾ as written with NMOS; PUN by DeMorgan with PMOS; the PUN and PDN are duals; 2N transistors plus inverters for complemented inputs.
  • VTC and noise margins. VOH=VDD, VOL=0; matched VIH=18(5VDD−2Vt), VIL=18(3VDD+2Vt), VM=VDD/2; general VM from r=Kp/Kn; NML=VIL−VOL, NMH=VOH−VIH; Wp=(μn/μp)Wn; A=L(Wn+Wp).
  • Propagation delay. Use 50% points for tPHL, tPLH and 10% to 90% for transition times; C=CINT+CEXT; Method 1 t=aC/(KVDD) with a=2/[74−3Vt/VDD+(Vt/VDD)2]; Method 2 t=0.693RC with R scaled by W/L; change VDD only with Method 1.
