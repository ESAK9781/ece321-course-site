1. The CMOS Voltage Transfer Characteristic and Noise Margins

The voltage transfer characteristic (VTC) is the steady output for each steady input. The four levels VOH, VOL, VIL and VIH define it, where VIL and VIH are the two points where the slope is −1. The noise margins measure how much noise a signal can carry before the next gate misreads it.

General method.

  1. Levels: for CMOS VOH=VDD and VOL=0.
  2. Convert to device voltages: VGSN=VIN, VDSN=VOUT, VSGP=VDD−VIN, VSDP=VDD−VOUT.
  3. Modes: NMOS is off for VIN<Vtn, saturated for VOUT>VIN−Vtn, triode below that. PMOS is off for VIN>VDD−|Vtp|, saturated for VOUT<VIN+|Vtp|, triode above that.
  4. Switching point VM (where VIN=VOUT, both saturated): set 12Kn(VM−Vtn)2=12Kp(VDD−VM−|Vtp|)2 and solve, giving the VM formula above.
  5. Matched (Kn=Kp, Vtn=|Vtp|): VIH and VIL from the formulas, VM=VDD/2; sizing: Wp=(μn/μp)Wn for equal channel lengths.
  6. Noise margins: NML=VIL−VOL and NMH=VOH−VIH. For a small mismatch the curve slides by ΔVM=VM−VDD/2: NML changes by about +ΔVM and NMH by about −ΔVM. Area is A=L(Wn+Wp).

Adapting the method. The VIH and VIL formulas hold only for the matched inverter; for |Vtp|≠Vtn or Kn≠Kp use VM to estimate the shift or work from measured levels with the definitions. A weaker PMOS (r<1) moves VM below VDD/2 (the output reaches the input level earlier); a stronger PMOS moves it above. Checks: the order VOL<VIL<VM<VIH<VOH must hold and both noise margins must be positive; a negative margin means a gate cannot reliably drive another identical gate. The shift estimate is best for modest changes in r.

Question 1.1. For a matched CMOS inverter, find VIL, VIH and both noise margins, and mark points A to D on its VTC.

Given
VDD=1.0V, Vtn=|Vtp|=Vt=0.35V (the 65-nm values of Lesson 30, page 30-11), Kn=Kp, λ=0.
Find
VOH, VOL, VM, VIL, VIH, NML, NMH and the corner points.
  1. Step 1

    Write the output levels.

    VOH=VDD=1.0V,VOL=0

    With the input low the PMOS is on and the NMOS is off, and with the input high the roles reverse, so each rail is reached fully.

  2. Step 2

    Find the corner points from the region conditions.

    A: VIN=Vtn=0.35V,D: VIN=VDD−|Vtp|=0.65V

    Left of A the NMOS is off; right of D the PMOS is off.

  3. Step 3

    Find the steep segment and its end points.

    VM=VDD2=0.5V,B: VOUT=VIN+Vt=0.85V,C: VOUT=VIN−Vt=0.15V

    Between B and C both devices are saturated and the gain is very large (no λ), so the curve drops almost vertically at VM. B is where the PMOS leaves triode and C is where the NMOS enters triode.

  4. Step 4

    Compute VIH from the matched-inverter formula.

    VIH=18(5VDD−2Vt)=18(5(1.0)−2(0.35))=0.5375V

    At VIH the NMOS is in triode and the PMOS is in saturation; setting the currents equal and requiring slope −1 gives this result.

  5. Step 5

    Get VIL from symmetry.

    VIL=VDD−VIH=18(3VDD+2Vt)=18(3(1.0)+2(0.35))=0.4625V

    The matched VTC is symmetric about (VDD/2,VDD/2), so the two unity-gain points are mirror images.

  6. Step 6

    Compute the noise margins.

    NMH=VOH−VIH=1.0−0.5375=0.4625V,NML=VIL−VOL=0.4625−0=0.4625V

    Both equal 0.46V, about 93% of the ideal VDD/2=0.5V. Order check: 0<0.4625<0.5<0.5375<1.0 ✓.

Question 1.3. How wide must the PMOS be for VM=VDD/2, and what do we lose by making it narrower?

Given
65-nm process: VDD=1.0V, Vtn=|Vtp|=0.35V, μn=5.4μp, μnCox=540µA/V², (W/L)n=1.5, L=65nm (Lesson 30, page 30-11).
Find
(a) Wp for VM=VDD/2; (b) the area; (c) the noise margins; (d) VM and NML for Wp=Wn; (e) the area saved.
  1. Step 1

    (a) Find Wn, then size the PMOS for equal strength.

    Wn=(W/L)nL=1.5(65nm)=97.5nm,Wp=μnμpWn=5.4(97.5nm)=526.5nm

    With equal lengths, Kn=Kp requires Wp/Wn=μn/μp. This ratio of 5.4 is larger than the usual 2 to 4 because this process has a large mobility ratio.

  2. Step 2

    (b) Find the area.

    A=WnL+WpL=(97.5nm)(65nm)+(526.5nm)(65nm)=40,560nm²

    Area is the sum of the two channel rectangles.

  3. Step 3

    (c) Find the noise margins for the matched case.

    VIH=0.5375V,VIL=0.4625V,NMH=VOH−VIH=0.46V,NML=VIL−VOL=0.46V

    These are the Question 1.1 results because the 65-nm inverter has the same VDD and Vt.

  4. Step 4

    (d) Compute r for Wp=Wn, then the new switching point.

    r=KpKn=μpCox(W/L)pμnCox(W/L)n=15.4(1)=0.430VM=Vtn+r(VDD−|Vtp|)1+r=0.35+0.430(1.0−0.35)1+0.430=0.44V

    The PMOS is now 1/5.4 as strong. Because VM appears in both numerator and denominator it is a weak function of r: a factor-of-five weaker PMOS only moves it 0.06V.

  5. Step 5

    Estimate the noise margin change.

    ΔVM=VM−VDD2=0.44−0.50=−0.06V,NML≈NML,matched+ΔVM=0.46−0.06=0.40V

    Assuming the whole curve slides left by ΔVM, NML shrinks by that amount and NMH grows by about the same.

  6. Step 6

    (e) Find the area saved.

    A=L(Wn+Wp)=65nm(97.5nm+97.5nm)=12,675nm²,1−12,67540,560=0.6875≈69%

    A modest loss of NML buys a 69% saving in area. Smaller area also means less capacitance and, as Lesson 31 shows, less delay.

Question 1.4. What are the noise margins of an inverter with the following measured levels?

Given
VOL=0.10V, VIL=0.40V, VIH=0.55V, VOH=0.95V (chosen).
Find
NML, NMH and the worst case.
  1. Step 1

    Pair each output level with its input threshold.

    NML=VIL−VOL,NMH=VOH−VIH

    A low driving a receiver is read as 0 until the input exceeds VIL; a high is read as 1 as long as it stays above VIH. The low output goes with VIL and the high output with VIH.

  2. Step 2

    Substitute.

    NML=0.40−0.10=0.30V,NMH=0.95−0.55=0.40V

    Noise up to 0.30V can be added to a low and noise down to −0.40V can be added to a high without a misread.

  3. Step 3

    Check and interpret.

    VOL<VIL<VIH<VOH(0.10<0.40<0.55<0.95)✓,NMworst=min(0.30,0.40)=0.30V

    Both margins are positive, so the gate can drive an identical gate. The worst case is the smaller margin.

Question 1.5. What are the noise margins of an unmatched inverter whose PMOS is only twice as wide as its NMOS?

Given
VDD=1.0V, Vtn=|Vtp|=0.35V, μn=3μp, (W/L)p=2(W/L)n (the Lesson 32 compromise); Kn=0.81mA/V² (chosen).
Find
r, VM, and estimates of NML and NMH.

    Find the strength ratio of the PMOS to the NMOS.

    KpKn=μp(W/L)pμn(W/L)n=13(2)=23,r=KpKn=23=0.816

    Matching would need (W/L)p=3(W/L)n. With only 2× the PMOS is 2/3 as strong as the NMOS, so r<1.

  2. Step 2

    Find the switching point.

    VM=Vtn+r(VDD−|Vtp|)1+r=0.35+0.816(1.0−0.35)1+0.816=0.485V

    At VM both devices are saturated and carry the same current. A weaker PMOS moves VM slightly below VDD/2.

    Estimate the noise margins.

    NML≈0.4625+ΔVM=0.4625−0.015=0.447V,NMH≈0.4625−ΔVM=0.4625+0.015=0.478V

    Both stay close to the ideal 0.5V, so a PMOS 2× wide instead of 3× wide costs almost nothing in noise margin and saves area and capacitance.

2. Propagation Delay

The output of an inverter charges or discharges the capacitance C at its output through a transistor that supplies a finite current. The propagation delay is measured from the midpoint of the input edge to the midpoint of the output edge, and the two directions are generally different.

General method.

  1. Definitions: tPHL and tPLH are measured between the 50% points of the input and output edges (the subscript gives the output direction). Rise, fall and transition times use the 10% and 90% points. tP=12(tPHL+tPLH).
  2. Load: CINT=2Cgd2+2Cgd1+Cdb2+Cdb1 and CEXT=(Cwire+Cg4+Cg3)×Fan_Out; C=CINT+CEXT.
  3. Method 1, average current: K=k′(W/L); a=2/[74−3(Vt/VDD)+(Vt/VDD)2]; tPHL=anC/(KnVDD); tPLH=apC/(KpVDD) with |Vtp| in ap.
  4. Method 2, equivalent resistance: scale RN=Reff,N(Weff/Leff)/(W/L)n and RP=Reff,P(Weff/Leff)/(W/L)p; tPHL=0.693RNC and tPLH=0.693RPC from vc=FV−(FV−IV)e−t/RC at the half-way point.
  5. Average: tP=12(tPHL+tPLH).

Adapting the method. The resistances Reff are measured at a reference supply, so to change VDD you must use Method 1, which contains VDD explicitly. a should fall between about 1 and 3. If Kn=Kp and Vtn=|Vtp| the two delays are equal. If the load is not another inverter, replace Cg3 and Cg4 by the input capacitance of what is driven. A transition time is not a propagation delay: the first is the steepness of the edge, the second is how late it is. If a question asks for tPLH only, do only the pull-up half. Units: μA/V^2 with fF gives picoseconds only if converted consistently.

Question 2.1. What are tPHL, tPLH, tP and the fall transition time read from these waveforms?

Given
Cursor readouts (chosen, from an RC model). Input rises through 50% at 20.0ps and falls through 50% at 120.0ps. Output falls through 90%, 50%, 10% at 22.7, 38.0, 79.9ps and later rises through 50% at 155.0ps.
Find
tPHL, tPLH, tP and tTHL.
  1. Step 1

    Mark the levels on each waveform.

    midpoint=12(VOL+VOH),10%and90%ofVOH−VOL

    The input rises, so the output falls; the subscript PHL names the output direction (high to low).

  2. Step 2

    Compute tPHL from the 50% points of the rising input and falling output.

    tPHL=tout,50%↓−tin,50%↑=38.0−20.0=18.0ps

    Subtract the time the input edge crosses its midpoint from the time the output crosses its midpoint.

  3. Step 3

    Compute tPLH from the falling input and rising output.

    tPLH=tout,50%↑−tin,50%↓=155.0−120.0=35.0ps

    The output is slower in this direction, a sign that the pull-up is weaker than the pull-down.

  4. Step 4

    Average the two delays.

    tP=12(tPHL+tPLH)=12(18.0+35.0)=26.5ps

    The average is the single delay figure used to compare gates.

  5. Step 5

    Compute the transition time of the falling output.

    tTHL=t10%−t90%=79.9−22.7=57.2ps

    This is the edge duration, not a delay. For a single time constant the 10% to 90% time is about 2.2RC: 18.0/0.693=26ps=RC and 2.2×26=57, consistent.

Question 2.2. Find tPLH, tPHL and tP by both methods, and the effect of lowering VDD to 0.8V (Example 17-2).

Given
65-nm process: VDD=1.0V, Vtn=−Vtp=0.35V, kn′=5.4kp′=540µA/V², (W/L)n=1.5, (W/L)p=3, C=5fF. Devices with W/L=1.5 have Reff,N=8kΩ and Reff,P=24kΩ.
Find
tPHL, tPLH, tP by average current and by equivalent resistance, then tP at VDD=0.8V.
  1. Step 1

    Method 1: compute the transconductance parameters.

    Kn=kn′(W/L)n=540×1.5=810µA/V²,Kp=kp′(W/L)p=100×3=300µA/V²

    kp′=540/5.4=100µA/V². The PMOS is weaker per unit width because μp<μn.

  2. Step 2

    Compute the factor a.

    an=ap=274−3VtVDD+(VtVDD)2=21.75−3(0.35)+(0.35)2=20.8225=2.43

    |Vtp|=Vtn, so ap=an. The value lies between 1 and 3 as expected.

  3. Step 3

    Compute the delays.

    tPHL=anCKnVDD=2.43(5fF)(810µA/V²)(1V)=15.0ps,tPLH=apCKpVDD=2.43(5fF)(300µA/V²)(1V)=40.5ps

    The PMOS delay is longer because Kp<Kn. Then tP=12(15.0+40.5)=27.8ps.

  4. Step 4

    Method 2: scale the effective resistances to the actual shapes.

    RN=Reff,N(Weff/Leff)(W/L)n=8kΩ×1.51.5=8kΩ,RP=24kΩ×1.53=12kΩ

    Resistance is proportional to L/W, so the PMOS, twice the reference width, has half the reference resistance.

  5. Step 5

    Compute the delays from the RC step response.

    tPHL=0.693RNC=0.693(8kΩ)(5fF)=27.7ps,tPLH=0.693(12kΩ)(5fF)=41.6ps

    From 12=1−e−t/RC we get t=RCln2=0.693RC. Then tP=12(27.7+41.6)=34.7ps.

  6. Step 6

    Lower the supply to 0.8V (Method 1 only).

    a=21.75−3(0.4375)+(0.4375)2=3.18,tPHL=3.18(5fF)(810µA/V²)(0.8V)=24.5ps,tPLH=3.18(5fF)(300µA/V²)(0.8V)=66.3ps

    Vt/VDD=0.35/0.8=0.4375. The resistances were measured at 1.0V, so Method 2 cannot be reused. tP=12(24.5+66.3)=45.4ps.

  7. Step 7

    Compare.

    tP(0.8V)tP(1.0V)=45.427.8=1.63

    Lowering the supply to save power slows the inverter by about 63%.

Question 2.3. What is the total load capacitance of an inverter with fan-out 2, and what tPHL follows from it?

Given
Cgd1=0.2, Cgd2=0.3, Cdb1=0.5, Cdb2=0.7fF for the driver; each driven inverter has Cg3=0.9 and Cg4=1.5fF; Cwire=0.8fF per branch; Fan_Out =2; RN=8kΩ (all chosen).
Find
CINT, CEXT, CTOT and tPHL by Method 2.
  1. Step 1

    Find the internal capacitance.

    CINT=2Cgd2+2Cgd1+Cdb2+Cdb1=2(0.3)+2(0.2)+0.7+0.5=2.2fF

    The gate-drain capacitances count twice because of the Miller effect: when the output falls by VDD the input rises by VDD, so the voltage across each changes by 2VDD. The drain-body capacitances count once.

  2. Step 2

    Find the external capacitance.

    CEXT=(Cwire+Cg4+Cg3)×Fan_Out=(0.8+1.5+0.9)(2)=6.4fF

    Each driven gate adds its own wire and gate capacitances, so the sum is multiplied by the fan-out.

  3. Step 3

    Add them.

    CTOT=CINT+CEXT=2.2+6.4=8.6fF

    This single capacitor replaces every capacitance on the output node.

  4. Step 4

    Use CTOT in the delay formula.

    tPHL=0.693RNCTOT=0.693(8kΩ)(8.6fF)=47.7ps

    Compared with 27.7ps for 5fF in Question 2.2, the delay scales directly with C: more fan-out means a slower gate.

3. Sizing Transistors in CMOS Logic Gates

An optimized inverter has (W/L)n=n and (W/L)p=p. A larger gate must charge and discharge its output as fast as that inverter does in the worst case, the input combination that leaves the fewest or the most series devices in the conducting path. Because R∝1/W for a fixed length, we size every device so that its worst-case path has the same resistance as the inverter's single transistor.

General method.

  1. Start from the inverter: n=(W/L)n,inv and p=(W/L)p,inv. Use the minimum channel length L for every device, so only W changes, and W=(W/L)L.
  2. PUN: find the longest series path. If it has k devices, every device on it must be k× wider than the inverter PMOS: kp.
  3. A device alone in a branch parallel to a series string must match that string: the branch needs the same resistance, so the lone device is sized from the string’s resistance rather than from the inverter.
  4. PDN: do the same with the NMOS devices and n; the longest series path with k devices gives kn for each device on it.
  5. Check every path from VDD to Y and from Y to ground: the resistance of each must be no greater than the inverter’s R (a path of k equal devices has kRdevice).
  6. Convert to widths: W=(ratio)L.

Adapting the method. If a question gives the gate as a logic expression, first build the PDN from Y‾ and the PUN as its dual (Lessons 29 and 30) and then size. NAND gates need less area than NOR gates because the long series stack is NMOS (ratio 4n) rather than PMOS (ratio 4p) and p>n; this is why NAND is preferred. A device that appears in every path of one network is sized by the longest path, since it carries the full current. The widths are for the worst case; other input combinations are faster. If the problem gives W in nm for the inverter, convert to ratios first, n=Wn/L.

Question 3.1. Size the transistors of a four-input NOR gate (Lesson 32, Example 1).

Given
The optimized inverter has (W/L)n=n=2 and (W/L)p=p=4, L=65nm (as in Example 3).
Find
The ratio and the width of each transistor in Y=A+B+C+D‾.
  1. Step 1

    Identify the worst-case paths.

    PUN: all four PMOS in series⇒k=4,PDN: one NMOS at a time⇒k=1

    The PUN conducts only when all inputs are low, so there are always four devices in series. The PDN conducts when any input is high; the worst case is one input high, a single device.

  2. Step 2

    Size the PMOS devices.

    (W/L)p,NOR=4p=4(4)=16,Wp=16(65nm)=1040nm

    Four equal resistances in series add to four times one, so each device must have a quarter of the inverter PMOS resistance, which is four times the width.

  3. Step 3

    Size the NMOS devices.

    (W/L)n,NOR=n=2,Wn=2(65nm)=130nm

    In the worst case one NMOS conducts alone, exactly as in the inverter, so no widening is needed. With more inputs high, more devices share the current and the gate is faster.

Question 3.3. Size the transistors of a complex gate, and find its logic function (Lesson 32, Example 3).

Given
The gate in Figure 3.3: PUN is A in series with the parallel combination of B and the series pair D, C; PDN is A in parallel with (B in series with the parallel pair C, D). Inverter n=2, p=4, L=65nm.
Find
The ratio and width of each device and the function Y.
  1. Step 1

    Find the longest series path in the PUN.

    paths: A-B(2 devices),A-D-C(3 devices)⇒k=3

    The path through D and C has three devices in series.

  2. Step 2

    Size A, C and D.

    (W/L)=3p=3(4)=12,W=12(65nm)=780nm

    Each device on the three-device path gets 1/3 of the inverter PMOS resistance. A is on every path, so it needs this size too.

  3. Step 3

    Size B so that its path matches.

    RB+RA=Rinv⇒RB=23Rinv⇒(W/L)B=32p=6,WB=6(65nm)=390nm

    A already has Rinv/3, so the A-B path needs B to have 2Rinv/3. A device with 2/3 the resistance has 3/2 the width: 1.5p=6. This is half the width of C and D, because the D-C branch has twice the resistance of one device.

  4. Step 4

    Find the longest series path in the PDN.

    paths: A(1 device),B-CorB-D(2 devices)⇒k=2

    The two-device paths are the worst case.

  5. Step 5

    Size the NMOS devices.

    B,C,D:(W/L)=2n=4,W=4(65nm)=260nm,A:(W/L)=n=2,W=2(65nm)=130nm

    A is a single-device path and keeps the inverter size.

  6. Step 6

    Find the logic function.

    fPDN=A+B(C+D),Y=A+B(C+D)‾

    The PDN conducts when A, or B together with C or D, is high; the output is the complement.

Question 3.4. Size a five-input gate whose pull-up has a parallel branch inside a series path (Lesson 32, Example 4).

Given
The gate in Figure 3.4: PUN is the parallel combination of A and the series string B, C, D, in series with E; PDN is its dual. Inverter n=2, p=4, L=65nm. In the notetaker some inputs are complemented; that changes which signal drives a gate, not its size.
Find
The ratio and width of every device and Y.
  1. Step 1

    Find the longest PUN path.

    paths: A-E(2 devices),B-C-D-E(4 devices)⇒k=4

    The four-device path is the worst case.

  2. Step 2

    Size B, C, D and E.

    (W/L)=4p=4(4)=16,W=16(65nm)=1040nm

    Each of four series devices has a quarter of the inverter resistance, so the path totals Rinv. E is on every path, so it has this size too.

  3. Step 3

    Size A to match the B-C-D string.

    RA=RBCD=3·Rinv4⇒WA=1040nm3=347nm,(W/L)A=163=5.3

    A is in parallel with the string, and the two must have equal resistance. The string has three devices of width 1040 nm, so its resistance is three times that of one, and A is a third as wide. The A-E path is then 34Rinv+14Rinv=Rinv.

  4. Step 4

    Build the dual PDN.

    fPDN=A(B+C+D)+E,longest path: A plus one of B,C,D⇒k=2

    Series in the PUN becomes parallel in the PDN and vice versa: A in series with the parallel trio B, C, D, and that branch in parallel with E.

  5. Step 5

    Size the NMOS devices.

    A,B,C,D:(W/L)=2n=4,W=260nm,E:(W/L)=n=2,W=130nm

    E is a single-device path and keeps the inverter size. The function is Y=A(B+C+D)+E‾.

Summary of the Methods

  • VTC and noise margins. VOH=VDD, VOL=0; matched VIH=18(5VDD−2Vt), VIL=18(3VDD+2Vt), VM=VDD/2; unmatched: VM from r=Kp/Kn, then shift both margins by ±ΔVM; measured levels: NML=VIL−VOL, NMH=VOH−VIH; Wp=(μn/μp)Wn to match.
  • Propagation delay. 50% points for tPHL and tPLH, 10% to 90% for transition times; C=CINT+CEXT; Method 1 t=aC/(KVDD); Method 2 t=0.693RC with R scaled by W/L; change VDD only with Method 1; tP is the average.
  • Sizing. Minimum L; find the longest series path (k devices) in each network; each device on it gets k times the inverter ratio (kp in the PUN, kn in the PDN); a parallel branch must match the resistance of the series string it sits beside; convert with W=(W/L)L. NAND is smaller than NOR.
